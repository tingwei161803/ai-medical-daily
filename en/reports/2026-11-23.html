Monday · Clinical Applications & Research

A week before the meeting, vendors measure turnaround time and the society measures patient outcomes

Monday, November 23, 2026 · Covering Nov 16–23 · Taipei time

RSNA 2026 opens in Chicago on Nov 29, and the week before it is the densest seven days of the year for medical-imaging AI press releases. This year those seven days came with an unusually clean control group: almost every number the vendors put out is a process endpoint — turnaround time, sensitivity, the share of AI descriptors a radiologist leaves alone — while almost every topic the society put on its own podium is an outcome endpoint: patient outcomes, false alarms, cognitive load, where diagnostic risk moves to. The two sides are not measuring the same thing, and that gap is what is worth recording today. Every figure below carries its source inline; anything vendor-reported, anything from a conference abstract not yet peer-reviewed, and anything whose full text I could not read is flagged in that item's "Discount this" row.

01 — Top Stories

Eight items, ordered by who is measuring what: the process the vendors measured, then the outcomes the society measured, then what the journals actually published in these five days.
Process endpoint SimonMedMSK X-ray / Chest VLM11/16

510,000 musculoskeletal X-rays: fracture reports go from two days to eight hours, down 83.8%

What

Sean Raj, SimonMed's chief medical and innovation officer, will present two studies at RSNA 2026, datelined Nov 16 and published Nov 19. The first evaluates AI worklist reprioritisation against report turnaround time across more than 200 US centres and over 510,000 musculoskeletal radiographs, spanning the period before the system's 2022 introduction through full integration in 2025: mean turnaround for fracture-positive exams fell 83.8%, from about two days to under eight hours, while fracture-negative studies also improved without slowing routine workflow (podium, Dec 2). The second evaluates a vision-language model generating outpatient chest X-ray reports: pre-trained on more than 5 million image-report pairs, then fine-tuned on more than 150,000 SimonMed adult outpatient exams and tested on a consecutive cohort from 143 outpatient centres. Fine-tuning raised sensitivity with a modest loss of specificity, and pulmonary nodule sensitivity rose from 62.6% to 80.8%, the largest gain of any finding (poster, Dec 2).

Why it matters

Turnaround time is the endpoint AI most reliably moves, because it measures queue order rather than interpretation — putting fracture films at the front of the list does not require the model to beat a human. What is notable about the 18-point nodule gain is not its size but its source: institution-specific fine-tuning. A generic model differing that much before and after it meets local data puts a price on the claim that general-purpose foundation models work out of the box.

Discount this

Both are conference abstracts, not peer-reviewed, and the figures come from a company release. The turnaround study is retrospective and spans 2022–2025, years in which staffing, scheduling and the PACS itself all changed, so the 83.8% cannot all be attributed to AI. How far specificity fell in the second study is not stated, and the model is labelled investigational.

Process endpoint DeepHealth / RadNetThyroid · DBT · Prostate11/19

Across 90,000-plus thyroid ultrasounds, radiologists changed nothing in 88.7% of AI nodule descriptions

What

DeepHealth, a RadNet subsidiary, announced on Nov 19 five podium presentations and two posters across breast, thyroid, lung and prostate imaging. The hardest number is the thyroid one: in a prospective real-world deployment covering more than 90,000 US exams, radiologists left all five AI-generated descriptors unmodified in 88.7% of nodules (Nov 29). The dense-breast work builds on the ASSURE study already published in Nature Health (579,583 exams, 2,762 cancers); the Dec 1 talk reports higher overall, invasive and aggressive cancer detection in women with dense breasts. The release also cites two published papers: in European Radiology (Guenzel et al.), combining radiologist assessment with AI risk classification could have avoided nearly one prostate biopsy in five while holding 98.4% sensitivity for clinically significant cancer; in Radiology (Paramasamy et al.), across 39,323 chest CTs at Erasmus Medical Center the lung AI was associated with a 14.6% reduction in adjusted median reporting time — and that lung AI is listed as not available in the US.

Why it matters

The 88.7% figure is interesting precisely because it does not measure correctness; it measures how often a physician did not bother to edit. In a structured-reporting world a high acceptance rate reads either as the AI writing well or as the leading edge of automation bias — the same number is a selling point in a vendor deck and a risk indicator in RSNA's cognitive-load sessions. DeepHealth is also the only vendor this week to hang its conference abstracts on already peer-reviewed publications, which is a habit the rest of the field could copy.

Discount this

This is a promotional release. Of the seven RSNA presentations, only the thyroid one carries a number in the release; the dense-breast item gives no detection rate at all, and the prostate multi-reader study, the image-only risk model, the breast arterial calcification poster and the priors-for-false-positives poster give none. The 98.4% and 14.6% come from published papers whose full texts I did not obtain, and are relayed here from the release only.

Cohort study HeartLung.AIAgatston-2.0 · AI-CVD · Sybil11/16

Among people scoring zero for calcium, AI found calcium in 21.7% — and their 20-year coronary risk was 7.7% against 3.8%

What

HeartLung.AI announced nine RSNA abstracts on Nov 16, all built on reading cardiovascular risk off scans acquired for some other reason. The strongest sits in MESA and the Framingham Heart Study: among people whose conventional Agatston score was zero, 21.7% still had calcium detectable by Agatston-2.0, and their 20-year coronary heart disease incidence was 7.7% against 3.8% for those without (Dec 1). On external validation in MIMIC-CXR, the chest-radiograph risk model AI-CX-CVD reached AUC 0.751 for incident composite cardiovascular disease and AUC 0.790 for cardiovascular mortality (Dec 2). The Sybil lung cancer risk model, run on coronary artery calcium CT over fifteen years, scored AUC 0.83 at one year, falling to 0.70 at fifteen (Nov 30). In the Miami Heart Study, among low-CAC participants median noncalcified plaque volume was 34.5 mm³ in the highest epicardial fat quartile against 11.9 mm³ in the lowest (Dec 1). A further abstract compares Sybil with the epidemiologic PLCOm2012 model: Sybil discriminated significantly better in the Spanish cohort, while the US difference did not reach significance.

Why it matters

The zero-score group is the population currently told it is low risk, and 21.7% plus a doubled 20-year incidence implies that current stratification puts a sizeable share of people on the wrong side of the line. This kind of opportunistic screening needs no new exam and no new radiation dose, only a re-read of images already acquired — one of the rare settings where the cost structure favours medical AI. The Sybil AUC falling from 0.83 to 0.70 is an honest number too: an imaging model's predictive power has a shelf life, and most vendors do not print the fifteen-year column.

Discount this

All of it is observational cohort work presented as conference abstracts, with no intervention anywhere — nobody has shown that acting on these classifications improves outcomes. The figures were compiled and released by the company, and four of the nine abstracts carry no numbers at all on that page. The company does state that the US Sybil difference was not significant, but "classifies more people as high risk" without outcome data could mean more sensitive or merely noisier. Agatston-2.0 and AI-CVD performance in these cohorts is not independently audited.

Head-to-head LunitINSIGHT11/17

Lunit brings 17 studies to Chicago, one of them a head-to-head of four FDA-cleared breast AI products

What

Lunit (KOSDAQ: 328130) said on Nov 17 it will present 17 studies at RSNA 2026, up from 14 last year, with 11 selected for oral presentation. Fifteen concern breast cancer, spanning detection, risk prediction, density analysis and imaging quality control. The most notable is a large-scale comparison of four FDA-cleared AI products on breast cancer detection; another assesses how accurately AI predicts five-year breast cancer risk, and another examines how the benefit of AI-assisted reading varies with physician experience. Two appear in a society-recommended breast imaging session: one on AI independently reading mammograms with low suspicion of cancer to flag patients needing further testing, one on guiding follow-up exams for women with dense breast tissue. Two chest X-ray studies cover automated report generation and the safety of AI independently reading images likely to be normal. CEO Suh Beom-seok said the company would market actively on site so that the research leads to business results.

Why it matters

What the field most lacks is any comparison of different vendors' models on the same images, because each reports its own numbers on its own dataset, which makes their sensitivities mutually incomparable. A head-to-head of four FDA-cleared products, if the methodology holds, would be the most useful paper of the year. The other axis is autonomous reading — letting AI clear the probably-normal studies by itself is the most direct answer to the radiologist bottleneck and the hardest regulatory ask; Lunit is pushing it on both mammography and chest radiography at once.

Discount this

Lunit is itself one of the four products being compared, and a head-to-head run by an interested party needs its methodology and operating thresholds inspected. The announcement contains no figures at all — no sample size, no sensitivity, not even which four products — and names no collaborating institution. The report comes from the English edition of a Korean outlet rather than the company's own filing.

Outcome endpoint RSNA 2026Scientific programme11/29–12/3

The society put the counter-evidence on its own podium: stroke AI did not improve outcomes, microbleed AI caught more and shouted more

What

Of the twenty sessions listed in AuntMinnie's RSNA 2026 AI programme preview, a whole cluster reports negative or qualified findings: AI at a comprehensive stroke centre did not measurably improve patient outcomes (Dec 2, W3-SSNR10-3); microbleed AI on brain MRI flagged far more lesions than radiologists and far more false alarms (Dec 2, W3-SSNR10-6); vendor-reported metrics do not guarantee clinical performance (Dec 1, T7-SSNPM02-4); AI shifts diagnostic risk rather than eliminating it (Dec 2, W3-SSIN06-3); AI does not always reduce radiologists' mental workload and sometimes increases it (Nov 30, M3-SSIN02-3); and one talk asks how radiologists' use and documentation of AI could sway jurors, and with them malpractice outcomes (Nov 30, M3-SSIN02-8). The positive items in the same preview are equally specific: an open-source system detecting pancreatic cancer on routine CT nearly a year before clinical diagnosis (Nov 30, M3-SSGI03-3); deep learning on chest radiographs predicting cardiovascular risk about as accurately as the AHA PREVENT score (Nov 30, M6-SSCA03-1); and eleven triage algorithms rolled out across three hospitals with uneven real-world results (Dec 3, R3-SSIN07-1).

Why it matters

This is not critics complaining; it is the society's own scientific committee choosing these topics for the podium. Read side by side, this week's vendor releases and the meeting's programme form a complete control pair: the first measures turnaround time, sensitivity and descriptor acceptance, the second measures patient outcomes, false-alarm rates, cognitive load and legal exposure. Procurement decisions in medical AI are made almost entirely on the first set of numbers, while what determines whether a tool survives in a department is the second. The microbleed session is the one to attend — "catches more" is a virtue in an abstract title and, in a workflow, possibly several dozen extra lesions a day that someone has to rule out.

Discount this

This is a pre-meeting preview: most sessions come with a single sentence of conclusion and no sample size, no effect size, and no authors or institutions — AuntMinnie does not list them either. The page is marked "Page 1 of 2" and I read only the first, so this list is incomplete. None of these conclusions has appeared in a journal, and what is said at the podium may differ from the abstract.

Peer-reviewed Mount SinaiRadiology · Margulis Award11/29

Synthetic X-rays: only 41% noticed when unwarned, and radiologists read abnormalities off the fakes with 92.4% accuracy

What

The Radiology paper "The Rise of Deepfake Medical Imaging: Radiologists' Diagnostic Accuracy in Detecting ChatGPT-generated Radiographs" has won RSNA's 2026 Alexander R. Margulis Award for Scientific Excellence, given to the best original scientific article published in Radiology during the award year, to be presented on Nov 29 in Chicago to co-lead authors Mickael Tordjman and Murat Yuce of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The three-phase retrospective study used 17 radiologists from 12 centres in six countries (US, France, Germany, Turkey, UK, UAE), with 0 to 40 years of experience, and two image sets: 154 X-rays (77 GPT-4o synthetic) and 110 chest X-rays (55 RoentGen synthetic). Results: in the unwarned phase, 7 of 17 readers (41%) spontaneously identified synthetic images; once informed, mean classification accuracy was 74.8% on the GPT-4o set and 70% on the RoentGen set, with individuals ranging from 58% to 92%. Diagnostic accuracy for abnormalities was 91.3% on authentic images and 92.4% on synthetic ones. Among multimodal LLMs on the GPT-4o set, GPT-4o itself scored 85.1% and GPT-5 82.5%, both beating Gemini 2.5 Pro and Llama 4 Maverick, though no model caught every fake. Years of experience showed no correlation with detection, while musculoskeletal subspecialists were significantly more accurate. The authors' proposed defences are watermarking, cryptographic authentication and AI-based detection.

Why it matters

The reason this paper won is not the 74.8% but the 92.4%. Radiologists "diagnosed" abnormalities off wholly fabricated images more accurately than off real ones — meaning the synthetic images not only beat the authenticity check but passed cleanly through the entire clinical reading pipeline, yielding a specific diagnosis about a patient who does not exist. The whole safety argument for medical AI rests on a human looking at the end, and this paper measures that human not seeing it. Set beside the other seven items today: every vendor is using its own data to prove how accurate its model is, and nobody is verifying that the image was real.

Discount this

This is a reader study, not a real-world forgery: the radiologists knew they were in a study, the images came from the research team rather than a malicious third party, and there was no clinical context or prior imaging to cross-check against. Seventeen readers is a small sample. The paper itself appeared in March 2026 and is not new this week; it is carried today because the award is presented on Nov 29. The two sources differ slightly on the RoentGen set (the RSNA release gives a 62%–78% range, dotmed a 70% mean) and both are given here. I did not obtain the full text; the figures are relayed from RSNA's own release and dotmed's report.

Clearance a2z Radiology AIa2z-Omni-Triage11/19

Nine findings in one 510(k): abdominopelvic CT triage cleared by the FDA, with no performance figures published

What

a2z Radiology AI's a2z-Omni-Triage received FDA 510(k) clearance as adjunctive AI triage software for abdominopelvic CT, announced by the company on Nov 19 and reported by Diagnostic Imaging on Nov 20. It may be used on contrast-enhanced and non-contrast CT and flags nine suspicious findings: pneumothorax, moderate or large layering pleural effusion, complex pleural collection, bowel perforation, non-sacral pelvic fracture, sacral or coccygeal fracture, soft tissue abscess, moderate or severe hydronephrosis, and aortic aneurysm of 5 cm or larger.

Why it matters

Nine findings in a single clearance is unusually broad for a triage device — most CADt clearances cover one or two time-critical findings, such as intracranial haemorrhage or pulmonary embolism. Abdominopelvic CT is also the highest-volume cross-section in emergency imaging, so the potential footprint is large. But this is exactly what the RSNA session on vendor metrics is about: a broad indication does not mean every finding is equally reliable, and with nine spread under one 510(k) the buyer has little way of knowing which is a strength and which barely cleared.

Discount this

No performance data is public: no sensitivity, no specificity, no sample size, no validation dataset, and Diagnostic Imaging's report cites no study at all. Those would have to come from the FDA 510(k) summary or the manufacturer's own materials. The word "adjunctive" also deserves attention: devices in this class are positioned to prioritise and prompt, not to read autonomously.

Journal watch npj Digital MedicineECG · Voice · Governance11/18–11/22

Twelve papers in five days: foundation models move from imaging into ECG and voice, and three of the twelve are about governance rather than accuracy

What

npj Digital Medicine published 12 articles over the five days from Nov 18 to Nov 22. The most clinically oriented: ECG foundation models for cardiovascular screening in young competitive athletes (Nov 21); a multicentre evaluation of radiomics and multimodal AI for colorectal cancer diagnosis and survival prediction (Nov 19); a systematic review and meta-analysis of imaging-based biological age gap against mortality and incident disease (Nov 19); a multimodal digital biomarker for asthma combining vocal, clinical and demographic factors (Nov 22); Tele-Cranio, a CT-free solution for remote diagnosis and planning of craniomaxillofacial deformities (Nov 20); virtual atrial fibrillation dynamics mapped against clinical outcomes after catheter ablation (Nov 20); and epidemiology-informed graph neural networks for predicting transmissible hospital-acquired infections (Nov 20). On the deployment and governance side: a comparative evaluation of cloud versus on-premise LLMs for urgent patient-portal message screening (Nov 20); physician AI disclosure, condition severity and public trust (Nov 19); and a comment on the proposed reform of the EU Medical Device Regulation and its implications for AI-enabled devices (Nov 18).

Why it matters

The shape of this list carries more information than any single paper. First, the foundation-model idea is spilling out of imaging into ECG and voice — two signals far cheaper to acquire than CT or MRI, so if they work the economics of screening change. Second, three of twelve papers are not about accuracy at all but about choosing between on-premise and cloud, how the public views a physician who discloses AI use, and what the EU's regulatory reform would move — a ratio that is the reverse of two or three years ago. Third, the colorectal paper is multicentre and the biological-age one is a meta-analysis, both consolidation genres that tend to appear when a topic starts to mature.

Discount this

This item carries no effect sizes, because I could not retrieve the full texts. Article pages on nature.com failed to fetch throughout this run and only the journal's article listing was readable, so for every paper above only the title, date and link are verified; no sample size, AUC or sensitivity is quoted, and none should be inferred from a title. Several titles look like methodological or simulation work (the cardiac mechanics surrogate, the virtual atrial fibrillation dynamics) and should not be confused with clinical validation.

02 — Product Analysis

Two things that surfaced today sit at opposite ends of a spectrum: one reports its numbers to the decimal and is still investigational, the other is FDA-cleared and has published no number at all.

SimonMed Chest X-ray VLM

Automated outpatient chest X-ray reporting · SimonMed (US) · investigational

Function and position. A vision-language model that drafts reports directly from outpatient chest radiographs, pre-trained on more than 5 million image-report pairs, fine-tuned on over 150,000 SimonMed adult outpatient exams, and tested on a consecutive cohort from 143 outpatient centres. It is not being sold to hospitals but pointed at SimonMed's own nationwide outpatient network — one of the largest independent outpatient imaging operators in the US — so the training data and the deployment site are the same machines read by the same radiologists.

  • Strength : the effect of institution-specific fine-tuning is actually measured — pulmonary nodule sensitivity rising from 62.6% to 80.8% — the one measured value in the whole release that supports the claim that a generic model is not enough to deploy as-is.
  • Strength : testing used a consecutive cohort from 143 centres rather than a curated dataset, and consecutive enrolment is one of the harder designs to game in this literature.
  • Concern : the "modest" fall in specificity is not quantified. For a report-generation model the cost of a false positive is downstream imaging, and without that figure there is no way to judge what the 18-point sensitivity gain was bought with.
  • Concern : trained on and tested within the same network, its external validity is unknown; the model is still labelled investigational with no regulatory clearance, and the entire evidence base is currently one poster.

a2z-Omni-Triage

Adjunctive abdominopelvic CT triage · a2z Radiology AI · FDA 510(k) cleared

Function and position. Flags nine suspicious findings on abdominopelvic CT for worklist prioritisation, on both contrast-enhanced and non-contrast studies. It is positioned as adjunctive — prompting and ordering, not replacing interpretation. The buyers are hospitals and imaging centres with heavy emergency volume, and the pitch is moving the studies that need acting on to the front of the queue.

  • Strength : nine findings written into one 510(k) is far broader coverage than the typical CADt device handling one or two, and abdominopelvic CT is the largest single slice of emergency cross-sectional volume.
  • Concern : no performance figures are public at all — no sensitivity, specificity, sample size or validation dataset, and the coverage cites no study. With nine findings under one clearance, a buyer cannot tell which hold up and which barely cleared.
  • Concern : the real risk in a triage device is alarm fatigue, which is precisely the subject of RSNA's Dec 3 session on eleven triage algorithms producing uneven results across three hospitals — departments running several triage models at once are already measuring that cost.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan's institutional design is bet on exactly today's gap: not more accuracy papers, but randomised outcome trials

(1) The third of the MOHW's three AI centres does exactly what RSNA is talking about this year. The ministry subsidises 19 centres across three types: 10 Responsible AI Execution Centres (governance, transparency disclosure, explainability, lifecycle monitoring), 4 Clinical AI Validation Centres (a FHIR-based cross-hospital validation database and a federated learning platform to speed up approval), and 5 Clinical AI Impact Evaluation Centres — whose stated mission is, in so many words, to use randomised controlled trials to measure the real-world clinical benefit of AI against conventional care, as the scientific basis for future pricing and reimbursement decisions. Set that against today's through-line: everything the vendors filed this week is turnaround time, sensitivity and descriptor acceptance, while Taiwan's institutions tie reimbursement eligibility to randomised outcome data. That is not following a trend; it writes the field's emptiest square directly into a budget line.

(2) The three-problem framing comes from the ministry's own information office, and the failure it names is a Taiwanese company. Information Department director Lee Chien-chang, writing on the MOHW blog, splits the obstacles into implementation, validation and reimbursement: implementation maps to the Responsible AI centres, where vendors must publish nine transparency indicators and supply explainability analysis, with mandatory re-checks every three to six months because clinical data drifts; validation faces a bar of tens of thousands of cases, which no single hospital can assemble, hence the cross-tier hospital alliance pooling data; and reimbursement, since most AI tools cannot be paid for out of pocket while full insurance coverage is unrealistic, has to rest on controlled trials showing that AI plus a clinician beats the clinician alone. The same post names Deep01 — the first Taiwanese company to win US FDA clearance, which ceased operations after four to five years — as evidence that the local ecosystem is not yet mature. It is worth reading line by line, because it is the regulator's own diagnosis of its own market.

(3) Putting data standards first has already bought bargaining power in international partnerships. The MOHW and Roche Diagnostics signed an MOU on August 21, 2026, with chronic kidney disease as the first phase, using routine laboratory data for risk stratification and early warning of declining kidney function. What is notable is Roche's framing: Matthew Prime, who heads its global clinical decision support and algorithms business, said AI models must be continuously validated against real-world data to suit different countries' health systems, and that Taiwan's standardised data and cross-hospital validation make it a strong partner. Health Minister Shih Chung-liang's matching line was that "faced with the rapid global development of AI in medicine, Taiwan chose to build data standards and governance systems first." The same report notes the ministry's adoption of FHIR and its own FHIR Box technology, with Chang Gung, Mackay and China Medical University Hospital having completed real-time cross-hospital record exchange. Read the three together: what the international vendors are fighting over today is the moat of consecutive data, and Taiwan is using institutions to turn nationwide consecutive data into verifiable, reimbursable public infrastructure — something none of the six companies above can do on its own.

One inconsistency worth flagging: the number of validation centres differs between two official documents — the three-centres site (updated 2026-06) gives 4 Clinical AI Validation Centres and 5 Impact Evaluation Centres, while the information office's blog post (published 2025-03) gives 5 validation centres. Both are MOHW sources; this report gives both rather than picking one.

  2. Deepfake X-Rays Fool Radiologists and AI — RSNA press release (2026-03-24)

    The official release for the award-winning paper, and it carries something the secondary coverage lacks: a description of what gives the fakes away — over-smooth bone, unnaturally straight spines, symmetrical lungs, uniform vessel patterns, clean fractures confined to one side of the bone.     The regulator's own diagnosis of its market, with concrete obligations — nine transparency indicators, re-checks every three to six months, a validation bar in the tens of thousands of cases — and Deep01's closure named as evidence of an immature ecosystem. Anyone building medical AI in Taiwan should read it line by line.

  4. npj Digital Medicine — 依發表日排序的文章列表

    Not for any single paper but for the shape of the list: three of twelve papers in five days are about governance and deployment rather than accuracy, and foundation models are spilling into ECG and voice. Use it to recalibrate where you think the research frontier is.

  5. SimonMed leads largest global study of AI in X-ray, 258,373 exams across 26 countries — Health Tech Digital (2026-06-23)

    A rare large multi-country evaluation this year, and it reports its four modules separately: AZtrauma at 96.4% specificity against AZchest at 87.9%. That spread inside one product line is the clearest illustration of why a single product-level sensitivity figure means nothing.
