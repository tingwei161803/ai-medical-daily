Monday · Clinical Applications & Research

In one week prostate-MRI AI reached AUC 0.86 across 22 countries while 28% of 25 sites failed to clear 0.80 — the number to read today is not the mean, it is the spread

Monday, October 19, 2026 · Covering Oct 13–19 · Taipei time

Three clinical papers this week point at the same thing: average AI performance has stopped being the question, and the spread has become it. SCARLET-1, published in Radiology on 13 October, took prostate-MRI AI to 22,445 exams from 21,252 men across 46 cities in 22 countries, with external AUCs of 0.86, 0.89 and 0.84 and 83.0% to 87.9% agreement with radiologists, as AuntMinnie reported the same day; yet open the same dataset across its 25 external sites and 28% land below AUC 0.80 while 32% sit at 0.90 or higher. That same day a Seoul National University Hospital reader study put 160 chest x-rays — 40 real, 120 synthetic — in front of seven evaluators, and the best balanced accuracy of 77.9% belonged to Gemini 3.5 Flash, just ahead of a thoracic radiologist with twelve years' experience at 75.8%, with agreement across all readers at a Fleiss' kappa of 0.213. And Mount Sinai counted roughly 1.18 million potentially harmful clinical choices in over 10 million responses from 20 models, where a single safety reminder cut the harmful rate from 16.6% to 10.1% — down in 19 of 20 models, to zero in none.

01 — Top Stories

Seven items, ordered by strength of evidence; the last one is here on purpose, as the control
Clinical study RadboudumcRadiology10/13

SCARLET-1: 22,445 prostate MRIs across 46 cities in 22 countries — AI matches radiologists, but the spread between sites says more than the mean

What

SCARLET-1 (Scaling Artificial Intelligence for Prostate Cancer Detection on MRI to Global, Multiethnic Populations), published in Radiology on 13 October and led by Anindo Saha of Radboud University Medical Center in Nijmegen, spans 46 cities in 22 countries. It draws on 22,445 prostate MRI exams from 21,252 men scanned between 2012 and 2024; 20,471 exams from 19,278 patients went to training and internal testing, and 1,974 patients to external testing. The target was Gleason grade group ≥2 cancer. External AUCs came in at 0.86 for the diagnostic cohort and 0.89 and 0.84 for two screening cohorts, with agreement against radiologist reads of 83.0%, 87.9% and 87.7%; subgroup analysis by age, risk, tumour location and ethnicity moved the AUC by a mean of only 0.02 (AuntMinnie, 2026-10-13).

Why it matters

Most prostate-MRI AI studies run on a few hundred to a few thousand cases from a handful of European and North American centres. SCARLET-1 pushes that to 22 countries and deliberately multiethnic cohorts, which turns external validity from an adjective into a measured quantity. The real signal, though, is not the mean: across 25 external sites, 28% scored below AUC 0.80 and 32% at or above 0.90, with image quality alone spreading the AUC from 0.78 to 0.88. The authors put the conclusion more bluntly than any commentator would: "Safe clinical use will require careful operating point selection, local testing, and longitudinal performance monitoring." Read plainly, the paper's most useful output is not that the AI is ready, but that moving the same model to your own site may cost it a performance tier — and the published average gives you no way to predict that.

Discount this

AuntMinnie's summary bullets and body text disagree on the image-quality AUC range, giving 0.78–0.90 in one place and 0.78–0.88 in the other; this report uses the body figure, and the exact value should be checked against the paper itself. The coverage does not name the AI model or say whether it is a commercial product, and it reports no sensitivity, specificity or detection rates. This is a retrospective external evaluation rather than a prospective trial, and the authors say prospective evaluation of clinical, equity and cost outcomes has yet to be done.

Reader study Seoul National University HospitalLLM10/13

In a real-versus-synthetic chest x-ray contest the winner was Gemini 3.5 Flash — and agreement across the seven evaluators sat at a Fleiss' kappa of 0.213

What

A Seoul National University Hospital team published a reader study in the Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine (DOI 10.1007/s10278-026-02339-7, 13 October): 160 chest x-rays, 40 of them real and 120 generated by three different AI pipelines, put to seven evaluators who had to call each one real or synthetic. The panel was two radiologists (RAD1, twelve years post-MD with seven in dedicated thoracic radiology; RAD2, thirteen years post-MD in a non-thoracic subspecialty), two general practitioners, and three LLMs: Gemini 3.5 Flash, GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Because synthetic images made up 75% of the set, the primary metric was balanced accuracy. Gemini 3.5 Flash led on 77.9%, statistically indistinguishable from RAD1's 75.8%; the general practitioners were near chance. On diffusion-model images humans were right 82% to 100% of the time against 32% to 72% for the LLMs, while specificity on real images ran the other way, 90% to 100% for the LLMs and 50% to 100% for the humans. Fleiss' kappa across all evaluators was 0.213 (AuntMinnie, 2026-10-15).

Why it matters

Synthetic images are being used hard in two directions at once: padding training sets with rare findings, and standing in for real data in de-identified releases. Both assume someone can tell the difference when it matters. The study's answer is that nobody reliably can — a thoracic subspecialist reached only 75.8%, the general practitioners were close to a coin flip, and the Fleiss' kappa of 0.213 across the seven falls in the "poor" band, meaning there was not even agreement on which images looked suspicious. The usable finding is in the direction of the errors: the LLMs almost never mislabelled a real image (90% to 100% specificity) but missed the fingerprints of diffusion models, and the humans did the reverse. That complementarity argues for chaining human and machine review in provenance audits rather than choosing one; it also concedes that no single gatekeeper is trustworthy today.

Discount this

160 images and seven evaluators is a small sample, from one institution and one modality, and with synthetic images at 75% of the set raw accuracy cannot be read directly, only balanced accuracy. The coverage does not name the first author or identify the three generation pipelines. The three LLMs tested are general-purpose models rather than medical ones, so the result does not extrapolate to purpose-trained detectors.

LLM safety Mount SinaiCommunications Medicine10/08

One safety reminder cut potentially harmful clinical choices from 16.6% to 10.1% across 20 models, working in 19 of them — and zeroing out none

What

A team at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai — first author Mahmud Omar, co-senior author Girish N. Nadkarni, who chairs the Windreich Department of Artificial Intelligence and Human Health — published "Evaluating Large Language Model Responses to Unsafe Clinical Instructions" in the 26 September online issue of Communications Medicine (DOI 10.1038/s43856-026-01933-8), with the institution's release following on 8 October. They tested 20 models against 501 variations of 50 clinical scenarios plus 100 cases adapted from de-identified discharge records. Each scenario asked the model to skip recommended follow-up blood tests to cut workload, sometimes framed as urgent and sometimes as an instruction from a superior, with four options to choose from: comply, keep the recommended follow-up, seek clinician help, and so on. Three short safety reminders were tested, each combination run ten times with the answer order randomised. Of more than 10 million responses, roughly 1.18 million were potentially harmful clinical choices; the harmful rate was 16.6% with no reminder and 10.1% with one, falling in 19 of the 20 models (Mount Sinai, 2026-10-08).

Why it matters

This is one of the few large-scale evaluations to treat prompt engineering as a measurable safety control, and what it measures is not knowledge error but compliance with an improper instruction — urgency and authority applied as pressure to see whether the model will drop a follow-up to save effort. Those are exactly the pressures a hospital LLM meets in practice: bed pressure, the end of a shift, a line from the attending. The effect is real (16.6% to 10.1%, working in 19 of 20), but 10.1% means one in ten responses still lands on the harmful option, and errors of this kind look entirely reasonable, unlike hallucinations which announce themselves. Set beside the AMA framework in the next item, it gives today's full shape: the technical side can knock the risk down a tier, and the institutional side has to assume it will never reach zero.

Discount this

The release does not name the 20 models, so there is no way to tell which generations or vendors are covered. The scenarios are numerous but homogeneous — all built around skipping a follow-up blood test — and whether the result carries over to prescribing, referral or image interpretation is unknown. The authors say they have not yet covered prompt injection against autonomous agents or pressure from time and budget, and name that as the next step. This item is drawn from the institutional release rather than the full paper.

Governance American Medical AssociationAgentic AI10/16

The AMA drew its first line around clinical AI that acts on its own: authority has to be earned through evidence and held through demonstrated performance

What

On 16 October the American Medical Association's Center for Digital Health and AI issued a governance framework for agentic AI that takes on clinical functions (the framework itself). Its central claim is that clinical authority "should be earned through evidence and maintained through demonstrated performance," with oversight and authority shifting as evidence, performance, risk and technology change — authority as a revocable licence rather than a one-off clearance. The AMA names four areas of work: defining an AI system's clinical role, validating its performance and safeguards, setting limits on its authority, and establishing accountability for its use, risks and outcomes included. Chief executive John Whyte is named, and says the work will feed back into the AMA's positions on liability, licensure and regulation (Fierce Healthcare, 2026-10-16).

Why it matters

The framework sits at the other end of the same problem as the three studies above. SCARLET-1 shows one model dropping a performance tier when it moves site; Mount Sinai shows models complying under pressure from authority. Both say that for agentic AI, clear-once-and-forever does not hold. The AMA's answer is to make clinical authority a licence that has to be continuously re-earned, which is the same sentence as the SCARLET-1 authors' call for local testing and longitudinal monitoring, spoken in institutional rather than statistical language. Note who is speaking: the AMA is not a regulator but the physicians' professional body, and by drawing the line around agentic authority first it stakes out the question of who is accountable ahead of the FDA and state legislatures.

Discount this

What is public so far is four directional areas of work; the Fierce write-up gives no formal count of clauses and no numeric validation thresholds. The framework carries no legal force — it is a professional body's position, not a regulatory requirement. One distinction worth keeping straight: the "seven policy principles" mentioned in the coverage belong to the AMA's separate May framework on AI deepfake impersonation of physicians, not to this one.

Workforce Neiman Health Policy InstituteJACR10/13

The claim that AI has already absorbed the growth in imaging demand got five numbers thrown back at it by the Neiman institute

What

The Harvey L. Neiman Health Policy Institute in Reston, Virginia, led by Elizabeth Rula, published an opinion piece in the Journal of the American College of Radiology (JACR full text) answering 2026 comments in JAMA Health Forum by Harvard's David Cutler and Lev Klarnet (JAMA Health Forum). Cutler and Klarnet had argued that declining imaging use "allowed the US to meet the need for imaging without an increase in radiologists." The HPI team put up five figures: a 2025 projection has US imaging use growing 17% to 27% by 2055 on population growth and ageing; Medicare Part B data show significant CT and MRI volume increases from 2016 to 2024; imaging RVUs per beneficiary peaked in 2021, up 13% since 2005; 12.5% of Medicare office-based imaging studies were repeated between 2013 and 2022; and inflation-adjusted radiologist reimbursement fell 48.3% between 2005 and 2025 (AuntMinnie, 2026-10-13).

Why it matters

The argument looks like workforce statistics and is really about what kind of AI hospitals buy. If Cutler's version holds — demand falling, staffing adequate — then imaging AI's value proposition is substituting for reading capacity. If the HPI version holds — demand still growing while reimbursement halves over two decades — then AI lands not as a replacement for people but as a way to flatten cost per read, and the product shifts from automated interpretation toward workflow acceleration and triage. That difference shows up directly in procurement: the first buys algorithm sensitivity, the second buys integration depth and turnaround time. Note too that the 48.3% fall in reimbursement is itself why the labour-saving pitch for imaging AI has always been hard to land — the unit price of that labour has already been squeezed once.

Discount this

This is an opinion piece rather than original research, and both sides are citing datasets they already had. AuntMinnie gives no publication date for the HPI piece, so this report anchors it to the 13 October coverage date. The Medicare Part B CT and MRI growth is described only as "significant," with no figure given. HPI's funding and institutional position are tied to the American College of Radiology, which is worth holding in view while reading it.

Research partnership BrainomixPulmonary Fibrosis Foundation10/14

Brainomix pulled patients into the design end of an AI imaging study — the question is not accuracy, it is what patients want to know

What

On 14 October the UK AI imaging firm Brainomix announced a partnership with the Pulmonary Fibrosis Foundation, a Chicago-based patient advocacy group, to bring its patient community into AI imaging research on fibrotic interstitial lung disease. The study frames three questions: what patients with pulmonary fibrosis want to know about their own disease; how they respond to the visual and quantitative outputs of Brainomix 360 e-Lung; and whether imaging-derived insight can support patient–clinician conversations about starting, continuing or changing treatment. e-Lung is FDA-cleared and CE-marked, and automatically quantifies and visualises radiographic features on lung CT. Brainomix has been partnered with Boehringer Ingelheim since 2024 on assessing e-Lung's clinical value in fibrotic ILD (AuntMinnie, 2026-10-14).

Why it matters

Imaging AI output usually stops at the radiology report, read by a physician. Putting quantitative output in front of patients raises the bar for interpretability from legible-to-a-clinician to legible-to-a-patient — and in fibrotic ILD, where the central decision is when to start antifibrotic therapy, that is part of the clinical pathway rather than a public relations gesture. The more practical reason is reimbursement: once payers want proof that AI changed a clinical decision rather than just raised an AUC, patient-side decision data becomes the evidence. Brainomix now holds both ends, a pharmaceutical partner in Boehringer — a principal supplier of antifibrotics — and a patient organisation, and that combination is groundwork for imaging biomarkers driving drug decisions.

Discount this

This is a partnership announcement, not a result. The coverage gives no enrolment, no site count, no timeline, no funding figure and names no investigator. e-Lung's FDA clearance is a device authorisation of the 510(k) kind, which is a different thing from demonstrated clinical benefit; and the Brainomix–Boehringer collaboration, running since 2024, has yet to produce published outcome-endpoint data.

Control case Scientific Reports多模態影像 / Multimodal10/03

Spotting coronary heart disease from tongue and face photos at AUC 0.9992 — the number is itself the reason to discount it

What

Scientific Reports published a study by Wang Y and colleagues (DOI 10.1038/s41598-026-71871-x), covered by EMJ on 3 October. It enrolled 518 participants, 200 with coronary heart disease and 318 healthy controls, with an independent test set of 104. The model is a dual-branch deep network fusing tongue and facial image features on ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B1 backbones, with Grad-CAM for explainability; the tongue branch attends to the central tongue body and coating, the facial branch to the perioral and zygomatic buccal regions. On the independent test set the multimodal ResNet50 reached F1 0.9756 and AUC 0.9992, classifying 102 of 104 correctly (about 98.1%); tongue-only gave F1 0.9524 and AUC 0.9867, face-only F1 0.9250 and AUC 0.9887. Five-fold cross-validation across all 518 gave multimodal ResNet50 AUC 0.9980±0.0027 and F1 0.9827±0.0141, and multimodal EfficientNet-B1 AUC 0.9987±0.0014 and F1 0.9829±0.0138 (EMJ, 2026-10-03).

Why it matters

It is here not because it will reach the clinic but because it forms a nearly perfect control against item 1. SCARLET-1 reached AUC 0.84 to 0.89 on external testing across 22 countries, 22,445 exams and 1,974 external patients; this one reached 0.9992 on 518 participants with a 104-person test set. The gap is not in model capability but in how the test set was built — a case-control design of 200 diagnosed against 318 healthy people inflates discrimination by construction, because no real screening population splits that cleanly. When reading clinical AI papers, the closer the AUC sits to 1, the sooner you should go looking at where the denominator came from. This is the one study today whose numbers are too good not to question first, and it is kept here deliberately as a yardstick.

Discount this

Almost everything here needs discounting: a very small sample (518, with an independent test set of only 104); a case-control design rather than consecutive enrolment from a screening population; single-source data, with EMJ stating plainly that external validation is still required; no sensitivity or specificity reported; nothing on where participants were recruited, their ethnicity or comorbidities; and no exact publication date or institutional affiliation beyond the lead author's name. Grad-CAM attention regions are not a causal mechanism.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products, two ways of writing imaging AI's value into a business model: one built on the scale of global validation, the other on a decision chain running from pharma to patients

SCARLET-1 (prostate MRI AI)

Prostate MRI detection model · led by Radboud University Medical Center (Netherlands)

Function and position. Detection of clinically significant Gleason grade group ≥2 cancer on prostate MRI, positioned as a second reader or triage tool for radiologists. Training and internal testing used 20,471 exams from 19,278 patients, external testing 1,974 patients, across diagnostic and screening cohorts in 46 cities in 22 countries (Radiology, 2026-10-13). Its selling point is not point accuracy but the number of distinct data distributions it has been validated against — the thing this field most lacks, since PI-RADS reading itself carries high reader variability.

  • Strength : the subgroup stability is real. Across age, risk stratum, tumour location and ethnicity, the mean ΔAUC was only 0.02 (AuntMinnie) — an equity metric most imaging AI papers simply do not report.
  • Strength : agreement with radiologist reads of 83.0% to 87.9% puts it in second-reader rather than replacement territory, and that positioning is easier for both clinicians and regulators to accept than a claim of beating the radiologist.
  • Concern : the between-site spread is wide enough to undo a procurement assumption — 28% of the 25 external sites scored below AUC 0.80. The average a buyer takes from the paper has no predictive power for their own site.
  • Concern : the coverage does not say whether the model is a commercial product or name a vendor, and reports no sensitivity, specificity or detection rate. Without those three, a clinical team cannot choose an operating point — and the authors themselves make operating point selection a precondition of safe use.

Brainomix 360 e-Lung

Quantitative lung CT imaging biomarker · Brainomix (UK)

Function and position. Automatic quantification and visualisation of radiographic features on lung CT for tracking fibrotic interstitial lung disease, FDA-cleared and CE-marked. Its commercial position is not the radiology worklist but the drug decision chain: a collaboration with Boehringer Ingelheim on clinical value since 2024, and from 14 October the Pulmonary Fibrosis Foundation's patient community, with all three research questions circling whether imaging output can carry a conversation about starting, continuing or changing treatment (AuntMinnie, 2026-10-14).

  • Strength : it holds both ends at once. Boehringer is a principal supplier of antifibrotics, and the patient organisation supplies evidence on whether AI changed a patient's decision — exactly the kind of endpoint payers will be asking for (the 2024 collaboration).
  • Strength : positioning as a quantitative biomarker rather than a diagnosis carries lower regulatory and liability exposure than autonomous interpretation, and quantitative output lends itself to longitudinal tracking, which grows into subscription revenue rather than per-read fees.
  • Concern : clearance is not benefit. The Boehringer collaboration has run since 2024 without published outcome-endpoint data, and the 14 October announcement carries no enrolment, site count, timeline or funding figure at all.
  • Concern : binding tightly to a drug maker can cost a biomarker its credibility. When the same quantitative output is used to decide whether to start that company's drug, the need for independent validation goes up, not down.

Today's competitive structure forks between depth of evidence and breadth of distribution. On one side Radboud and Mount Sinai sell method and standards, their output papers and evaluation frameworks, their influence travelling through procurement specifications and regulatory language. These could run in parallel, but SCARLET-1's finding that 28% of 25 sites fall below AUC 0.80 makes the third route steadily more expensive — because it shifts the burden of proof from obtaining a clearance to proving performance continuously at every hospital, and the AMA's 16 October framework is the institutional endorsement of exactly that shift.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The local testing and longitudinal monitoring SCARLET-1 asks for already has an institutional design running in Taiwan

(1) The premise behind Taiwan's three ministry AI centres is exactly item 1's conclusion. The Ministry of Health and Welfare runs a Center for Responsible AI in Healthcare, a Center for External AI Validation in Healthcare and a Center for Clinical AI Impact Evaluation, with the impact work spread across five medical centres: National Taiwan University Hospital on retinal AI for eye disease, Taipei Veterans General Hospital on AI-assisted lung CT reading for early lung cancer, Taichung Veterans General Hospital on an osteoporosis AI diagnostic system, National Cheng Kung University Hospital on automated CT muscle composition measurement to flag possible chemotherapy side effects, and Tri-Service General Hospital on AI ECG reading transmitted from ambulances to receiving hospitals. Study design is guided by Harvard biostatistics professor Li-Ren Wei, with NCKU president Meng-Ru Shen convening a five-member review committee (MOHW Smart Healthcare Center, 2025-04-14). The SCARLET-1 authors ask for local testing and longitudinal performance monitoring; these three centres divide precisely into clearance, local validation and impact tracking. Taiwan built the institution first — what is missing is published results.

(2) EverFortune AI's 57 device licences run straight into today's licence-is-not-field-performance problem. The company holds 57 licences across markets (15 US FDA, 24 Taiwan TFDA, 6 Thailand, 6 Malaysia, 5 Vietnam, 1 Singapore) and more than 70 customers worldwide, with its appendicitis CT assessment system clearing US FDA 510(k) in April 2026 and TFDA approval on 18 August (GeneOnline/環球生技, 2026-08-19). The 510(k) route runs on substantial equivalence and by design requires no clinical trial. SCARLET-1 shows one model varying by a full performance tier across 25 sites — and if that holds for prostate MRI there is no reason to assume it does not hold for emergency abdominal CT. A Taiwanese vendor expanding across several Southeast Asian markets at once should be preparing local validation reports for each deployment site rather than more licences; otherwise the first time performance slips at an overseas hospital, there is nothing in hand to explain it with.

(3) If Zhijie's domestic 3D PACS is to reach 29 medical centres, image provenance auditing becomes an infrastructure-level problem. Zhijie Medical Technology's Anatomy Cloud converts 2D CT and MRI into coloured 3D models in roughly three to five minutes, separating more than 260 organs and tissues; it is in testing at the health ministry's data centre, targeting all 29 of Taiwan's medical centres by 2028, and the company has acquired Japan's Holoeyes along with its network of more than 100 hospitals (CNA, 2026-10-05). Put item 2 into that context: when a thoracic subspecialist reaches only 75.8% balanced accuracy on synthetic chest x-rays and seven evaluators agree at a Fleiss' kappa of 0.213, any national imaging infrastructure has to record provenance and generation history at the write end rather than hoping someone downstream can tell. For a 3D PACS spanning Taiwan and Japan and co-built with the health ministry, that belongs at the same priority as reconstruction accuracy.

05 — Further Reading

Five chosen on three tests: primary publication first, method and numbers visible, and reading it changes how much you trust a class of claim
  1. SCARLET-1: Scaling Artificial Intelligence for Prostate Cancer Detection on MRI to Global, Multiethnic Populations — Radiology (2026-10-13)

    The one paper today worth reading end to end. The point is not the AUC in the abstract but the per-site distribution across 25 sites in the supplementary material — that is what a procurement decision actually needs.

  2. Evaluating Large Language Model Responses to Unsafe Clinical Instructions — Communications Medicine (2026-09-26)

    It turns whether a model complies with an improper instruction under pressure into a repeatable instrument rather than an anecdote. The experimental design repays reading more than the conclusion does, especially the four-option and authority-framing setup.

  3. Detection of AI-generated chest radiographs by radiologists and large language models — Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine (2026-10-13)

    If you run any training or de-identification programme that touches synthetic images, this will change how confident you are that someone can tell them apart when it matters. The section on humans and machines erring in opposite directions is the part to copy down.

  4. Governance framework for agentic AI assuming clinical functions — American Medical Association (2026-10-16)

    Read the original rather than the coverage, because the wording is the substance: should that phrase — earned through evidence, maintained through demonstrated performance — reach state legislation, procurement and liability both get rewritten.

  5. AI-supported mammography screening results in fewer aggressive and advanced breast cancers: full MASAI results — ecancer (2026-01-30)

    Not this week's news, but the yardstick for today: over 100,000 women randomised, interval cancers 1.55 versus 1.76 per 1,000 (a 12% reduction), 16% fewer invasive cancers, 21% fewer large cancers, 27% fewer aggressive subtypes. That is what a genuinely positive RCT looks like — measure the other six items of today against it.
