Saturday · General AI

On a single day — 15 October — three companies put three different kinds of "big" on the same table; and the same day's Nature paper handed an AI the PET-CT scans of 101,243 patients and asked it to find brown fat

Saturday, October 17, 2026 · Covering Oct 12–16 · Taipei time

Line up the three releases of 15 October and you find them measuring three different dimensions of the same thing. Microsoft AI measured frontier pre-training itself: MAI-Base-2 is a mixture-of-experts model with 2.8T total and 80B active parameters, trained on 16,000 GB200 GPUs over 38 days on a 50T-token mixture, 18% of it VLM data — but the weights stay in, to be licensed to "trusted partners" through Foundry. Meta measured who can get hold of one: Muse Spark Open's 816B total parameters, 42B active and 1M-token context went up the same day on Hugging Face, SageMaker, Google Cloud Model Garden and Microsoft Foundry, and it runs on two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios — about 1 TB of unified memory between them — under MLX. NVIDIA measured how many users a single GPU can serve: Nemotron 3.5 Super VL has only 120B total and about 12B active parameters, yet it was the one of the three that could name healthcare customers on the day — R1 for medical claims analysis and revenue cycle management, TrainLoop post-training it on dermatology data from Nolla. What actually put "AI reading" through peer review that same day was the brown-fat paper in Nature: 101,243 adults who had had clinical 18F-FDG-PET-CT scans, 8,709 of them BAT-positive, with an AI semi-automatically quantifying metabolic activity across six depots — and, after matching, 58% lower odds of type 2 diabetes and 47% lower odds of chronic ischaemic heart disease. The three model releases are statements about capability. The Nature paper is a statement about whom capability lands on.

01 — Top Stories

Five stories, ordered capability → deployment → governance: three model releases from a single day, the paper pushing AI into biology, and the one that began drawing boundaries around capability
Frontier pre-training Microsoft AIMAI-Base-210/15

Microsoft's own 2.8-trillion-parameter base model: 16,000 GB200s for 38 days on 50T tokens, claimed at 4.2–12.3x the FLOPs efficiency of DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base — and the weights stay in

What

On 15 October, Microsoft AI published the pre-training details of MAI-Base-2: a mixture-of-experts model with 2.8T total and 80B active parameters, larger and sparser than MAI-Base-1 and with MoE in every layer where the previous generation alternated MoE and dense. It is natively vision-capable through an encoder-free VLM architecture, and uses the Muon optimizer with a dropless multi-round MoE design and activation gradient clipping. Training ran across August and September 2026 on 16,000 GB200 GPUs for 38 days at 21% average MFU and 89% goodput, over a 50T-token mixture of which 18% was VLM data; the corpus grew about 36% in unique tokens over MAI-Base-1, and Microsoft states all of it is organic rather than distilled from other models. Evaluation is on held-out text (bits-per-byte), code, STEM, maths and Q&A, and Microsoft reports lower BPB than every open-weight model it tested — DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base, Kimi K3, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B and MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL. Pre-training is complete and post-training underway, with weights to be licensed to "trusted partners" through Foundry.

Why it matters

This is the most granular account Microsoft has given of doing frontier pre-training itself — GPU count, days, MFU, goodput and optimizer choice all on the page. For healthcare buyers the significance is not the model, whose context length and expert count are still unpublished, but that Microsoft is converting its dependence on OpenAI into a second leg: the base model a health system buys through Epic, Nuance/DAX or Azure may in future have more than one lineage. The encoder-free native VLM choice matters particularly for image-heavy clinical settings — the model is not a text model with a vision encoder bolted on, but something that looked at pictures and read words from the first day of pre-training.

Discount this

Every figure is Microsoft's own, with no third-party audit. The BPB comparison carries a caveat Microsoft itself flags: of the four baselines, only DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base was available as a base model, the other three only as post-trained checkpoints, which is not a clean basis for a bits-per-byte comparison. The FLOPs-efficiency claim appears as 4.2–12.3x in the body and 4–12x in the introduction. Mustafa Suleyman separately told Business Insider it used about a quarter of a comparable OpenAI model's compute, also on private tests, reaching this report through secondary coverage. There are no public post-trained scores such as SWE-Bench, no pricing and no firm availability date.

Open weights MetaMuse Spark Open10/15

Meta put the weights of an 816B natively multimodal model in the open — a 1M-token context, two Mac Studios enough to run it — under a licence with commercial limits

What

Also on 15 October, Meta released Muse Spark Open: 816B total parameters, 42B active per token, a 1M-token context window, natively multimodal, and described by Meta as "the most capable US open-weight model to date." The licence is the Muse Model License, with limited commercial terms that Meta says let many developers use it for free. It runs on two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios under MLX — up to 512 GB of unified memory each, about 1 TB combined, on a chip with 1.2 TB/s of memory bandwidth. Inference is supported through SGLang, vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, Hugging Face Transformers, llama.cpp and Unsloth, with optimizations for AMD, Apple, Intel and NVIDIA; SGLang kernel speedups are reported at 1.03–3.95x on NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. The weights are on Hugging Face, Amazon SageMaker AI, Google Cloud Model Garden and Microsoft Foundry, with hosted APIs from Baseten, Fireworks AI, Groq, Modal, Nebius, OpenRouter and Together AI.

Why it matters

"Two Mac Studios will run an 816B natively multimodal model" means something quite different to a hospital than to a developer. What hospitals want is rarely the strongest model; it is that records and images never leave their own machine room — and until now that condition cost you a small model. Here it buys a 1M-token context with native vision. A whole inpatient course, every lab trend and every imaging report fits in one pass, with no chunking and no RAG retrieval, a qualitative difference for clinical summarization and chart review. llama.cpp and Unsloth are both on the support list, so the bar for community fine-tuning drops too: a hospital can LoRA on its own de-identified data without sending any of it out.

Discount this

"The most capable US open-weight model to date" is Meta's own claim, and the release links an evaluation methodology report without listing a single score, so that ranking could not be checked on the day — still less how it performs on clinical tasks. The licence is not MIT or Apache either: the Muse Model License carries commercial limits that have to be read term by term, and a health system putting it inside a paid clinical product will not find the legal work lighter than with a genuinely open model. The 1.03–3.95x SGLang figure is a very wide range whose bottom end is close to no speedup at all. And "runs on two Mac Studios" says nothing about how fast — there is no tokens/s figure, and inference latency decides clinical usability more often than peak capability does.

Multimodal agent NVIDIANemotron 3.5 Super VL10/15

NVIDIA's 120B open agentic model named healthcare customers on day one: R1 for claims and revenue cycle, TrainLoop post-training it on Nolla's dermatology data

What

The same day, NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, an open 120-billion-parameter model (the Hugging Face weights are named 120B-A12B, indicating about 12B active). NVIDIA calls it "the fastest open agentic model in its class," about 5x faster than the next best; on NVIDIA Dynamo it can serve up to 8x more users with 85% lower latency. Training involved about 26 trillion tokens of pre-training and continued pre-training, then post-training on roughly 3.8 million samples plus reinforcement learning; NVIDIA says accuracy is comparable to its largest reasoning model at about one-fifth the parameter count. It ships with open weights, open datasets and training recipes, and is available on Hugging Face, OpenRouter, ModelScope and as an NVIDIA NIM, running on DGX Spark, DGX Station, RTX PRO and RTX systems, NVIDIA Jetson, and in data centre or cloud environments. The use cases are multimodal agent tasks: instruction following, tool use, multistep workflows, and reading OCR text, tables, charts, PDFs and web pages. In healthcare, R1 uses it for medical claims analysis and revenue cycle management, and TrainLoop post-trained it on dermatology data from Nolla; other sectors named are insurance fraud screening, financial credit review, cybersecurity and engineering design.

Why it matters

This is the only one of the three releases with healthcare in the day-one customer column, and it names two quite different kinds of use. R1's claims and revenue cycle work is back-office — precisely the 36.2% of real clinical AI use that documentation and administration accounted for in the NYU study cited in this daily on 15 October, and precisely the part benchmarks barely cover; the capability list of OCR, tables, PDFs and charts is a description of what claims processing handles every day. TrainLoop's dermatology post-training is something else again: it shows that a 120B model with ~12B active is small enough for a third party to reshape with specialty data without owning a pre-training cluster. Together they suggest that while frontier labs compare trillion-parameter counts, what actually walks into the healthcare back office is a mid-sized model that serves many users off one GPU.

Discount this

"Fastest," "5x," "8x more users" and "85% lower latency" are all NVIDIA's own, and the blog shows Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index charts without a single score, so the in-class comparison cannot be checked. The ~12B active figure is inferred from a Hugging Face filename, not stated in NVIDIA's text. The licence is never named — only "open weights, open datasets and training recipes," three phrases that mean very different things in different vendors' hands. Both healthcare examples are customer names supplied by the vendor, with no deployment scale, no accuracy figure, no third-party evaluation and no clinical outcome at all; a dermatology-post-trained model is a long way from FDA-cleared assistive diagnosis.

Medical imaging · peer-reviewed Nature18F-FDG-PET-CT10/15

Nature: an AI phenotyped brown fat across 101,243 PET-CT scans — 58% lower odds of type 2 diabetes, 47% lower odds of chronic ischaemic heart disease — and pushed losartan forward as a research candidate

What

On 15 October a study titled "Artificial intelligence-enabled clinical phenotyping of human brown fat" appeared in Nature (Gómez-Banoy, N., et al., 2026; DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-11162-7). It drew on 101,243 adults who had clinical 18F-FDG-PET-CT scans between June 2009 and July 2024, with an AI image-analysis system semi-automatically quantifying brown adipose tissue metabolic activity in the 8,709 BAT-positive patients by combining glucose tracer uptake and tissue volume across six depots; controls were matched by propensity score on age, sex, BMI and outdoor ambient temperature. BAT-positive individuals had 58% lower odds of type 2 diabetes and 47% lower odds of chronic ischaemic heart disease, plus lower odds of primary hypertension, and BAT presence was also associated with lower odds of depressive episodes and anxiety disorders. Axillary BAT was most strongly tied to favourable cardiometabolic outcomes and to less visceral fat; BAT presence was additionally linked to lower vertebral bone-density scores, whose significance is unclear. On drugs, angiotensin receptor blockers were positively associated with BAT status; in cultured brown adipocytes losartan induced UCP1 and raised oxygen consumption, and in mice it increased energy expenditure at room temperature and during cold exposure without measurable weight loss, acting through PPARγ signalling independently of the classical AT1 receptor.

Why it matters

The method here deserves more attention than the finding. Human brown-fat research has been stuck at the same bottleneck: measuring BAT requires cold-stimulated PET-CT, recruitment is expensive, and cohorts stay in the dozens to the low hundreds. What this paper does is treat fifteen years of clinical scans taken for cancer staging as an existing imaging biobank, and use an AI to pull out a phenotype nobody had set out to measure — turning an unaffordable prospective cohort into retrospective data already sitting in PACS. For medical AI as a field that is a more concrete demonstration of capability than any benchmark score: the most immediate value may not be replacing readers, but letting existing images answer questions that were never fundable. And the line running from an imaging association to UCP1 induction and oxygen-consumption experiments with losartan shows how such mining can generate a testable mechanistic hypothesis rather than just a pile of correlations.

Discount this

The authors draw the lines themselves: the study is retrospective and restricted to a cancer-centre population, so all 101,243 had an oncological reason to be scanned and are not a general population; no scan involved prior cold stimulation; and glucose uptake is only an indirect measure of thermogenesis. The authors state plainly that the findings cannot establish that brown fat prevents disease. The coverage reports odds ratios only, with no hazard ratios. Losartan's effect on human brown fat has never been tested in a clinical trial, and even the mouse work showed no weight loss — any reading of it as a metabolic or weight-loss drug runs far past the evidence. The AI image-analysis system is not named and no validation metrics for it are given. These details also reach this report through News-Medical's coverage rather than the Nature paper itself.

AI safety Hinton · Bengio · RussellAnthropic Frontier Red Team10/12 · 10/16

420 scientists asked for cyber-capable models to be airgapped by default — and four days earlier Anthropic's own numbers were: fifteen $55 Android TV boxes running a 26B model, and GLM-5.2 spreading to all eight nodes in four of five trials

What

On 16 October an open letter signed by 420 scientists became public, with signatories including Geoffrey Hinton of the University of Toronto, Yoshua Bengio of the Université de Montréal, Stuart Russell of Berkeley and Herbert Bos of Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, who shared it on LinkedIn; researchers from Oxford, Cambridge, UCL and Imperial College London are also on the list. The demands: organizations developing, deploying and testing cyber-capable models should immediately adopt containment systems giving high assurance that the models cannot act outside their permitted environments; as an initial precaution, keep such models airgapped; for models that remain connected, balance their permitted actions against the limited ability to predict whether those actions could cause a security failure; improve alignment alongside stronger sandboxing; establish an international body, in consultation with governments and AI and cybersecurity experts, to standardize alignment and containment practice and track containment failures; report such incidents to that body with eventual public disclosure; and increase collaboration between AI and security researchers.

Four days before the letter, on 12 October, William Zhou and Daniel Freeman of Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research putting numbers on that threat. They ran Gemma4-26B-A4B distributed across fifteen Android TV boxes at about $55 and 4GB RAM each, two model layers per box, for 0.39 tokens/s; a 9B model got inference running on 8 of 9 hardware types; and the end-to-end replication test used 8 GCP nodes running Android 15 emulators inside a firewalled VPC, where GLM-5.2 was the only open-weight model to complete the process reliably — reaching all 8 nodes in 4 of 5 trials and 7 in the fifth, while the others failed consistently. Notably GLM-5.2 did not copy itself: it configured the devices to run Llama3.1-8B while operating separately. In an "inheritance" experiment, Qwen3.5-9B's inference-setup success rose from 45% in the first generation to 61–84% in later ones with no change to the model weights — the gain came from passing along notes from earlier attempts. Anthropic estimates that, on a historical trend of about 13.7 Epoch Capabilities Index points per year, a small model could reach GLM-5.2's level by the end of 2026; and that a Kimwolf-sized network of more than two million devices would supply inference capacity equal to roughly 40 H100s. On defence they point to network-flow anomaly detection, ISP quarantine of infected hosts — which cleared 92% of Mirai infections within 14 days — and stronger default IoT security.

Why it matters

These belong in one story because they are two halves of one argument: the letter says keep these models shut in, and Anthropic's research says why. Health systems sit awkwardly in this. Hospitals are among the densest IoT environments anywhere, with the longest device lifecycles and the hardest operating systems to update — infusion pumps, monitors, imaging workstations, bedside terminals, each a scaled-up version of that $55 Android box in the study, and none of them casually taken offline to patch. The implication of Anthropic's inheritance numbers — 45% to 84% with unchanged weights, purely by passing notes along — is that pinning capability assessment to a model version is not enough, because capability can accumulate in the environment without the model changing. Separately, The Neuron's 16 October digest notes that Bank of America has brought in about 1,000 outside contractors, alongside roughly 3,000 existing staff, to fix flaws that advanced AI models including Anthropic's Mythos uncovered in its own and vendors' software, with CEO Brian Moynihan citing quarterly costs of "a couple hundred million dollars plus" while grouping that figure with anti-money-laundering expenses. That is the order of magnitude a large regulated institution is actually paying on this question — and health systems' security budgets run far below large banks'.

Discount this

Anthropic marks the limits more clearly than most coverage: the exploit was deliberately trivial, an unauthenticated ADB port, so the results are an upper bound on real-world success rather than an estimate; the Epoch index is a rough proxy that does not measure replication; the compute estimate is anchored to one class of device and one past botnet; the Memento harness improves reliability but is not universally strong; and they did not test whether an AI botnet could counter defenders. They also state plainly that they have not demonstrated a fully autonomous AI worm, nor a model small enough to run on cheap consumer hardware and complete the whole process on its own. Small models were weak at spearphishing, so social engineering remains more plausibly a large-model threat, and access to a frontier "advisor" model, Opus 4.8, produced no statistically significant improvement. On the letter: the coverage does not say who organized it and does not link to the letter itself; the headline says "more than 400" while the body says 420; there is no timetable for the international body and no deadline for public disclosure; and the letter mentions neither biosecurity nor healthcare — connecting it to hospital IoT above is this report's inference, not a claim in the letter.

02 — Product Analysis

Two open-weight models released the same day, nearly sevenfold apart in size, competing for the same healthcare buyers — one selling "it all fits in," the other "how many people one GPU serves"

Muse Spark Open

816B total / 42B active, 1M-token context, natively multimodal open weights · Meta (US)

Function and position. It sells the ceiling of capability, not deployment economics. For healthcare buyers the core value is the 1M-token context with native vision: an entire inpatient course, lab trends and imaging reports in one pass, with no chunking and no RAG. What gets it into a hospital is the hardware floor — two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios under MLX, up to 512 GB of unified memory each and about 1 TB combined: a specification a hospital IT department can buy and never connect to an outside network.

  • Strength : distribution with almost no gaps — weights up simultaneously on Hugging Face, SageMaker AI, Google Cloud Model Garden and Microsoft Foundry, seven hosted-API providers, and inference support including llama.cpp and Unsloth. In practice: pilot in the cloud, move in-house later without changing model; and llama.cpp/Unsloth mean a hospital can LoRA on its own de-identified data without sending any of it out.
  • Concern : no published benchmark score of any kind, so "the most capable US open-weight model" cannot be verified, still less its clinical-task performance; the licence is the Muse Model License with commercial limits, not MIT or Apache, so the legal work for a paid clinical product is no lighter; and there is no tokens/s figure, while inference latency decides clinical usability more often than peak capability does.

Nemotron 3.5 Super VL

120B total / ~12B active open multimodal agentic model · NVIDIA (US)

Function and position. It sells unit economics, not the ceiling. NVIDIA calls it the fastest in its class at about 5x the next best, serving up to 8x more users with 85% lower latency on Dynamo, with accuracy said to match its largest reasoning model at about a fifth of the parameters. The capability list points precisely at back-office work: instruction following, tool use, multistep workflows, and reading OCR text, tables, charts, PDFs and web pages — close to a job description for medical claims and revenue cycle work, and NVIDIA named the customer on the day.

  • Strength : the only one of the three same-day releases in production in healthcare — R1 on claims and revenue cycle, TrainLoop post-training on Nolla's dermatology data, one administrative and one specialty; and claims is exactly the documentation-and-administration category that was 36.2% of real clinical AI use in the NYU study cited here on 15 October. The deployment range is unusually wide, from Jetson to the data centre, which for device makers means the same model family can move toward embedded hardware.
  • Concern : the performance claims are all the vendor's own and the charts carry no scores, so "fastest" and the 5x/8x/85% figures cannot be verified against peers; the ~12B active count is inferred from the Hugging Face filename 120B-A12B rather than stated; and the licence is never named. Both healthcare examples are customer names only, with no deployment scale, accuracy figure, third-party evaluation or clinical outcome.

The line between them. They put two mutually exclusive questions to a healthcare buyer: Muse Spark Open asks whether you will buy two Mac Studios to fit an entire chart in one pass; Nemotron asks how many PDFs your claims department processes a day. The first is clinical depth, the second administrative throughput. Tellingly, the one with healthcare customers to name on the day was the second — consistent with the NYU study of 127,833 real clinical conversations covered here on 15 October, where 36.2% of real use was documentation and administration and only 3.7% was diagnosis. This race may not be decided by whose benchmark is higher, but by who first works out what hospitals are actually buying.

To close in one sentence: today's competition is not about whose model is strongest, but about who gets to decide where capability sits. Microsoft locked 2.8 trillion parameters inside a Foundry licence, Meta put 816 billion on four clouds for anyone to download, and NVIDIA paired 120 billion with its own inference stack and sold unit economics. The real answer to that question appeared outside the three releases: Anthropic showed that a model whose weights are already out, GLM-5.2, can complete a replication process across eight cheap nodes, and four days later 420 scientists asked for such capability to be airgapped by default. Put another way: the very selling point Meta and NVIDIA offered today — the weights are in your hands — is the thing the letter would like to take back. Healthcare buyers stand in the middle of that contradiction: they most need data never to leave the building, which argues for open weights, and they are least able to absorb a containment failure, which is what the letter is about.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Today's three open-weight releases land inside the window in which Taiwan's AI Basic Act risk-classification framework has just taken effect and every ministry must report by 2028

(1) "Weights on the premises" means more to a Taiwanese hospital than to an American one. Muse Spark Open's floor is two Mac Studios; Nemotron 3.5 Super VL's is lower still — it runs from Jetson up to RTX systems. At Taiwanese scale that is something a regional hospital's IT department can buy and never connect to an outside network. Medical AI in Taiwan has a structural constraint: cross-border transfer of NHI data and medical records, de-identification requirements and internal audit processes make "send the chart to a cloud API" uneconomic on compliance cost alone in many settings. An open-weight model that is natively multimodal with a 1M-token context makes on-premise deployment, data that never leaves, and near-frontier capability possible at the same time for the first time. The precondition is staff who can operate a model in-house — precisely what Taiwan's small and mid-sized hospitals lack — so near-term beneficiaries will likely remain a handful of medical centres.

(2) On the regulatory calendar Taiwan is at the "framework set, details pending" stage — and today's news tests how flexible that stage is. The AI Basic Act passed its third reading on 24 December 2025 and was promulgated on 14 January 2026; the Executive Yuan issued an implementation plan on 21 May, and the Ministry of Digital Affairs set an AI Risk Classification Framework on 7 July — four steps: inventory use cases, identify risk, assess risk, respond to risk. Every competent authority must complete its sector's risk-management rules, industry guidance and legal adaptation before January 2028; for healthcare that authority is the Ministry of Health and Welfare. In the statute, Article 4 lists seven principles including privacy and data governance, security, transparency, fairness and accountability; Article 16 directs the Ministry of Digital Affairs to advance an internationally interoperable risk-classification framework; Article 17 requires clear liability attribution and remedy, compensation or insurance mechanisms for high-risk AI; and Article 19 requires risk assessment when government agencies use AI. Detailed judgements on medical products still run through TFDA, which revised its CADe/CADx device registration technical guidance in August 2025 and updated its AI/ML device registration Q&A. The practical consequence: a hospital's in-house model used only for administration and documentation — the kind of claims and revenue-cycle work R1 does in the US — falls under the Basic Act's framework rather than TFDA's device pathway; the moment it influences diagnostic judgement, it goes through a different door. Nemotron's two healthcare use cases today sit one on each side of that line, and how Taiwan draws it awaits the Ministry's own filing. Separately, the 420-scientist letter maps onto hospital IoT here rather than onto frontier labs: the $55 Android box and unauthenticated ADB port Anthropic measured scale up into the bedside terminals and imaging workstations that run decade-plus lifecycles in Taiwanese hospitals, are hard to update, and cannot casually be taken offline to patch. The same day open weights made on-premise deployment feasible, they also made "there is a model inside our network that can use tools and act over multiple steps" a new line on the security inventory — and those two things belong in one budget, not two fiscal years.

05 — Further Reading

Chosen for being primary publications rather than coverage, and for explaining each other
  1. Self-replicating AI — Anthropic Frontier Red Team (2026-10-12)

    The only primary research today that converts an abstract risk into citable numbers, and it flags its own upper bounds, proxy metrics and untested questions — the annotation habit is worth more than the conclusion. Hospital security leads should read the defence section directly.

  2. Frontier pre-training at Microsoft AI — microsoft.ai (2026-10-15)

    Few vendors publish MFU, goodput, optimizer and MoE design decisions together. Even if MAI-Base-2 itself is of no interest, this is the most concrete available account of what trillion-parameter pre-training actually looks like.

  3. AI model decodes the language cells use to communicate — MIT News (2026-10-15)

    A story that did not make today's top section but repays reading: IRIS, from Pulin Li's group at MIT's Whitehead Institute, infers which developmental signals a cell has received from its gene activity. Published in Nature Methods on 8 September, it predicted signalling timing for heart, gut, muscle and spinal-cord lineages during mouse gastrulation, and its lung prediction was experimentally confirmed. Read it for the predict-then-confirm loop, still rare in medical AI papers — and note the coverage gives no quantitative accuracy metric.

  4. AI study of 101,000 patients links brown fat to heart and mental health — News-Medical (2026-10-15)

    Read it as a methodological example rather than a finding: fifteen years of clinical PET-CT treated as an existing imaging biobank, mined for a phenotype nobody set out to measure.
