Thursday · Technology Breakthroughs

Three companies, three days, three shapes of “AI doing the biology itself” — at GTC Berlin on 10/21 NVIDIA and the UW Institute for Protein Design put RFD4-Proteina on Hugging Face under BSD 3-Clause: 435.6M parameters, 1.74 GB of FP32 weights, 5.0 seconds for a 250-token design on one H200 (2.8s with regional compilation), beating every evaluated baseline across four benchmark suites; on 10/20 Anthropic reported Claude running roughly 64 hours largely unsupervised inside Claude Science, driving 12 design tools to generate 318,484 designs, screening 553,052 design–toxin pairs and picking 180 for the bench — of 211 tested, 17 bound, 11 of 60 (18%) in the single-toxin arm, with Cn2-B1 and Cn2-B2 at single-digit nanomolar — while Anthropic states in writing that only binding, not neutralisation, has been shown; and on 10/21 OpenAI, PerturbAI and Scripps ran thousands of GPT-Rosalind agents over an 8-million-cell mouse whole-brain CRISPR atlas in 32 minutes, citing 700+ references against a single agent’s 11, with blinded experts preferring the distributed output 75% of the time

Thursday, October 22, 2026 · Covering Oct 13–21 · Taipei time

The week’s technology story is not any single model’s score. It is that three things held true inside one 72-hour window: weights actually shipped (RFD4-Proteina under BSD 3-Clause, weights dated 10/19), an agent was actually left running for more than two days (Claude, ~64 hours and ~10,000 GPU hours), and agent scale-out finally got a control arm (OpenAI’s single agent cited 11 references, the distributed run 700+). In the same week Epic said Connectors will run on the Model Context Protocol (Fierce Healthcare, 10/21), so the hospital-side agent interface is converging on the same wire too. The common footnote across all three reports is identical: everything here is computational or in-vitro binding. None of it reaches efficacy.

01 — Top Stories

Seven stories, ordered by distance from the bench: weights and agents first, then the protocols and interfaces inside hospitals, and last the model that already made it into a journal.
Open weights NVIDIAUW IPDRFD4-Proteina10/19–10/21

NVIDIA and David Baker’s institute open a protein-design model under BSD 3-Clause: 435.6M parameters, five seconds per design on one H200

What

At GTC Berlin on 10/21, NVIDIA and the UW Medicine Institute for Protein Design released RFD4-Proteina, an open BioNeMo model that couples programmable design with search (NVIDIA newsroom). The specs: a flow-matching architecture generating sequence and all-atom structure together, 435.6 million parameters, 1.74 GB of FP32 weights, running in BF16; a 250-token design takes 5.0 seconds on a single H200, or 2.8 seconds with regional compilation. The NIM deployment lists seven tasks — generation, motif scaffolding, enzyme design, small-molecule binder design, antibody design, partial diffusion and inverse folding — and supports reward-guided beam search during diffusion. Weights are dated 10/19, diffusers support merged 10/20, and the NIM listing went up on build.nvidia.com and NGC the same day (unite.ai’s technical write-up).

Why it matters

The combination of licence and size is the point. BSD 3-Clause is about as permissive as it gets, and the NIM card marks the model ready for commercial use; 1.74 GB of weights means one data-centre card — or a good consumer one — rather than a cluster. That pushes the marginal cost of designing a binder down towards the electricity bill, and it makes the model small enough to sit in an agent’s toolbox. In the same release, Genedata’s AI co-scientist uses RFD4-Proteina to coordinate design and testing inside Danaher’s autonomous lab, while the model ships simultaneously through Amazon Bio Discovery, Anthropic’s Claude Science, Benchling, Databricks, Google Cloud, Hugging Face, Nebius, OpenAI and Tamarind Bio (full GlobeNewswire text). One model landing inside the science products of three rival AI labs at once is the more quotable fact here.

Discount this

“Beat every evaluated baseline on four suites” is NVIDIA’s own claim, sourced to a research draft that has not been peer-reviewed, and neither the release nor the secondary coverage gives a single number — no success rate, no absolute throughput, no list of baselines. The training-data composition is undisclosed. The four worked examples on the model card (a PD-L1 binder, a HER2 antibody redesign, an enzyme catalytic motif, a small-molecule pocket) are marked on the card itself as not experimental binding or catalytic results.

Autonomous agent AnthropicClaude ScienceAdaptyv Bio10/20

Claude runs 64 autonomous hours designing scorpion-antivenom binders: 318,484 designs in, 17 that actually bind — and Anthropic closes the door on overclaiming first

What

On 10/20 Anthropic’s Science team described using an internal research Claude, inside Claude Science, to design minibinders against scorpion neurotoxins (Anthropic’s research report). The run: Claude worked autonomously for about 24 hours after the initial prompt, then took four follow-ups, for roughly 64 hours and about 10,000 GPU hours in all, choosing the toxin structures and hotspots, setting its own quality thresholds, picking the tools and drafting the report. Targets were five neurotoxins from four regions: Cn2 (Mexico), Ts1 (Brazil), AaH I and AaH II (North Africa), LqhIII (Middle East). The compute: 12 design tools, 318,484 designs, 553,052 design–toxin pairs co-folded and screened, 180 selected by Claude plus 31 added by staff for 211 total, sent to Adaptyv Bio (cell-free expression) and Twist Bioscience (mammalian cells, Fc fusions) and measured by SPR. The bar was set hard: a hit at both labs in every replicate, KD at or below 1 µM, and no signal against the off-target protein CLEC12A. 17 of 211 cleared it — seven against Cn2, four AaH II, four AaH I, two LqhIII — and 11 of 60 (18%) in the single-toxin arm, while no multi-toxin design met the bar for more than one toxin. The best two, Cn2-B1 and Cn2-B2, bound at single-digit nanomolar, and competition experiments put their site on or near that of antibody LR, reported to neutralise Cn2 in mice.

Why it matters

This is the most complete public account yet of autonomous agent-hours tied to wet-lab verification in one report. The 18% single-toxin hit rate is, by Anthropic’s own framing, in line with ordinary protein-design campaigns — meaning the agent performed no magic, but it did run to completion a campaign that normally takes a team weeks to plan, without step-by-step human direction. More usefully, the failures are reported in full: the pan-specific multi-toxin designs failed across the board, and that is precisely the hard, valuable part of antivenom work. All 211 designs, predicted structures, scores and experimental data are public on Hugging Face. For scale: scorpion stings affect at least 1.2 million people a year and cause more than 3,250 deaths, mostly children with limited access to care.

Discount this

Anthropic lists its own limitations, and more honestly than most vendors: only recombinant toxins were tested, neutralisation was not tested in cells or animals, and nothing was tested against venom-purified toxin. The company states plainly that it has not confirmed neutralisation and does not claim to have produced an antivenom. Next steps would be venom binding, patch-clamp electrophysiology and mouse studies. Separately, 31 of the 211 designs were added by Anthropic staff, so “fully autonomous” needs a small discount, and the prompt was adapted with Citrini Research, an investment research firm — disclosed in the report, but worth the reader’s own weighing.

Multi-agent OpenAIPerturbAIScripps ResearchGPT-Rosalind10/21

Thousands of agents, 32 minutes, 8 million cells: OpenAI finally puts a control arm next to “more agents”

What

On 10/21 OpenAI, PerturbAI and Scripps Research described what they call “distributed biological reasoning”: thousands of agents powered by GPT-Rosalind, running in parallel over a mouse whole-brain CRISPR atlas of roughly 8 million cells, each agent taking one gene perturbation — analysing the cellular response, searching the literature, proposing explanations — and the outputs assembled into a “reasoning atlas” linking genes, cellular effects, publications and candidate mechanisms. The whole run took 32 minutes (OpenAI’s announcement). The control arm is the interesting part: with model and resources held fixed, a single agent cited only 11 references at the largest workload, while the distributed agents cited more than 700, and in blinded comparison human experts preferred the distributed reports 75% of the time. Two concrete hypotheses came out: in the cerebellum, disrupting Tsc2 shifted the activity of dozens of neuronal-communication genes while disrupting Tsc1 barely moved them (both regulate mTOR, with possible bearing on the learning and behavioural difficulties of tuberous sclerosis); and disrupting Dctn1 raised expression of cholesterol-synthesis genes, which the agents tied to existing work on cholesterol transport defects and thus to neurodegeneration in Perry syndrome. The atlas is on GitHub, the dataset on Hugging Face, the paper on bioRxiv.

Why it matters

Whether more agents actually helps has been an article of faith, because papers report the final result and skip the single-agent baseline. Eleven references against 700+ is the cleanest control the year has produced: same model, same resources, the only difference being that the work was cut up and run in parallel. It also shows what distribution costs — 700 citations means 700 reasoning chains someone has to spot-check, and the 75% preference rate says that a quarter of the time the experts wanted the single-agent report instead. The methodological consequence for medical AI is direct: if agent count buys literature coverage this cleanly, then “can multi-agent setups close guideline coverage in clinical decision support” stops being a philosophical question and becomes a designable experiment.

Discount this

“Thousands” is never given a number, and the announcement does not describe GPT-Rosalind’s architecture or training. “Months to a year for a human team” is the team’s own estimate, not a measurement. The claim that novelty and value assessments “aligned” with blinded expert judgement is qualitative, with no agreement statistic. The single-agent comparison ran at one fixed setup, so generality is not established. Neither hypothesis has been experimentally validated, and the announcement itself says scientists remain central to discovery. This is biomedical research, not clinical use.

Protocol EpicMCPAgent Factory10/21

Epic builds Connectors on the Model Context Protocol — when the EHR incumbent picks a wire, the ecosystem wires to it

What

Ahead of the Open@Epic conference on 10/21, Epic announced Connectors: a way for third-party developers to link their apps to Epic’s AI agents, so those agents can pull information from an outside app and complete tasks in it (Epic’s own post). They work with the three existing assistants — Emmie for patients, Art for clinicians, Penny for revenue cycle and operations — and with custom agents a health system builds in Agent Factory, which debuted at August’s User Group Meeting carrying over 100 pre-built AI features. The technical choice that matters: Connectors will initially use the Model Context Protocol (MCP). On timing, Epic plans to start testing with selected health system partners before the end of 2026, and developer-built Connectors list on Epic Showroom from March 2027; Epic will charge developers to distribute there, at undisclosed prices (Fierce Healthcare, 10/21). Named launch partners: Availity (coverage, prior authorisation, claims), Gozio Health (wayfinding), VPL (medication delivery timing).

Why it matters

For twenty years the language of health interoperability has been HL7 and FHIR — built to move data. MCP is built to let a model call a tool, which is a different problem: not shipping a lab value across, but letting an agent do something inside another system. Epic picking MCP effectively sets the de facto standard for the agent layer in North American hospitals, and for third-party vendors it decides where the next two years of engineering goes. The governance design is worth noting too: Epic says each health system controls permissions and can review what a Connector may read and change before enabling it, and stresses that Connectors add no new autonomous actions. Keeping the decision about what an agent may do on the hospital side is a direct answer to the “where does responsibility sit” argument in the FDA generative-AI device docket that closed on 10/19.

Discount this

This is an announcement, not a shipment: Showroom opens in March 2027 and testing has yet to begin. Epic published no adoption figures at all — not how many health systems run Art, Emmie or Penny, not how many agents are live in Agent Factory. The only two numbers are the 100+ pre-built features and the “thousands of test cases” used to evaluate Emmie, both self-reported. The Showroom fee is undisclosed, and for small developers that is the variable that decides everything.

Foundation model Mass General BrighamFAHR-FaceLancet Digital Health10/19

FAHR-Face, trained on 40 million faces, lands in Lancet Digital Health: one photo yields both biological age and cancer survival risk — and the most important part is what the paper does not establish

What

On 10/19 Lancet Digital Health published Mass General Brigham’s FAHR-Face (Foundation Artificial Intelligence for Health Recognition), a foundation model trained on more than 40 million face images, with two derived tools: FAHR-FaceAge for biological age and FAHR-FaceSurvival for cancer survival risk, DOI 10.1016/j.landig.2026.101056 (MGB newsroom). Raymond Mak is corresponding author, Hugo JWL Aerts co-senior author, funding from the EU ERC. The findings: the gap between FAHR-FaceAge and chronological age was independently associated with survival; the two tools’ predictions are only weakly correlated, so they capture complementary information, and combining them improved survival-model fit in an external cancer cohort; heavy alcohol, drug and tobacco use were associated with looking biologically older; and age estimates held steady across pose, lighting, expression, makeup and most plastic surgery, with a small shift after rhinoplasty. The team also opened a public upload tool, the FaceAge Research Study at faceage.bwh.harvard.edu, covered by the Boston Globe on 10/20.

Why it matters

This is an extreme case of the “cheapest possible input signal” direction: no blood draw, no imaging equipment, no chart — one face yields two continuous variables associated with survival. In radiation oncology the target is the judgement clinicians have long made on impression alone, whether a patient can withstand aggressive treatment. Technically the most interesting result is that the two tools are only weakly correlated, implying the face encodes at least two non-overlapping classes of health signal. The Washington Post on 10/20 and CNN the same day pushed it into public view, which is exactly where a tool like this warrants the most care.

Discount this

Neither the release nor the secondary coverage gives a single performance figure — no MAE, no C-index, no hazard ratio, no confidence intervals, no cohort sizes for development or external validation, no named comparison models. This is a computational development-and-validation study that did not test whether physicians using these scores made better decisions or got better outcomes; the lifestyle links are associations, not causation; facial attention maps show what the model used, not a biological mechanism; and no clinical decision threshold, treatment-selection rule or safe-use subgroup was established. MGB concedes that more evaluation is needed to know how the models perform across broader demographic and clinical settings — and no subgroup fairness data appears anywhere, which for a face-input model is a serious gap. Disclosure: MGB has filed a US provisional patent (No. 63/769,840) on next-generation facial health algorithms, naming Mak, Haugg and Aerts.

Clinical agent AbridgeAbridge Assistant10/13

Abridge turns the scribe into an Assistant: the reasoning steps are shown as they run, and the citations point at the chart section used

What

On 10/13 Abridge launched Abridge Assistant, a chat-and-voice assistant that presents the same way inside the EHR and on the clinician’s phone, drawing on the patient’s chart (notes, results, outside records), the live conversation, clinical guidelines, and the health system’s own pathways and policy libraries (Abridge’s post). Tasks span summarising what changed for a patient, answering clinical questions, opening calculators prefilled from the chart, personalising notes, and drafting referrals and handoffs. Two design details are worth recording: it breaks complex requests into a chain of steps — gathering context, checking completeness — and shows those steps as they run; and it cites the chart sections and sources it used, so the clinician can trace the reasoning. Evaluation runs human and LLM judges against clinician-built rubrics, before and after each capability ships. Since the September release, every clinician at every Abridge health system has access, whether or not they use Abridge for documentation. The company reports serving more than 300 enterprise health systems.

Why it matters

The ambient scribe lane is close to its technical ceiling — turning a conversation into a note is converging across vendors. Assistant is Abridge using its installed EHR connections as distribution and expanding into “ask anything inside the chart”. The relationship to Epic Connectors is the interesting part: Abridge is building its own assistant layer in the same week Epic standardised the agent layer on MCP, which makes the two simultaneously complementary and head-on — Epic’s Art is the clinician assistant. And the two design choices here, showing the execution steps and citing the chart section used, are the only concretely checkable explainability commitments made on the hospital side this week.

Discount this

Abridge does not disclose the underlying model vendors, architecture, retrieval method, latency or any accuracy metric, nor its data-retention policy. The “300+ enterprise health systems” figure and Sutter Health’s “78% of clinicians reporting improved work satisfaction” are both vendor-reported and not independently audited; the latter is a satisfaction survey, not a clinical quality or safety measure. “Every clinician has access” describes entitlement, not use — and Abridge has published no utilisation figure.

Therapy hardware Siemens HealthineersVarianNemotronHoloscan10/21

Siemens Healthineers puts agents into radiotherapy planning: open-weight models plus CUDA dose calculation plus live sensor streams

What

Also at GTC Berlin on 10/21, Siemens Healthineers and NVIDIA announced joint work on next-generation adaptive radiotherapy, built from three pieces: agentic co-planning powered by NVIDIA’s open Nemotron models to help oncologists evaluate treatment strategies, CUDA-accelerated dose calculation for patient-specific simulation, and the Holoscan platform to connect live sensor data to AI (NVIDIA newsroom). Arthur Kaindl, who heads Varian, the Siemens Healthineers cancer-care business, tied the work to the recently launched Accela system and said treatment “should adapt to the patient, not the other way around”. The scale figure cited in the release is a projection of roughly 35 million cancer diagnoses worldwide each year by 2050.

Why it matters

Six of this week’s stories sit in software; this is the only one pushing agents onto treatment hardware. The bottleneck in adaptive radiotherapy has always been time: the patient is on the table, the anatomy shifts day to day, and recomputing the dose takes long enough that in practice the original plan usually gets delivered anyway. CUDA-accelerated dose calculation plus agent-assisted decisions attacks exactly that online-replanning window. It is also where NVIDIA’s open-weight Nemotron strategy lands concretely: not just selling cards, but making an open model the default substrate for a device maker.

Discount this

The verbs are “developing” and “exploring”, not shipping. The release gives no performance figures at all — no replanning time, no dose accuracy, no clinical outcome, no timeline and no regulatory path. The 35 million figure is a 2050 epidemiological projection unrelated to this system’s performance; it is market-scale framing.

02 — Product Analysis

The same problem — design a protein that sticks to a target — solved by two opposite product shapes: one hands you 1.74 GB of weights, the other hands you an agent that runs itself for 64 hours.

RFD4-Proteina

Open-weight protein design model · NVIDIA × UW Medicine IPD (USA)

Function and position. A flow-matching model generating sequence and all-atom structure together, conditioned on what the user wants preserved: a binding target, an enzyme motif, a ligand, a structural feature. The buyer is unambiguous — anyone making protein design a step in their own pipeline, from pharma to academic labs to agent developers. It is not an end product; it is a part (specs and licence).

  • Strength : the licence-plus-size combination has few rivals — BSD 3-Clause, 1.74 GB of FP32 weights, 5.0 seconds per design on an H200, and simultaneous distribution across nine platforms, including Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s own science products.
  • Strength : seven tasks is broad coverage (antibody design and inverse folding included), and reward-guided beam search during diffusion means an external scoring function can be plugged straight in — this is a model designed to be driven by an agent.
  • Concern : not one published quantitative benchmark score. “Beat the evaluated baselines” never says by how much or against whom, and the source is an unreviewed draft. The training-data composition is undisclosed, which is a real obstacle for anyone needing to reason about out-of-distribution behaviour.
  • Concern : all four worked examples on the model card are computational, and the card says so. Open weights lower the cost of trying, not the cost of wet-lab verification — and verification is the actual bottleneck.

Claude Science

Long-horizon autonomous science agent environment · Anthropic (USA)

Function and position. Not a model but an environment in which a model picks its own tools, sets its own thresholds, runs an entire design campaign and writes the report. In the scorpion work Claude ran autonomously for about 64 hours inside Claude Science, called 12 design tools and consumed roughly 10,000 GPU hours, on one initial prompt plus four follow-ups (Anthropic’s report). What it sells is outsourcing a whole stretch of the research process.

  • Strength : there is wet-lab verification, and the bar is stricter than most papers set — two independent labs, a hit in every replicate, KD at or below 1 µM, plus an off-target control (CLEC12A). The 17-of-211 figure was produced under that bar.
  • Strength : the failures are reported. That no multi-toxin design cleared the bar for more than one toxin is in the body of the report, and all 211 designs with their experimental data are public on Hugging Face for outside recomputation.
  • Concern : that an 18% hit rate is “consistent with typical campaigns” is Anthropic’s own qualitative framing, with no side-by-side control. There is no “same tools, human-operated” baseline, so the agent’s gain cannot be quantified — only that it did not lose ground.
  • Concern : 31 of 211 designs were added by staff, about 15%, which comes off the “autonomous” claim; and roughly 10,000 GPU hours for 17 in-vitro binders, with no neutralisation tested, means neither the unit cost nor the distance to clinic has yet been honestly priced.

In one sentence: this week’s competitive structure is a layered sandwich. At the bottom NVIDIA uses BSD 3-Clause to commoditise the model layer and lock in the hardware. In the middle Anthropic and OpenAI compete on the agent’s depth (64 autonomous hours) and its width (thousands in parallel), while both are simultaneously downstream distribution channels for NVIDIA’s model. At the top Epic holds the hospital entrance with MCP and a paid Showroom, leaving application-layer vendors like Abridge to compete against Epic’s own Art under Epic’s rules. The position nobody occupies is wet-lab verification — the limitations section of all three technical reports points at the same gap, and it is the slowest, most expensive stretch, and the one least convertible into compute.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Open weights, MCP, and running agents over data you already hold — three different heights of barrier for Taiwan

(1) RFD4-Proteina’s 1.74 GB brings the barrier inside what a Taiwanese lab can absorb. BSD 3-Clause plus a single data-centre card means academic groups and biotechs here can fold protein design into their own pipelines for the first time without a licence negotiation or a cluster rental. The bottleneck moves immediately from compute to wet-lab verification capacity — Anthropic sent its 211 designs to Adaptyv Bio and Twist for duplicate SPR, and that specification, two independent labs with a hit in every replicate, has no directly purchasable commercial equivalent in Taiwan today. Whoever closes that gap will matter more than whoever buys more cards.

(2) Epic choosing MCP is a clear timing signal for Taiwan’s hospital IT vendors. The FHIR Box cross-hospital record exchange the Ministry of Health and Welfare has pushed this year solves the data-exchange layer; Epic’s move solves the layer above it: letting an agent act inside another system. The two do not conflict, but the order matters. If the effort here goes into FHIR without a parallel plan for the permission and audit model around agent tool calls, then by March 2027, when Epic Showroom opens and an international Connector ecosystem forms, Taiwanese HIS vendors will find they have a data standard and no agent-interface standard. Epic’s governance design — each hospital reviewing what a Connector may read and may change — is an off-the-shelf specification worth copying.

(3) OpenAI’s run demonstrates “data you already have plus a lot of agents”, and that is the lowest barrier of the three for Taiwan. The 8-million-cell atlas was existing data; the agents simply read in parallel what people could not finish reading. Taiwan’s closest assets of that shape are the National Health Insurance database and the imaging and pathology archives of its medical centres. At the ministry’s 2026 Domestic AI, 3D Medical Imaging and Metaverse Application Conference on 10/5, NTUH president Yu Chung-jen said the hospital aims to complete a hospital-wide automated AI 3D imaging translation framework by 2028, Minister Shih Chung-liang discussed FHIR Box and next-generation 3D PACS, and Zhijie Medical’s Anatomy Cloud platform turns 2D scans into 3D anatomical models in about five minutes, with ten donated workstations to be deployed as demonstration equipment. All of that is infrastructure. Turning it into something thousands of agents can run over requires not a model but programmatically accessible data interfaces and the governance to authorise them — which is to say, point (2).
