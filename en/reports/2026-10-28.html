Health-AI rulemaking split in two this week: ONC struck all 31 source attributes for predictive AI out of EHR certification, while EFF's new records show CMS had to correct four of WISeR's six vendors

Wednesday, October 28, 2026 · Covering Oct 21–28 · Taipei time

This week's regulatory news has one shape: the duty of transparency is moving out of regulation and into contracts. On 26 October HHS finalized HTI-5, removing the 31 source attributes for predictive decision support, the developer maintenance obligation and the drift-monitoring audit from EHR certification in one pass, cutting 14 of 60 criteria and revising 7, for a stated net present value above $1.55 billion. ONC's reasoning is that algorithmic safety belongs to FDA and to civil-rights rules at 45 CFR §92.210 — though STAT reports many of the changes were made over commenters' objections. The next day, 27 October, EFF published a second tranche of litigation-obtained WISeR records, more than 800 pages, showing that in the first three months of that AI prior-authorization demonstration CMS sent requests for information or corrective action plans to at least four of its six vendors, Virtix, Zyter and Innovaccer each named for specific failures. Further out, the EU's Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 has already pushed Annex I high-risk obligations, where medical devices sit, to 2 Aug 2028, while the UK confirmed indefinite CE-mark recognition on 21 October and accepted all 44 of its AI regulation recommendations on 6 October. One question, four answers.

01 — Top Stories

Seven items, one through-line: transparency written into regulation is retreating, and what replaces it has not grown in yet.
Lead ONC / ASTP HTI-5 10/26

HTI-5 is final: all 31 source attributes for predictive AI, and drift-monitoring audits with them, leave EHR certification

What

HHS released the HTI-5 final rule — Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability: ONC Deregulatory Actions to Unleash Prosperity — on Monday 26 October, per the HHS press release. ONC removed 14 of 60 certification criteria, substantially revised 7 and left 39 untouched, Healthcare Innovation reports. The sharpest edge for AI sits in §170.315(b)(11), decision support interventions: since 1 January 2025 certified health IT has had to surface 31 source attributes for predictive tools and 13 for evidence-based ones, healthsystemCIO notes. HTI-5 deletes the predictive disclosure duty outright, and with it the developer maintenance obligation at §170.402(b)(4) and the intervention risk management requirements at §170.315(b)(11)(vi), drift-monitoring audits included; the data classes that trigger a predictive DSI narrow to clinical notes and the assessment and plan of treatment. The stated net present value is more than $1.55 billion, about $108.1 million annualized at a 7% discount rate from 2027, and the AHA summarized it the same day.

Why it matters

This is the first time the United States has demoted "must the model card reach the clinician" from a compliance question to a commercial one. ONC's reasoning is that algorithmic safety oversight sits with FDA and with civil-rights rules at 45 CFR §92.210 rather than with the certification program. The practical consequence is that a CMIO or chief AI officer who wants a predictive model's training data, validation method and performance must now ask the vendor, with nothing behind the request. STAT reports that many of the changes were made over commenters' objections, those objections being precisely that they would weaken the security and privacy of sensitive health information and the safety of AI-reliant products, and ONC says only that it "may consider restoring some source-attribute requirements in future rulemaking." Note what went besides the disclosure: the drift-monitoring audit was the one clause in the whole program obliging a developer to keep checking whether a model had decayed.

Discount this

The $1.55 billion is HHS's own estimate of net regulatory-cost savings, counting developer-side compliance only — it does not quantify direct savings or new costs on the hospital side. The information-blocking proposals in HTI-5 were not finalized and remain under consideration. Effective dates are layered: most provisions take effect 60 days after Federal Register publication, scheduled for 28 October; the rest on 1 January 2027; and the transitions-of-care revision on 1 January 2030.

Investigation CMS / CMMI WISeR 10/27

EFF obtains 800-plus more pages: in WISeR's first three months CMS sent RFIs or corrective action plans to four of six vendors

What

The Electronic Frontier Foundation published a second tranche of litigation-obtained WISeR (Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction) records on 27 October, more than 800 pages; the first tranche, roughly 1,000 pages, came out on 8 September. The new material includes requests for information or corrective action plans CMS sent to four of the model's six vendors, applications from the 66 companies that applied, and the task orders and contracts for evaluation, implementation and monitoring. In detail: Virtix's RFI is dated 17 March 2026 and cites turnaround times beyond guidelines, denials that appear inaccurate and inadequate customer service. Zyter's, dated 27 February, cites late determination letters and possibly inadequate escalation processes; the company runs on Palantir's software infrastructure. Innovaccer received two CAP requests and a performance notice of non-compliance dated 20 February for failing to deliver a fully functional provider portal, and says it warned CMS months earlier that it might not be ready for launch.

Why it matters

WISeR is the first time traditional Medicare has routed prior authorization through an AI-assisted process; it launched on 15 January 2026 in Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington. What matters here is not that one vendor stumbled but that four did at once, which points at the launch timetable rather than at any single firm's competence. CMS itself acknowledged an "extremely condensed timeline": six months from announcement to go-live, over providers' objections. EFF also notes that CMS's FAQ says a non-affirmation requires review by a human clinician, while the details of that review — time spent, authority to override — are not public. The "1.2 seconds per case" figure EFF cites comes from a different system, offered as analogy, not as WISeR data.

Context

Politically, GAO determined on 12 May that WISeR is a "rule" under the Administrative Procedure Act; the Democrats' Congressional Review Act resolution of disapproval was blocked along party lines in the Senate on 16 July, as Fierce Healthcare reported; and the House Appropriations Committee adopted an amendment by voice vote on 9–10 June barring CMS from spending FY 2027 funds on WISeR or similar models, though that bill has passed neither chamber (per Manatt's health AI policy tracker).

EU European Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 7/24

The Digital Omnibus is in force: Annex I high-risk duties, where medical devices sit, move to 2 Aug 2028 and Annex III to 2 Dec 2027

What

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, amends the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689); it was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July. The new schedule: Annex III standalone systems, including health and life insurance pricing, move from 2 Aug 2026 to 2 Dec 2027 (Art. 113(c)(i)); Annex I regulated products, where AI is a safety component and medical devices sit, move from 2 Aug 2027 to 2 Aug 2028 (Art. 113(c)(ii)); systems already in service with public authorities get to 2 Aug 2030 (Art. 111(2)). Notified bodies already designated under Annex I, Section A sectoral law must apply for AI Act designation by 28 Jan 2028 (Art. 43(3)). Penalties are unchanged: up to €35M or 7% of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices, up to €15M or 3% for most other duties.

Why it matters

For device makers the variable is not the delay itself but that it pushes the seam between the AI Act and MDR/IVDR back a year — MDCG 2025-6 already exists to handle how the two regimes stack, and whether notified-body capacity arrives before 2028 remains the open bottleneck. Hunton reads the Annex I deferral as breathing room for the supply chain and notified bodies rather than as lighter obligations. And a delay is not a pause: Article 50 transparency duties have applied since 2 Aug 2026, machine-readable marking for generative systems placed on the market before that date is granted until 2 Dec 2026 (Art. 111(4)), and two new prohibitions take effect the same day.

Discount this

The article numbers and dates here come from secondary legal and industry analysis (ActuIA, 6 Oct 2026, Gibson Dunn, 27 May 2026) rather than a clause-by-clause check against EUR-Lex; ActuIA itself notes the Official Journal text governs.

UK MHRA / DHSC AI Airlock 10/21

The UK confirmed indefinite CE-mark recognition on 21 Oct, accepted all 44 AI regulation recommendations on 6 Oct, and closes AI Airlock Phase 3 on 31 Oct

What

The National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, which the MHRA set up in September 2025, published its report with 44 recommendations on 10 September 2026; the Government responded on 6 October confirming it intends to implement all of them, per Covington's 21 Oct note. On 21 October it separately confirmed indefinite recognition of CE-marked medical devices and IVDs on the Great Britain market. The calendar from here is dense: applications for Phase 3 of the MHRA's AI Airlock regulatory sandbox close on 31 October; draft guidance on managing changes to AI-enabled devices arrives in December 2026; a consultation on qualifying and classifying AI-enabled devices opens in early 2027; and a full implementation roadmap follows in spring 2027.

Why it matters

Britain is doing the opposite of what Washington did this week: writing continuous monitoring into the future regime — change-management guidance, lifecycle monitoring — while the US deletes drift audits from certification. For a manufacturer selling into both, the same model's update process will face two quite different sets of requirements after 2027. The second signal is the indefinite CE-mark recognition: the UK is choosing not to build a parallel review burden, concentrating regulatory capacity on the questions specific to AI — change, classification, sandboxing — and the MHRA took the same plan to AdvaMed in the US that month.

US FDA CDRH GenAI / CADe 10/19

Two FDA tracks at once: comments on the generative-AI device discussion paper closed 19 Oct, while the 510(k) exemption for radiology CAD was formally denied on 17 Sep

What

FDA opened a public docket on generative-AI-enabled medical devices on 18 August 2026, per the FDA announcement, covering risk assessment, premarket evaluation, postmarket monitoring, and foundation models and agentic AI. Comments closed on 19 October, with submissions from bodies including the Bipartisan Policy Center, as MD+DI reported. On the second track, FDA formally denied the petition to exempt radiology computer-aided detection and diagnosis (CADe/CADx) and computer-aided triage and notification (CADt) devices from premarket notification, in a Federal Register final order dated 17 September 2026, the full order. The petition came from Australia's Harrison.ai through the consultancy Rubrum Advising and argued the current pathway is "burdensome and ill-suited" to AI; nearly 40 public comments were filed, with RSNA, the Patient Alliance for Safe Imaging and the Coalition for Health AI — which represents close to 3,000 member organizations — opposing.

Why it matters

Put together, the two actions say FDA's position is that the architecture for generative AI is still an open question while the front door for existing imaging AI stays shut. The CAD denial matters most: had it been granted, the final order would have amended the classification regulation directly, without ordinary notice-and-comment, letting any manufacturer holding one CAD-type clearance ship successors without a 510(k). FDA declined. Read alongside HTI-5's framing that safety belongs to FDA, that leaves an awkward gap — certification loosened, premarket did not, and neither side has a clear answer on who watches the postmarket.

Discount this

FDA has not published an outcome from the generative-AI docket or a timeline for follow-on guidance. The docket number is not reproduced here, and the closing date rests on FDA's announcement and secondary coverage.

States Pennsylvania HB 1925 9/30

States fill the gap: Pennsylvania's House passed HB 1925 by 144–59, requiring AI disclosure and barring prior-authorization denials resting on AI alone

What

Pennsylvania's House passed House Bill 1925 by 144 to 59 on 30 September 2026, per the American College of Radiology. The bill requires healthcare facilities, imaging centers and physician practices included, to disclose AI use in clinical decision-making on their websites and in written patient notices, general treatment consent forms among them, with written notice required at least once per episode of care. Providers must review AI system performance at least annually, and AI may not override a provider's "independent clinical judgment." On the payment side, before issuing or upholding an adverse determination such as a prior-authorization denial, reviewers at insurers — Medicaid and CHIP managed care plans included — must examine the individual clinical record, document the review, and rely on their own judgment rather than an AI recommendation. The bill now sits in the Senate Institutional Sustainability and Innovation Committee.

Why it matters

In the same month the federal government deleted a transparency requirement, states are writing a near-identical one into statute — and writing it onto providers and insurers rather than onto software developers. Manatt's health AI policy tracker lists the nearby dates: Alabama's payer AI law took effect 1 Oct 2026, barring prior-authorization denials resting on AI alone; Minnesota's equivalent took effect 25 Aug 2026; Colorado's chatbot law arrives 1 Jan 2027 and its AI psychotherapy law took effect 12 Aug 2026; Illinois's downcoding law waits until 1 Jan 2028. For a national vendor, the compliance work HTI-5 removes is likely to grow back on the state side, in up to fifty different shapes.

Payment CMS CY2027 PFS 待定案

The other shoe still in the air: CMS's "Software as a Medical Service" payment framework, with the CY2027 final rule due this fall

What

CMS released the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule (CMS-1848-P) on 14 July 2026, using the term "Software as a Medical Service" (SaMS) for the first time and defining it as software-based technology that supports clinical decision-making through algorithmic analysis, distinct from prescription digital therapeutics and from remote physiologic and therapeutic monitoring. As Wilson Sonsini notes, the PFS does not use the outpatient system's ambulatory payment classifications and status indicators; payment runs through relative value units, or through contractor pricing where no national rate exists. The substantive move on the PFS side is to pay certain algorithm-only analyses being removed from the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule — genomic reanalysis, AI-based digital pathology review — through contractor pricing, alongside a request for information on whether a consistent cross-setting SaMS framework should exist at all. Comments closed on 14 September, per McDermott+'s read of the proposed rule.

Why it matters

The gap between clearance and actual use has always been filled by payment. If the term SaMS survives into the final rule, it is the first time CMS has treated algorithmic analysis as a service in its own right rather than an accessory to a device — HIMSS lists it alongside MVPs and interoperability as one of three pillars of the CY2027 digital-health turn. But CMS describes the arrangement as interim, leaving a permanent methodology to later rulemaking, and contractor pricing means the same AI analysis may be paid differently from region to region.

Discount this

This describes a proposed rule, not a final one. CMS normally issues the CY final rule in early November and had not done so as of writing, the morning of 28 October Taipei time; the final text may differ from the proposal.

02 — Product Analysis

Two mechanisms built to fill the gap before the rules are written: one run by the regulator itself, one run privately and already failed once.

MHRA AI Airlock

The UK medical-device regulatory sandbox · MHRA (United Kingdom)

Function and position. AI Airlock is a regulatory sandbox the MHRA runs so that AI devices awkward to assess under current frameworks can be tested under supervision, with the evidence feeding back into how the rules get written. Phase 3 applications close on 31 October 2026, per the timeline Covington compiled. What it sells is not market access but finding the problems before the rules are fixed — the customer is the MHRA itself, and manufacturers willing to make their product a test case.

  • Strength : it is one of the few mechanisms treating change management as the central problem; the MHRA has flagged draft guidance on managing changes to AI-enabled devices for December 2026 and a consultation on qualification and classification for early 2027 (source), testing first and drafting after.
  • Concern : a sandbox produces knowledge, not authorization, so the direct commercial return on participating is thin; and the full implementation roadmap for the 44 recommendations does not arrive until spring 2027 (source), leaving the shape of the eventual rules blank until then. The public record does not give application or acceptance counts per phase, so the real scale is hard to judge.

CHAI Assurance Resource Provider

Private health-AI assurance (since pivoted) · Coalition for Health AI (United States)

Function and position. CHAI set out to build a national network of AI assurance labs — independent labs validating models before procurement. Four founding health systems, Duke, Mayo Clinic, Stanford Health and Johns Hopkins, pledged $1.25 million; roughly 30 labs were planned and at least 32 companies said they wanted tools validated. Fierce Healthcare's post-mortem reports the concept collapsed in early 2025, after which it ran as an "assurance resource provider" program and pivoted to governance; on 11 February 2026 Signal1 was told to stop describing itself as CHAI-certified.

  • Strength : the pivot to governance and post-deployment monitoring lands exactly on the hole HTI-5 left this week — with the drift audit at §170.315(b)(11)(vi) rescinded, continuous monitoring has only private mechanisms to lean on (compare). CHAI's founder has since said the real demand was post-deployment monitoring, not pre-procurement assurance.
  • Concern : this is a model that has already failed once. In October 2025 HHS Deputy Secretary Jim O'Neill and FDA Commissioner Marty Makary criticized CHAI in a Washington Examiner op-ed and said HHS would not fund assurance labs; Microsoft and Amazon then left (source). Without a government backer or a payer, a voluntary assurance scheme has neither verifiable coverage nor verifiable authority, and no figures on ARP validation volume are public.

Today's competitive structure is regulatory power leaking out of certification and into payment and state law. What a manufacturer has to watch is no longer one regulator but four pulling in different directions.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The MOHW's draft rules for medical AI are due, by its own clock, next week.

(1) The MOHW's draft AI implementing rules are due, by its own clock, now. On 6 August 2026, the day the Legislative Yuan's Health and Welfare Committee stood up a Smart Medical Care Committee, MOHW information department head Lee Chien-chang said a draft of implementing rules for AI in healthcare would come within three months, and said plainly that those rules would be binding — unlike the ministry's May guidelines on generative AI use in medical institutions, which are advisory only (CNA, 6 Aug 2026). Counting from 6 August, the self-imposed deadline lands in early November, which is next week. The question HTI-5 just answered in Washington — leave model-card disclosure to the market, or write it into regulation — is one of the questions that draft has to answer.

(2) Taiwan already has the skeleton of risk tiering; what is missing is the healthcare flesh on it. The AI Basic Act passed its third reading on 23 December 2025, establishing seven principles — sustainable development and wellbeing, human autonomy, privacy protection and data governance, information security and safety, transparency and explainability, fairness and non-discrimination, and accountability — with the Ministry of Digital Affairs taking over work on an AI risk classification framework (MODA press release). A framework law is a framework; what decides how medical AI is actually governed is each ministry's subordinate rules. The EU puts devices in Annex I, the UK intends to test in a sandbox before drafting, the US has handed transparency back to the market. Taiwan's rules have to choose among those three routes, and the window for choosing is closing.

(3) On payment, Taiwan is arguably a step ahead. CMS only began using the term "Software as a Medical Service" in its CY2027 proposal (Wilson Sonsini), while Taiwan's smart medical device project office (MOHW) and its AI/ML medical device information and matchmaking platform (TFDA) are already handling review and matchmaking, and the single-payer system has been assessing AI-assisted diagnostic tools for coverage for some time. The cautionary note is what CMS chose: contractor pricing, meaning the same AI analysis may be paid differently by region. A single payer avoids that — but it also means one pricing mistake is a national one.
