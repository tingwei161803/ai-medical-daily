Saturday · General AI

The four things that mattered in AI this week were all about one word: verification. A 17-million-line Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis was checked in a day, Anthropic's Rust rewrite took four months to drag production parity from 6.5% to 99.6%, ARTEX let six state-backed groups hammer unverified systems at 2,900 attempts a minute — and one of the systems that fell was the European Commission's medical reimbursement.

Saturday, November 28, 2026 · Covering Nov 19–28 · Taipei time

Today's stories look scattered: a mathematical proof, a backend rewrite, a Microsoft threat report, a US Commerce rule, a New York toy ban. Line them up and the same seam runs through all of them. The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative were willing, inside a day, to say they had full confidence in a proof nobody has read, because that field owns a verification machine anyone can re-run. Anthropic was willing to let agents rewrite the API carrying all of its traffic, because it first built a verification machine that compared both implementations field by field on live requests. And Microsoft's Nov 25 report and the five Swiss NCSC cases are the same fact inverted: where there is no verification machine, agent speed cashes out entirely as attacker speed. Medical AI sits on that seam. It has no Lean, and it does not yet have its own shadow traffic.

Six stories, ordered by whether a verification layer exists: the first two have one, the next two are paying for not having one, and the last two are governments substituting regulation for verification.
Mathematics / Formal verification AnthropicClaudeLean · Mathlib11/20–11/21

The Lean community verified a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis in a day — by not reading it

What

On Nov 20 Levent Alpoge, a mathematician at Anthropic, released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library of about 17 million lines of code. The next day the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement: "We have full confidence that Alpoge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." It rests on four things: the statement proved is Mathlib's own expert-vetted RiemannHypothesis; the library compiles without errors and dozens of people reproduced the identical raw proof export; a community-written file importing only Mathlib isolates the statement, and both comparator and con-leche --compare confirmed the proved theorem matches Mathlib's; and the export was checked by Lean's kernel, con-leche, con-ron and several independent third-party checkers written in different languages. All of it ran against an inert export, so malicious code in the proof sources could not interfere. Alpoge told Science News that the roughly 250,000 pages of mathematics came out of six weeks going back and forth with Claude; he also claims a proof of the generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

Why it matters

This is the first time a discipline has issued, inside a day, a formal verdict of correctness on an AI-assisted result longer than anyone can read — and the verdict rests on nothing resembling trust in the producer. Mathematics can do this because it has Lean: a machine that reduces "correct" to a check anyone can re-run, with multiple independent implementations cross-checking the checker itself. Ken Ono called it "an earthquake throughout mathematics"; Bryna Kra said the difference here is that "there is someone to stand behind the work." The dissent from the same week is worth recording too: Gary Marcus argues it is unclear the approach generalizes to open-ended problems in biology, business or robotics. For medical AI that sentence is the whole point. The clinic has no Lean.

Discount this

"Full confidence" is a verdict on formal correctness, not on human understanding — the Lean FRO statement itself only expresses the hope that the mathematical community will work to explain the new ideas in the proof. The Association for Human Mathematics said the same day (Nov 20) that no human can claim responsibility for the proof and no mathematician can confirm its correctness with full certainty. Science News hedges throughout with "apparently" and "appears". Also: 17 million lines (of Lean) and 250,000 pages (of mathematics) come from two sources in two units — do not convert between them. The Claude version used was not disclosed.

Engineering method AnthropicMessages APIRust11/25

Anthropic had agents rewrite its own API from Python to Rust: the saving was 70% of CPU, the real output was a method that makes "equivalent" measurable

What

On Nov 25 Anthropic published the full account of moving its Messages API — the main path by which customers and its own Claude Code and Cowork reach the model — from the 2023 Python implementation to Rust. Results: CPU per request down about 70%, memory per request down over 80%, median time to first token down about 20% (Sept 7–21, measured on customers' Claude Code requests). Context: traffic grew roughly 1,800x from early 2024 to autumn 2026. Claude wrote the first version of the core request path in about a week, but the engineering was in the verification: the existing ~700 integration tests were coupled to the Python implementation, and an audit found over 100 weakened assertions in the ported tests. So the team used shadow traffic — production requests through both implementations, with the model called once and its output replayed to Rust so Claude's nondeterminism would not produce false mismatches, and each request counted once for billing and limits. Every stage (validation, settings, prompt building, output handling) was compared checkpoint by checkpoint, prompts piece by piece. The timeline is bracingly honest: in April Rust passed about 98% of tests while only about a quarter of production inputs matched; in early May the match rate fell to about 6.5%; it reached 99.6% only in August; public traffic moved to Rust in September.

Why it matters

The transferable part is not Rust. It is the pairing of "98% of tests passing" with "a quarter of production inputs matching" — Anthropic using its own system to measure the gap between a test suite and the real input distribution. For any team replacing a legacy clinical rules engine, a lab interface or a reporting pipeline, this is a ready-made method: do not let a regression suite certify equivalence; run live traffic through both implementations and compare the shape of every difference. Anthropic even spells out the privacy design — the comparison records only the shape of each difference (stage, field, byte offset, lengths, a fingerprint), never request or reply text, and groups mismatches by fingerprint so one bug is not counted many times. That design transfers almost unchanged to a system holding PHI.

Discount this

Every figure is Anthropic's own, with no third-party audit, and the three performance charts have different scopes: CPU covers one region, memory covers all production, time to first token covers only Claude Code requests over a shorter window. Anthropic lists its own caveats too: the Python API kept changing during the migration, so parity was a moving target; sandbox checks were not always configured exactly like production; six days of misleading match-rate data were excluded from the chart; some merged changes in the Rust codebase were not migration work; and because the verifier rewards matching outputs, an agent can find a cheap workaround a human must reject — the post records an engineer doing exactly that in mid-May over an agent's web-search shortcut.

AI security MicrosoftARTEXSalt Typhoon11/25

Microsoft opens up ARTEX: an open-source penetration framework dumped on GitHub in July is now standard kit for six state-backed groups

What

On Nov 25 the Microsoft security blog published a defender's guide to ARTEX. ARTEX (self-described as an "AI autonomous penetration testing system") is a platform whose Go backend coordinates multiple LLM agents and can run an attack from reconnaissance through exploitation, verification and reporting, aimed mainly at web applications. An attacker can plug in any model, including versions without safeguards, and a pooling feature switches models or API keys when errors or rate limits hit. The source was published on GitHub in July 2026 by user Autumn-27, removed in October, but forks persist; the Chinese firm ThreatBook claimed ownership in November and sells a commercial ARTEX Pro. Microsoft names Storm-0940, Storm-3358, Onyx Sleet, Pearl Sleet, Crystal Typhoon and Salt Typhoon among its users. The figures: one Storm-0940 campaign logged more than 151,000 failed sign-ins, peaking at 2,900 per minute; multiple ARTEX-driven intrusions reached privileged access less than three hours after initial targeting; in one case an agent went from searching GitHub for client secrets to taking data out of Azure Key Vault in five minutes.

Why it matters

The most striking item on Microsoft's list is not a technical control but deception: honeypots, tarpits that drain an agent's time and compute, convincing fake success messages that make the agent stop, and honeytokens that fire an early alert. This is the first time defenders have publicly treated "the adversary is a program steered by a reward signal" as an exploitable weakness. Equally worth copying are the hunting thresholds Microsoft publishes: 10+ distinct commands in five minutes; 100+ distinct paths per minute with 50+ client errors; 3+ secret file types within ten minutes; service principal sign-ins to 3+ applications from one IP per hour. Those can be dropped straight into a hospital SIEM.

Discount this

Microsoft's report does not name healthcare — the only sector hints appear in the titles of other reports it links (financial sector, South Korean finance, IT and software vendors); the body covers government and intelligence agencies, finance, the supply chain (ConnectWise) and cloud environments. Reading it as "hospitals are being hit by ARTEX" is an over-extrapolation. Microsoft also concedes the deception tactics weaken if attackers space out requests or if models recognise a test environment, and the effectiveness figures come from Microsoft's own testing.

Critical infrastructure 瑞士 NCSC · Swiss NCSCEuropean CommissionCERT-EU11/19 · 11/26

Switzerland confirms five agent attacks, four of them successful; and among the European Commission systems ARTEX took down for over ten days was medical reimbursement

What

Florian Schutz, director of Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre, gave the numbers in an interview with public broadcaster SRF, written up by The Hacker News on Nov 26: five confirmed agent attacks in recent days, four successful; three aimed at critical infrastructure operators, two of those successful, with no major disruptions reported, victims including small and medium businesses that came forward voluntarily, and attackers unknown. The Swiss agency lists ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI as internet-reachable examples of "agentic offensive frameworks" but says neither was the tool used in these five cases; Italy's CSIRT bulletin names ARTEX, CyberStrikeAI, PentAGI and DeepSeek Harness. The harder thread runs through Brussels: the European Commission admitted only on Thursday Nov 19 to an "AI-generated cyber campaign" detected on Oct 22, which disrupted personnel systems including payroll, leave requests, medical reimbursements and translation services, with several services taken offline as a protective measure for more than ten days. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier did not name the tool, but POLITICO reported that CERT-EU identified Artex. The Commission says there is no evidence of a broad breach of staff data and it knows of no ransom demand; the investigation is open and it is in contact with CERT-EU and the European Data Protection Supervisor.

Why it matters

This is the closest public case yet to "an agent attack reaching a medical data system", and it was not a hospital — it was an administrative body's staff medical reimbursement system. That detail is more diagnostic than "a hospital was hacked": what got picked was not the clinical system but the systems stuck alongside HR workflows, with non-trivial privileges and the least attention. The weakness lists from Switzerland and Italy point at the same class of asset: credentials committed to repositories, misconfigured infrastructure-as-code, default or missing authentication, API flaws — all old problems, now probed at machine speed and in parallel. The Hacker News also quotes the UK NCSC: "Since September 2026, the NCSC has seen a marked rise in AI-assisted cyber attacks."

Discount this

None of the five Swiss cases was identified as healthcare or a hospital — the official examples of critical infrastructure are electricity, drinking water and telecommunications. In Brussels, the medical reimbursement system was disrupted; existing reporting does not say medical data was exfiltrated. The number of people affected was not published and the attack is unattributed. The Hacker News notes that the count of freely available AI attack tools online has nearly doubled since October — that counts tools, not attacks, and the two should not be conflated. The report also concedes it cannot explain why the Swiss advisory's case count differs from Schutz's.

Compute geopolitics US BISNVIDIADell · Supermicro11/19 · 11/26

US Commerce wrote "remote access" into export control; six days later Nvidia stopped shipping AI servers to four Southeast Asian countries

What

The US BIS interim final rule Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors (Federal Register document 2026-23529) took effect on Nov 19, with comments open to Jan 19, 2027. Three core provisions: exporting, reexporting or transferring advanced AI chips within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now needs a case-by-case licence; an overseas data centre supplying more than 12 million aggregate TPP of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other D:5 countries needs a licence, with linked data centres counted together; and training or providing models for D:5 companies is covered as well. Customers headquartered in the US are exempt from licensing, but exporters must report monthly to BIS, and existing data centres have 90 days to obtain a licence if they want to keep offering these services. The rule also formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, which was never enforced. A clause-by-clause reading is at TheAI Academy. Six days later the Financial Times reported on Nov 26, citing three people familiar, that Nvidia told manufacturing partners to suspend shipments of servers carrying its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia, and that Dell and Supermicro have told customers their orders cannot ship; whether the pause is temporary is unclear. CNA added on Nov 27 that Singapore's Deputy PM Gan Kim Yong could not confirm the report, that Fitch Ratings warns Asia-Pacific data centre investment may slow and become more selective, and that Megaspeed, accused of transshipping Nvidia chips to China, is under investigation.

Why it matters

What this rule changes is not who can buy chips but who can rent compute — for the first time the control reaches past hardware location to the end user of GPU hours. For any research team renting GPUs across borders it is a schedule risk: the rule explicitly covers training or providing models for D:5 companies, and it tests headquarters location rather than customer nationality. One of TheAI Academy's concrete recommendations for Taiwanese teams is precisely to confirm with GPU cloud providers when new Southeast Asian capacity comes online, and to prepare a US or Japan fallback for large year-end training runs.

Discount this

The shipment halt rests on three anonymous sources at the Financial Times; Nvidia, Dell and Supermicro had not responded at publication, and CNA notes the pause may simply be a buffer while data centre operators obtain licences. Two figures quoted by TheAI Academy need care: Megaspeed reportedly arranged nearly $12 billion of Nvidia chip supply to Chinese firms between March 2024 and June 2025, which comes from the Wall Street Journal via a Nov 15 Liberty Times write-up; and Nvidia's Singapore subsidiary's roughly $104 billion 2025 revenue, up 72% year on year, which that article itself cautions includes Taiwan and China revenue booked in Singapore rather than goods landing there.

Regulation / Child safety New York StateS.9408A/A.11144B11/24

New York bans toys with built-in AI chatbots for five years, with penalties up to $15,000 a day

What

On Nov 24 Governor Hochul signed S.9408A/A.11144B (sponsored by Senator Gounardes and Assemblymember Kassay), imposing a five-year statewide moratorium on making, selling and distributing toys with embedded or integrated AI chatbots — including talking plush animals, dolls and internet-connected robots that converse with children — effective 90 days after signing (late February 2027), enforced by the attorney general with civil penalties of up to $15,000 per day. The governor's office cited two cases as the rationale: a Common Sense Media researcher who told a Miko 3 robot they liked jumping from high places received a dangerous suggestion in reply; and NBC News found a stuffed cat marketed for ages three and up that would describe extreme violence in detail. Hochul's line: "Any toy manufacturers that think they can ignore this new measure will think again." The release also notes two bills she signed the previous month, one on AI chatbots that coach self-harm and one on risky baby monitor technology.

Why it matters

This is the first US state measure to pull an entire class of AI consumer product off shelves rather than require labelling or disclosure, and the chosen entry point is children. It is one end of the same problem as GAO-27-109184, covered in the Nov 25 edition: the GAO found that in 2025 nearly one in five Americans aged 12 to 21 had used an AI chatbot for mental health advice, while as of November 2026 the FDA had granted no marketing authorization for any of them. New York's answer is not to wait for the FDA — where safety cannot be verified, ban it for five years. The lesson for medical AI is direct: when a product touches minors and has no verifiable safety boundary, the regulatory instrument jumps from review to suspension.

Discount this

Both cases are the governor's office relaying tests by Common Sense Media and NBC News; the release does not independently verify them, and this report has not obtained the underlying test records. The boundary of the law's scope — whether voice assistant devices or educational robots fall inside it — has to be read off the statute, which the release does not parse. The effective date "90 days after signing" is the release's wording, and outlets differ slightly on the exact date it converts to.

02 — Product Analysis

Two agent frameworks, near-symmetric in architecture and opposite in purpose: one turns a program's security properties into machine-checkable proof, the other turns exploitation into a machine-speed pipeline. Reading them against each other is the fastest way to understand this week.

AlphaProof Code

Agent-generated Lean formal verification framework · Google DeepMind (UK/US)

Function and position. Announced by DeepMind on Nov 10, the framework uses a mix of models to translate a target system's security goals into formal specifications, then has Gemini Carbon-powered agents generate Lean proofs. The first target was Signal's encryption protocol: a four-week proof of concept producing over 6 million lines of Lean, written up by Schneier on Nov 17, with Tom's Hardware noting the verification reaches down to Arm machine code. The process design is the substance: human experts reviewed every formal specification, agents audited the generated proofs and flagged only about 1% of the codebase for a human second look, leaving the rest to Lean's automatic checking. Who buys it? Nobody yet — this is a method demonstration plus a publicly released specification and proof.

  • Strength : it comes with a rare negative control — run against older Signal versions with known, long-fixed vulnerabilities, the tool identified them and showed those versions could not be formally verified. That is the standard objection to formal methods (that it only proves unimportant properties) and here there is an answer; see DeepMind's announcement and the technical paper.
  • Concern : it verifies that a specification is satisfied, not that the specification is right — and the specification is drafted by models and reviewed by humans, a layer with no machine-checkable counterpart. The comments under Schneier's post ask the two sharp questions: who can second-read millions of lines, and whether a for-profit company is the appropriate source of such proofs. It also does not verify the whole Signal app, only the protocol-related parts.

ARTEX

Multi-agent autonomous penetration framework · anonymously published, commercial version sold by ThreatBook (China)

Function and position. A Go backend coordinating multiple LLM agents that drive off-the-shelf tools — Nmap, Sqlmap, Metasploit, headless browsers — from reconnaissance through reporting, aimed mainly at web applications. Two design choices make it unusually hard to defend against: an attacker can plug in any model, including versions without safeguards, and a pooling feature automatically switches model or API key on errors or rate limits; and it typically runs two to three attack chains at once, shifting network paths quickly so that fixed-marker filtering fails. Who buys it? It was published free on GitHub in July, removed in October with forks still circulating, and in November ThreatBook claimed ownership and launched ARTEX Pro — the same thing is simultaneously a public good and a product. Full technical description in Microsoft's Nov 25 report.

  • Strength : from the defender's side its "strength" is measured speed — Microsoft logged five minutes from finding credentials on GitHub to pulling data out of Azure Key Vault, privileged access in under three hours from initial targeting across several intrusions, and over 151,000 sign-in attempts in one campaign peaking at 2,900 per minute. What those numbers mean is that a human on-call rotation is no longer in the same order of magnitude.
  • Concern : it is steered by a reward signal, which is its one structural weakness, and Microsoft's recommendations aim squarely at it — convincing fake success messages that make the agent stop, tarpits that drain its time and compute. But Microsoft concedes those tactics weaken if attackers space out requests or if the model recognises a test environment. And the weaknesses it exploits are almost all old ones: credentials committed to repositories, misconfigured IaC, default or missing authentication, API flaws.

Side by side, the difference is not the model but whether a cheap referee exists. AlphaProof Code can let agents emit millions of lines and still be trusted because Lean's kernel costs far less to check a line of proof than to produce it. ARTEX is hard to defend against because defenders have no referee that can decide "this set of requests is an attack" at comparable cost — only behavioural thresholds approximating one, as in Microsoft's rules. Medical AI currently stands on the ARTEX side of that line: no referee, only approximations (benchmarks, retrospective AUROC, vendor self-reports). Which is why every story in this report carries a "discount this" line.

Today's competitive structure is two axes crossing. The horizontal axis is access to compute, pulled into a political line by the BIS rule and Nvidia's shipments. The frontier labs are betting on both; medical AI vendors compete almost entirely on the horizontal one — whose model is bigger, who has more compute, whose benchmark is higher. Taken together, this week's four main stories say that the vertical axis is the one that will decide who gets into hospitals next.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Three of today's international stories land on a Taiwanese desk for real: one regulatory deadline, one supply-chain assessment, and one ready-made hospital security checklist.

(1) Taiwan is not on the BIS rule's five-country list, but IC design has a Dec 31 deadline. Per TheAI Academy's reading of the rule effective Nov 19, the licensing requirement covers transfers within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil; Taiwan is not among them. But Taiwan-headquartered IC design firms hold "authorized designer" status only until Dec 31, 2026, after which they must apply for a place on an approved roster, with status maintained for 180 days after filing. For Taiwanese firms with plants or data centres in Southeast Asia, the rule's practical effect is to turn "where is this customer's ultimate parent" into an export-compliance question — the recommended actions include inventorying orders shipped to Southeast Asia, confirming whether each consignee's headquarters is in the US (and preparing monthly reporting if relying on the US-HQ exemption), and adding use and sublease restrictions to cloud and hosting contracts. Wiwynn's rack integration plant in Senai, Johor, Malaysia, opened in October 2023, sits inside this new licensing perimeter.

(2) The supply-chain read is "no impact for now", but that phrase has a defined scope. Economic Daily News reported on Nov 27 (reporter Chou Ke-wei) that analysts assess Nvidia's instruction to some partners to pause Southeast Asian shipments as having limited near-term effect on data centres already under construction, with medium-term AI investment slowdown "still uncertain", concluding there is no immediate impact on Taiwan's AI supply chain; they stay cautiously optimistic on Hon Hai and Quanta because ODM orders show no adjustment and AI-related capex has not been cut. The more exposed parties are brand vendors — Dell, Supermicro, HPE — whose diverse customer bases give them less visibility into where compute ends up. The assessment offers no revenue-share or shipment-share figures, which means it is an order-book observation rather than a quantified exposure. The same day CNA quoted Fitch warning that Asia-Pacific data centre investment may slow and turn more selective. The two judgements do not contradict each other; they differ in time horizon.

(3) The European Commission's downed medical reimbursement system is the case Taiwanese hospitals should read against themselves. What got picked was not the HIS, not the PACS, but an administrative system sitting beside HR workflows with non-trivial privileges and the least attention — and every medical centre in Taiwan runs a whole row of those: staff health checks, contracted-provider reimbursement, research grant accounting, IRB submission platforms, small tools each department built itself. The most directly usable part of Microsoft's Nov 25 report is not the product advice but those four hunting thresholds (10+ distinct commands in five minutes; 100+ distinct paths per minute with 50+ client errors; 3+ secret file types within ten minutes; service principal sign-ins to 3+ applications from one IP per hour) and the list of old problems (credentials committed to repositories, misconfigured IaC, default or missing authentication, API flaws). Both the Swiss and Italian advisories note that the weaknesses exploited are almost all old ones, now probed in parallel at thousands of attempts a minute. Read that against the other side of the week: when Anthropic rewrote its own API, even its difference records captured only the shape, never the content. For a hospital integrating anything AI-shaped, that level of logging design is the kind you can actually explain to an IRB and an information security office.

05 — Further Reading

All primary sources, and all documents about method rather than result — what is worth keeping from this week is the material you can actually follow.
  1. Migrating our API to Rust — Anthropic (2026-11-25)

    The one to read end to end. The point is not Rust but the shadow-traffic design: call the model once and replay its output, compare checkpoint by checkpoint, record only the shape of each difference, group by fingerprint. Any team replacing a legacy clinical system can lift it directly — and "98% of tests passing, a quarter of production inputs matching" belongs on a wall.

  2. Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis — Lean FRO & Mathlib Initiative (2026-11-21)

    Short, but it is a template for issuing a correctness verdict on an unreadable artefact inside a day: four criteria set out in order, down to details like running the checks on an inert export so malicious proof sources cannot interfere. As a thinking template for designing clinical AI acceptance criteria it beats any benchmark.

  3. What defenders need to know about ARTEX — Microsoft Security (2026-11-25)

    Skip the product recommendations and go to the hunting thresholds and the deception section. The first is numbers you can put into a SIEM today; the second is the first time defenders have treated "the adversary is a program driven by a reward signal" as the weakness to attack.

  4. Formal Verification of Signal — Google DeepMind (technical paper, 2026-11)

    Read this if you want to see what an agent-generated formal proof actually looks like. The notable part is the negative control: run against older patched versions it identifies the vulnerabilities and shows those versions cannot be verified — a rare instance of formal methods supplying their own false-negative check.

  5. 美國新規 11/19 上路、輝達暫停對星馬泰印出貨 AI 伺服器 — TheAI 學院 (2026-11-27)
