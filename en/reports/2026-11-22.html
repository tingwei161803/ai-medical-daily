Sunday · Weekly Review & Selected Long Reads

The biggest IPO in history priced into the worst week the AI trade has had, and almost every number medical AI sold this week was measured by the seller

Sunday, November 22, 2026 · Covering Nov 16–22 · Taipei time

Two lines reached their end points this week, and they point in opposite directions. The first is price: on November 19, Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares at $83 and raised $81.34bn — the largest IPO on record — opening at $111.50 and closing at $97.17, up 17.1%; BNN Bloomberg ran it that day as a company arriving at a $1.5tn valuation. The next day the price took it back: by the close on November 20, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index was down about 17% on the week and had lost roughly a third of its value since September 30, the Nasdaq 100 fell 5.61% in the session to finish a whisker short of a bear market, and Nvidia had shed about $2.7tn of market value since its October 6 peak.

The second is evidence, and it did not move all week. OpenAI put computer-use agents inside the existing software of CVS Health, Cigna Healthcare, Abridge and Ambience on November 16, and the only quantitative result in the announcement is that in over 1,300 blinded reviews physicians scored the new experience higher across all eight dimensions. Isomorphic Labs' $5bn Series C of November 17 discloses no valuation, no programme count and no clinical timeline. The timeline compressions seven large drugmakers reported on November 20 are company-reported throughout. The only document this week that actually binds a seller was not written in Washington. It was written in Taipei.

Capital markets AnthropicNasdaq: ANT11/19

The largest IPO ever closes up 17.1% — and its first full trading day after listing was the worst day the AI trade has had in years

What

The SEC declared the registration effective and the deal priced on the evening of November 18; Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under ANT on November 19. It priced at $83 a share, above a $70–78 marketing range, selling 980 million Class A shares (956,351,354 from the company, 23,648,646 from selling holders) for $81.34bn gross and $78.84bn net to the company after roughly $550m of underwriting discount, alongside a separate $26.0bn private placement of non-voting Class N stock at the same price to seven technology companies. The stock opened at $111.50, about 34% above the offer, and closed at $97.17, up 17.1%, on more than 846 million shares, with retail taking about 15% of the offering. That is about $1.5tn at the offer price and roughly $1.8tn at the close. Amazon holds about 14.6% and Google about 13.4%; the seven co-founders control 50.1% of the vote through a single Class F share, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust's Class T stock elects a majority of the board.

Why it matters

The week only makes sense taken together. The day after the listing, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index was down about 17% on the week — its worst since 2001 — the Nasdaq 100 fell 5.61% in the session and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28%, erasing its 2026 gains; Nvidia had lost some $2.7tn of market value since its October 6 peak, and the same report notes that its first results since Jensen Huang's departure showed revenue more than doubling while the stock still fell. Financial outlets framed the week as the Nasdaq 100's worst since 2020. In other words: the primary market set an all-time record price for AI in the same week the secondary market began repricing the same story. For medical AI the consequence is direct — the next round's terms, acquisition prices and customers' procurement patience will all regrow from this week's discount rate.

Agents in production OpenAICVS Health · Cigna11/16

OpenAI puts computer-use agents inside four healthcare customers' existing software, with 21 plugins — and its own announcement carries exactly one quantitative result

What

OpenAI announced three updates to ChatGPT for Healthcare on November 16: computer-use agents that read screens, click, type and move between applications; improved trusted clinical search; and "more than 20" healthcare plugins — the page lists 21, among them UpToDate Expert AI, Redox, Availity Extend, the IMO Health MCP Server, Oracle HDI Overview, PenguinAI Medical Coding, Stedi, Trizetto and Turquoise. Four organisations are named as deploying agents: Abridge, Ambience, Cigna Healthcare and CVS Health, for processing referrals, moving documents between systems and bringing care information together. The single quantitative result in the announcement concerns clinical search: in over 1,300 blinded reviews, physicians rated the updated experience more highly across all eight dimensions, including factual correctness, safety, actionability and source authoritativeness.

Why it matters

This is the week's most concrete step, and what it buys is not model capability but position. The deployment detail in secondary coverage shows why: at CVS one agent cleared a months-old document-sending backlog over a single weekend while another handles the software steps for tens of thousands of specialist consultations a day, with validation checks, human oversight and screenshots plus action records confirming that applications reached the correct states; at Cigna, case managers use agents to pull coverage, provider, appointment and prior-interaction details from more than ten separate systems in seconds; Ambience's agent pauses to ask a clinician when the record is ambiguous. This layer needs no FDA clearance and rewrites no chart — it only clicks the mouse for a human, and so it bypasses almost every evidentiary threshold that exists.

Discount this

This is the week's most necessary correction. The safety figures quoted everywhere — 99% of 325 EHR inbox tasks rated safe by physicians for GPT-6 Astra, 93% of 646 assessments rated very or extremely helpful, 94.3% for Astra against 91.8% for Luna on HealthAdminBench, about 8.5 cents per benchmark task — do not appear in OpenAI's own announcement. They reach us through secondary coverage of OpenAI's internal evaluations; we checked the official page line by line, and the only figure on it is those 1,300-plus blinded reviews. None of these evaluations carries third-party audit or published methodology, and while HealthAdminBench was built by Stanford researchers, the coverage does not say who ran the scoring. The deployment outcomes — backlogs cleared, tens of thousands of consultations a day — are customer- and vendor-reported with no independent measurement.

What

Isomorphic Labs announced a $5bn Series C on November 17, co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital, with Andreessen Horowitz among new investors, continued investment from Alphabet, and participation from existing backers Thrive Capital, MGX, GV, CapitalG, Temasek and the UK Sovereign AI Fund. Why it matters

The $5bn buys time, not anything yet proven. Until some of those five become approved medicines, a $5bn price is still being set on preclinical speed figures.

Drug development Novartis · Novo Nordisk · SanofiReuters11/20

Seven big drugmakers report timelines cut by up to half, every figure measured by themselves — while the only peer-reviewed number in the same story says just five have reached Phase 3

What

On November 20, executives at Novartis, Novo Nordisk, Sanofi, Bristol Myers Squibb, AstraZeneca, GSK and Eli Lilly described AI-driven compression of preclinical timelines, summarised as cuts of up to half. Three figures carry a name. Sanofi says it saved at least a full year on the asthma drug lunsekimig by skipping an intermediate dose-selection stage. Novartis cut project initiation to candidate selection in nephrology from three years to 18–24 months. Novo Nordisk reduced its design–make–test–analyze cycles from eight to three and each cycle from eight weeks to four, taking lead optimisation from 64 weeks to 12. BMS chief research officer Robert Plenge said a sickle cell candidate would not have been identified without internal AI, while Aliza Apple, who runs Eli Lilly's TuneLab platform, cautioned that claims of AI swiftly curing disease run ahead of the science.

Why it matters

This is the week's densest concentration of seller-measured numbers, and the easiest to misread. Every named figure sits before candidate identification, while the industry's failures happen mostly after it: the same story cites PhRMA benchmarks of 10–15 years per medicine and a roughly 90% clinical failure rate. Halving the front end, with the failure rate unchanged, does not put more medicines on the market — it makes the same number of failures arrive sooner and more cheaply. The metric worth tracking is not how many months it takes to find a candidate, but the Phase 2 and Phase 3 pass rates over the next two to three years for those 129 assets already in human trials.

Discount this

The up-to-half figure is an aggregate claim: the coverage does not attribute it to a company or explain how it was calculated, and the individual company figures do not all fall inside it — Novo Nordisk's single-stage reduction works out at about 81%, above that ceiling. Every timeline figure is company-reported, with no methodology, no baseline and no peer review. We could not obtain the Reuters original and have worked from a secondary compilation; the only independently published data on that page is the Cancer Treatment Reviews count (Haque et al.) of 129 assets, 74 of them (57.3%) in oncology, 24 (18.9%) through Phase 2 and five at Phase 3. STAT's Pharmalittle column the same day is paywalled and was used only to confirm the story exists.

Regulation FDAEO 1443411/16

From 11/16 the FDA rewrites artificial intelligence as Super Intelligence in new documents, stating plainly that the change is terminological and adds no obligations

What

Under Executive Order 14434, Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence, signed September 29, 2026 and published in the Federal Register on October 2 (91 FR 63129), agencies must, so far as the law allows, replace Artificial Intelligence and AI with Super Intelligence and SI in correspondence, press announcements, public reports and digital guidance, aligning non-statutory documents within 45 days. The FDA began implementation on November 16, 2026 for materials issued on or after that date, starting with CDRH, CDER and CBER, with remaining web and guidance updates expected through early 2027. The order defines the term by reference to 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), so it sets no higher capability threshold.

Why it matters

What matters is precisely that nothing else changed. The FDA states the update is strictly terminological and does not alter underlying regulatory requirements: no new evidentiary burden on device developers, drug sponsors or academic investigators; no product renaming, software revision or relabelling of cleared devices; Good Machine Learning Practice and Predetermined Change Control Plan frameworks remain in force; the 510(k), De Novo and PMA pathways and their standards are unchanged; and statutory text keeps the original artificial intelligence language until Congress acts. So the only US regulatory move this week was a change of word, while the document that could actually move the evidentiary bar — draft guidance on lifecycle management for adaptive clinical algorithms — is not expected until early 2027. That is the same thread running through the four items above: the seller's numbers acquired no new adversary.

Discount this

We could not retrieve the FDA's November 16 notice or the Federal Register text directly; the account above comes from a compilation page that states its own sources as the FDA press notice of 2026-11-16, the Federal Register, and client alerts from Arnold & Porter (2026-10-14) and Wiley Rein (2026-10-16). The statutory amendments proposed by OSTP have not yet been issued.

Security ARTEX · CERT-EUDNSFilter11/18–11/19

A free open-source AI attack framework kept the European Commission's HR and medical-reimbursement systems down for over ten days, while 70% of healthcare organisations have an AI policy and only 40% enforce it

What

CERT-EU detected ARTEX activity in Commission systems earlier in November, having warned on November 5 that the framework chains isolated misconfigurations into serious attack paths. Affected systems include Sysper (payslips, hour logging, leave requests) and MyPMO (medical reimbursements); translation services failed around November 17, staff HR systems were offline for more than ten days, and Sysper went down again on November 18, though most pay still arrived on time. ARTEX is a free open-source AI penetration-testing framework that appeared on GitHub in July 2026: it takes a plain-language goal, hands it to LLM agents that run real offensive tools, and lets an operator steer from a web console. Censys counted 1,465 publicly exposed consoles by November 9, and the FBI issued a FLASH alert on November 13. In the same week, DNSFilter's AI Security in Healthcare report found that 70% of healthcare IT and security managers say their organisation has an AI policy while only 40% say it is consistently enforced — the lowest of any sector surveyed; 47% say employees can authorise a new AI tool to access company systems without higher-level approval; 27% were aware of unauthorised AI connections and 23% suspected them without proof; and 10% say an unapproved AI tool has already caused a security incident.

Why it matters

This is the week's only agent scorecard not measured by a seller, and it points the other way. The items above all buy the ability to have an agent operate across systems in a human's place; ARTEX shows the same capability is just as cheap on the offensive side — free, open-source, and already enough to keep medical reimbursements down for over ten days at an institution with its own dedicated CERT. DNSFilter supplies the other half: healthcare's governance gap is not an inability to write policy but an absence of anyone watching. The report's line — a tool nobody is watching cannot raise an alert — is the security-side statement of this week's evidence problem in medical AI.

Discount this

Two figures from this site's own November 21 edition need correcting here: the FBI FLASH alert is dated November 13, not October 13, and the count of publicly exposed consoles is Censys's 1,465 as of November 9, not over 1,600 — and it comes from Censys, not the FBI. Our sources here also do not say healthcare was named among the FLASH advisory's top target sectors; that claim could not be re-verified and is withdrawn. The DNSFilter survey was fielded in June 2026 across 400 managers in total, with a healthcare subset of only 30 respondents, so the percentages are indicative rather than a population estimate — and the report is published by a security vendor about its own market. The Commission has not confirmed whether any data was taken.

02 — Product Analysis

The week's two most concrete product moves are opposite plays for the same position: one has the agent act in the human's place, the other explicitly refuses write access. The contrast is not in the feature list but in the liability surface.

ChatGPT for Healthcare — Computer-Use Agents

An agent layer that operates existing software in the human's place · OpenAI (US)

Function and position. The agents read screens, click, type and move between applications, asking the customer to replace or rewrite nothing; the announcement also lists 21 healthcare plugins — UpToDate Expert AI, Redox, Availity Extend, Stedi, Trizetto, Turquoise among them — alongside access controls and audit logs, with a BAA available for HIPAA-compliant workflows. The buyer is the large payer or pharmacy retailer that already runs a sprawl of systems it cannot consolidate soon: Cigna's case managers pull data from more than ten separate systems, and CVS had a long-standing document backlog.

  • Strength : deployment cost is close to licensing and permissions alone — one CVS agent cleared a months-old document-sending backlog over a single weekend, the kind of result that integration-dependent products usually measure in quarters.
  • Strength : the failure modes are designed to be observable. CVS uses validation checks, human oversight and screenshots plus action records to confirm applications reached the correct states, and Ambience's agent pauses to ask a clinician when the record is ambiguous — closer to a handoff clinicians can accept than any model confidence score.
  • Concern : the provenance of the safety evidence is opaque. The widely quoted figures — 99% of 325 EHR inbox tasks rated safe, 94.3% on HealthAdminBench — are not in the official announcement, whose only quantitative result is those 1,300-plus blinded physician reviews, and those concern clinical search, not agent actions. So the agent layer itself currently has no public, third-party-reproducible safety evaluation at all.
  • Concern : this layer falls precisely into the regulatory gap. It is not a medical device, makes no diagnosis and rewrites no chart, so it needs no 510(k) — and the FDA's only move this week was rewriting AI as Super Intelligence while stating that no obligations are added. The risk of an agent moving PHI across ten systems on a human's behalf is watched by no existing pathway.

Gemini for Healthcare

A read-only layer for record synthesis and coverage review (preview) · Google Cloud (US)

Function and position. Launched in preview on November 10, it reaches Epic through open.epic APIs and Oracle Health through Ignite FHIR APIs to retrieve conditions, medications and recent labs, and explicitly does not write back to the EHR. Its uses are record synthesis, appointment preparation, insurance coverage reviews and payer benefit determinations, with connections to Optum, Suki, InterSystems and Verato. The seven launch customers span hospital systems (HCA, Northwell, Hartford), payers (BCBS Massachusetts, Highmark, Optum) and an academic centre (Stanford School of Medicine) — a mix that shows it is really selling into two markets.

  • Strength : refusing write access is the week's most underrated product decision. Read-only keeps it clear of liability for chart correctness, avoids a governance negotiation with Epic and Oracle, and shrinks the security review to a small problem — the fastest route to deployment permission in the current evidence vacuum.
  • Strength : the customer list covers providers and payers at once (HCA, Northwell, Hartford, BCBS Massachusetts, Highmark, Optum, Stanford), and coverage review and benefit determination are workflows payers will pay for directly, without waiting for clinical-benefit evidence.
  • Concern : read-only also caps the ceiling. OpenAI's layer is already completing actions this week — sending documents, building authorisation requests — while synthesising a record completes nothing for anyone. What it saves is reading time, and reading time has historically been the hardest saving in medical AI to convert into money.
  • Concern : both price and validation are blank. The coverage states explicitly that no amounts were published, only that customers choose per-user subscriptions or pay-as-you-go; the product is still in preview rather than generally available, and there is no third-party evaluation of any kind. The Fierce Healthcare version we had intended to cite could not be retrieved at check time, so the details here rest on the Becker's and TNW reports instead.

Taken together they say the same thing. When nobody is required to prove clinical benefit, product competition concentrates in the cell marked smallest liability surface times fastest deployment — OpenAI's answer being that the customer need not change a system, Google's that the customer need not hand over write access. Both route around the positions that would demand prospective evidence, and that is the structural fact of the week worth writing down.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The world spent the week selling self-measured numbers; the one place that wrote down physicians must verify, and must keep the power to stop was Taipei

(1) The Taipei Declaration is the week's only document that puts responsibility back on a person. The 38th World Congress of Internal Medicine (WCIM 2026) ran from November 12 to 15 at Hall 2 of the Nangang Exhibition Center in Taipei — the first time in twenty years the congress has come to Taiwan — with National Taiwan University Hospital taking part alongside the Taiwan Medical Association and the Taiwan Society of Internal Medicine. An invitation-only global forum there presented a draft Taipei Declaration on AI, Medicine and Humanities, running to 14 principles, built on human-centred care, patient-physician trust, professional responsibility, health data management and global equity. Two of them aim squarely at the gap in this week's international news: physicians must verify AI output and decide for themselves after weighing the patient's values and circumstances; and physicians must retain the ability to question, correct or stop using a system. The draft also asks institutions, developers and regulators to ensure safety validation, continuing monitoring and a clear division of responsibility. United Daily News reports the draft was commissioned by NTUH superintendent Wu Ming-shiang, and Knews notes the congress returned to Taiwan after a twenty-year absence. Set those 14 principles against the American side of the same week: the FDA stated plainly that its terminology update adds no obligations, while the Taipei draft asks for exactly what the FDA is for now not asking — continuous monitoring, assigned responsibility, and the right to switch the thing off.

(2) The domestic award list already shows the supply side has changed composition. Taiwan's TFDA announced the 2026 National Pharmaceutical Technology R&D Award winners on November 17, updated November 19: 13 awards from 41 applications — one gold, one silver and two bronze for drugs; two gold, two silver and two bronze for medical devices; one silver and two bronze for manufacturing technology, with no gold awarded — presented jointly by the Ministry of Health and Welfare and the Ministry of Economic Affairs at the National Biotechnology Research Park. The notice states that nearly half of the winning teams adopted AI, and names two related policies: accelerated project review for smart medical devices, and a regulatory consultation platform for precision new drugs. That proportion is worth recording: medical AI in Taiwan is no longer a handful of labs but half of a mainstream R&D award. The practical implication for readers here is that getting work into this kind of award, or into accelerated review, calls for regulatory files and validation data rather than benchmark scores — which is precisely what the European route this week was asking for (IntelliProve's €2.7m is explicitly earmarked for MDR Class IIa certification).

Note the sequence: the report states that MediaTek fell 10% in Taipei the same day, but Taipei trading had closed hours before Kuo posted — so that fall had nothing to do with this news and should not be read as the market's verdict on MediaTek winning the work. Neither Google nor Broadcom commented publicly, Kuo himself said the details are not final, and Alphabet was flat. The significance for Taiwan is structural rather than same-day: orders for ASIC design can change hands on the strength of one industry survey, and that business is single-customer risk by construction — Broadcom's 11.4% session loss this week is the price of that risk.
