Paying more, disclosing less — Washington moved the name, the price and the disclosure duty of medical AI in three directions

Wednesday, November 18, 2026 · Covering Oct 28 – Nov 18 · Taipei time

Three moves. The rename: under Executive Order 14434 of September 29, 2026, the FDA from November 16 writes "SI" for "AI" in newly issued documents, the same page stating it does not "create any new requirement" for submitters. The direction: CMS published on November 13 A Clinical Super Intelligence Roadmap, seeking national standards over "a confusing patchwork of state rules" — mandating nothing, with no deadline. The third moves money and duties: the two CY2027 final rules of November 6 and 9 give software a paid status indicator, O1, topping out at $2,250.50 a code; while HTI-5, published October 28 and effective December 28, deletes the training-data and subgroup-performance disclosures for predictive decision support tools. Two hands moving in opposite directions, from one department.

Terminology FDAEO 1443411/16

From Nov 16 the FDA writes "Super Intelligence" for "AI" — and says on the same page that nothing new is required

What

Executive Order 14434, signed September 29, 2026, directs agencies to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" for "artificial intelligence" and "AI," "to the maximum extent permitted by law." The FDA's page states the new terms appear in newly issued public-facing documents from November 16, both in use during the transition; Section 3(a) defines them by reference to the statutory definition of AI at 15 U.S.C. 9401(3), so the definition is unchanged.

Why it matters

This is a retrieval problem, not a compliance one: FDA documents issued after November 16 must be searched under "SI," while statutes, regulations, the Federal Register and existing guidance still say "AI," which the FDA quotes as written. One body of rules now carries two names, and both must be searched.

Discount this

Substantively nothing changed. The FDA writes that the order "does not create any new requirement for sponsors, applicants, manufacturers, or others who submit information to FDA"; existing guidance and regulations "remain in effect as written." Reading it as loosening or tightening would both be wrong.

Policy roadmap CMS11/13

The CMS roadmap seeks national standards over state rules and payment for SI-led care — mandating nothing, with no deadline, on benchmark evidence

What

CMS published on November 13, 2026 A Clinical Super Intelligence Roadmap, on four pillars: Earn Public Trust, Build the Data Foundation, Establish Clear Rules of the Road, and Pay for What Works. On payment it proposes outcome-based chronic disease management and preventive care, SI-led accountable care building on the ACCESS, AHEAD and LEAD models, and delivery of parts of the Medicare annual wellness visit by SI. On regulation it seeks national standards over "a confusing patchwork of state rules," notes some SI-enabled devices may pursue authorization through actions that "may trigger express preemption of state or local requirements," and floats a competency-based premarket approach with post-market surveillance anchored on predetermined change control plans. The release bar is "better than today."

Why it matters

It is the first federal document to make "paying for AI-led care" a stated direction, and it binds payment and preemption into one text. For device makers it decides how many gates they face — one or fifty. Its own risk list is candid: hallucinations, inconsistent performance across patient populations, privacy and security vulnerabilities, and commercial conflicts of interest.

Discount this

It has no legal force: the lawyers call it aspirational, non-binding, with no duty to adopt, and a section titled "What will not be done" states it mandates no adoption, creates no new agency, demands no perfection, and sets no effective date. The evidence deserves the sharper discount — o3 ranking the final diagnosis first in 60% of complex cases against 15.9% for internal medicine physicians, MAI-DxO at 85.5% against "roughly 20%," Cedars-Sinai at 77.1% against 67.1%, all benchmark and vignette work rather than clinical outcomes; and public comfort is sourced to a December 2022 survey, four years old by late 2026. AMA chief executive John Whyte's September rebuttal went to exactly this: many studies are simulations rather than blinded trials, and these tools, he said, "have to be utilized in the context of a care plan that's governed by a physician."

Payment CMS-1850-FCO1 / SaMS11/6–11/9

The CY2027 final rules actually pay: 14 CPT codes leave the lab fee schedule, O1 funds software separately at up to $2,250.50 — and beneficiaries now owe coinsurance

What

The outpatient final rule CMS-1850-FC appeared in the Federal Register on November 6, 2026 and the physician fee schedule final rule CMS-1848-F on November 9. CMS settled that stand-alone algorithms run on existing laboratory data are "other diagnostic tests" rather than clinical laboratory tests, and so leave the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule. By one tally 14 CPT codes were finalized, up from 10 proposed; under the OPPS they move into new technology APCs under a new status indicator O1, "Software as a Medical Service," and under the PFS they get national pricing with procedure status A. Rates were crosswalked from 2026 CLFS levels and rounded into bands: 81417 at $350.50 in APC 1505, 0510U at $450.50 in APC 1506, eleven codes including 1063T and 1064T at $750.50 in APC 1509, and 81427 at $2,250.50 in APC 1522, all before wage-index adjustment. Against the proposal, 0511U and 81416 stay on the CLFS as they include wet-lab work, 81417 and 81427 were added, and both the proposed multiple-procedure discount for O1 and MAC pricing under the PFS were dropped. These services are not subject to the 14-day laboratory date-of-service rule.

Why it matters

This is the only item today with a code, a price and an effective date — the half the roadmap lacks. O1 pays like status S: separately, without multiple-procedure discounting, and codes in new technology APCs are exempt from comprehensive APC packaging. For the first time an algorithm is not folded into a bundled procedure price but bills on its own. The old sale, where the hospital buys the software and absorbs the cost, now has a path to billing Medicare directly. The cost appears too: off the CLFS these services carry beneficiary coinsurance where before they carried none — roughly $450 on the $2,250.50 code.

Discount this

First, CMS itself calls 2027 interim, deferring a full valuation methodology to CY2028; the bands are transitional and derive from a crosswalk of 2026 CLFS rates plus rounding, not from any valuation of the algorithms. Second, the code count is uncertain: the tally cited says "14" while its own list of additions and removals does not reconcile, and its author flags the figures as unclear; this report passes that through with the caveat. Third, all bands precede wage-index adjustment, so actual payment varies by location.

Transparency rollback ASTP / ONCHTI-510/28 → 12/28

HTI-5 deletes the training-data and subgroup-performance disclosures for predictive decision support, effective Dec 28 — certification no longer guarantees access

What

The HTI-5 final rule appeared in the Federal Register on October 28, 2026 (91 FR 67916, document 2026-21943), amending the health IT certification program at 45 CFR Part 170, and takes effect December 28, 2026, with certain provisions effective January 1, 2027. ONC finalized 14 removals (down from 34 proposed) and 7 revisions, leaving 39 of 60 criteria unchanged, and finalized nothing on information blocking in Part 171. The pivot is the revision of § 170.315(b)(11), the decision support intervention criterion HTI-1 created: source attribute support for predictive and evidence-based DSIs, and the requirements for accessing and modifying those attributes, are removed — covering bibliographic citations, training details and performance across patient groups; and developers no longer must apply risk management practices to predictive DSIs. ONC's rationale is "reducing the Department's regulatory footprint," adding that the information still exists and users may request it from developers.

Why it matters

It runs against the roadmap's direction and it has an effective date. HTI-1's source attributes were the only institutional channel in the US guaranteeing a buyer the right to know what data a model was trained on and how it performs across groups, and it was tied to certification, so hospitals did not have to negotiate. After HTI-5 it becomes a contract term: the information exists, but whether and how much is handed over returns to the table. For systems treating US certification as a baseline, "ONC certified" stops meaning "comes with a model card" on December 28. Note that Section 1557 nondiscrimination duties for patient care decision support tools are untouched, so the legal exposure around subgroup performance does not vanish — it merely becomes harder to verify.

Discount this

Do not read this as the end of AI transparency in the US. ONC removed a certification criterion, not disclosure itself: Section 1557 stands, FDA labeling requirements stand, and state law stands, mostly pointing the other way. This report's provision-level detail comes from McDermott's analysis rather than a line-by-line reading of the Federal Register; that analysis also notes the phrase "AI model card" never appears in the regulatory text and is industry shorthand for the source attributes.

Prior auth CMS WISeREFF FOIA11/2

The evidence runs the other way: WISeR's FOIA records show one vendor rejecting 53% of over 6,000 requests in three months, and one request unanswered for 83 days

What

Medicare's WISeR model has run AI-assisted prior authorization since January 1, 2026 in six states — New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona and Washington — through six vendors, scheduled to December 31, 2031, participation voluntary, covering more than a dozen procedures. The Electronic Frontier Foundation obtained and published the records via a FOIA lawsuit filed against CMS in March 2026; as compiled by MedCity News on November 2, 2026: Virtix Health rejected 53% of more than 6,000 requests over three months, attributing it to processing problems from a claims surge; one request sent to Genzeon went unanswered for 83 days; and CMS sent letters over missed turnaround times to Virtix (March 17) and Zyter (February). Vendors can receive a percentage of spending tied to care deemed wasteful and prevented, and CMS requires a licensed clinician to review every denial recommendation.

Why it matters

WISeR is an AI decision system the US government runs itself, governing whether care happens rather than how. Its records are the most direct test of "better than today": the roadmap says a tool may be released once it beats the status quo and is monitored — and WISeR is a case already running, already monitored, whose monitoring results took a lawsuit to obtain. The sharper point is the incentive structure, pay tied to spending prevented: the same thing as the roadmap's own listed risk of commercial conflicts of interest, inside the federal government's own pilot.

Discount this

The 53% does not by itself show the denials were wrong — the MedCity report says as much. What is unpublished is how many were overturned on appeal, the number that would settle accuracy. "CMS sent a letter" is not a fine: the report gives no penalty amount, no count of flags and no percentage for the vendors' share. And while clinician review of every denial holds on paper, the records cannot show whether those clinicians can in practice overturn the AI.

02 — Product Analysis

A newly opened payment rail, and an imaging package cleared by the routine route the same week. The contrast is which was required to produce data.

O1 / Software as a Medical Service (SaMS)

Medicare outpatient payment status indicator · CMS (US) · from CY2027

Function and position. Renamed from the proposal's "Software as a Service," which CMS said could be confused with generic cloud computing. It gives algorithmic services their own identity in outpatient payment: it pays like status S, and codes in new technology APCs are exempt from comprehensive APC packaging. Bands and code assignments are compiled here. The buyers are hospital outpatient departments and physician offices; the payer is Medicare.

  • Strength : for the first time an algorithm is not folded into a bundled price, and CMS dropped both the multiple-procedure discount and MAC pricing under the PFS, sharply raising predictability; leaving the CLFS also lifts the 14-day date-of-service rule.
  • Concern : the prices were converted rather than calculated (a crosswalk of 2026 CLFS rates plus rounding), and CMS calls 2027 interim; earning O1 requires no demonstration of clinical benefit — yet patients now owe coinsurance, roughly $450. "Paid for" and "works" remain two things.

Critical Care Suite 3.0

On-device AI imaging suite for mobile x-ray · GE HealthCare (US) · 510(k) cleared Nov 2026

Function and position. Reported on November 17, 2026 to have taken a 510(k): it detects enteric feeding tubes and marks the tube path, tip and side port while highlighting the airways and diaphragm as anatomical references, in adults and children — the algorithm's first paediatric support. It runs on GE's mobile x-ray units, so the machine is wheeled to the bed after placement and the result appears there.

  • Strength : the vendor says it needs no internet or cloud — a real advantage in critical care, and one that simplifies data governance and cross-border transfer. The positioning is conservatively correct too: it does not adjudicate placement, only marks the reference points, leaving the judgement to a human.
  • Concern : the 99.2% sensitivity and 91.6% mean Dice are vendor-measured, with no specificity published; the paediatric extension is the news yet carries no paediatric breakout, no dataset size and no 510(k) number. Which is exactly what the disclosure HTI-5 deletes from December 28 was built to answer.

The only player here untouched by any of the rules above: not a device, not in the payment codes, not bound by ONC certification. That is both moat and risk — not being a device forecloses O1, but equally requires no source attributes.

Today's structure is a fork, and the blade is whether a thing counts as a device. What is a device travels the 510(k), produces performance figures, and can now reach the O1 rail; what is not owes nothing, but has no route to billing Medicare. The class the government runs itself sits in a third box: neither device nor payment code, holding decision authority by contract, constrained by CMS letters and — as it turns out — FOIA litigation. The three boxes carry utterly different disclosure burdens, and HTI-5, effective December 28, removes the last requirement the first two had in common.

04 — Taiwan Angle

America pays first and gathers evidence after; Taiwan demands evidence first — today's news puts numbers on the difference

(1) America has priced algorithms; Taiwan's NHI has not. The CY2027 rules opened four bands from $350.50 to $2,250.50, and earning the indicator requires no demonstration of clinical benefit. Taiwan is the inverse: AI tools reading x-rays, measuring bone density and screening for eye disease are all licensed in Taiwan, and the NHI pays for none. The ministry's answer was institutional: the AI Impact Research Center, run by its Department of Information Management since 2024, with five demonstration sites at Taipei Veterans General, Taichung Veterans General, Tri-Service General, NCKU and NTU hospitals, its data going to the NHIA's HTA center and then the joint drafting committee; the launch was November 20, 2025, and then department head Li Chien-chang said it would be about three years before anything reached that committee. America bets paying first will make the market produce evidence; Taiwan bets getting evidence right first keeps payment from being wrong — and America's side produced its first bill today: the roughly $450 the patient owes.

(2) From December 28, "ONC certified" no longer means "comes with a model card" — which bears directly on Taiwanese procurement terms. HTI-1's source attributes were a free ride for systems treating US certification as a baseline: pass ONC certification and bibliographic citations, training details and subgroup performance had to be supplied. With HTI-5 removing them, ONC says the information still exists and users may request it — an institutional guarantee becomes bargaining power. The concrete task is to move these out of "assume the vendor has them" and into the contract annex: training data composition, performance by age and sex subgroup, and change management for model updates. Taiwan's position is slightly ahead: the Artificial Intelligence Basic Act passed its third reading on December 23, 2025, with the NSTC as competent authority, so the framework exists; the gap will show in whether healthcare's subordinate rules carry the duty down to procurement and audit. The ministry's three AI centers target responsible AI, licensing and NHI payment — precisely the three questions America, Britain and the EU each picked today; the advantage is a dedicated body on all three, the risk that all three are still building.
