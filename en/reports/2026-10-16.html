Friday, October 16, 2026 · Covering Oct 8–16 · Taipei time

This week put two companies on the same table, and they are arriving at the same position from opposite ends. The study published in JAMA on 10/15 (DOI 10.1001/jama.2026.21868) is the first peer-reviewed account of OpenEvidence's scale: 271,000,000 queries and 952,008 NPI-verified US clinicians between January 2024 and June 2026, monthly active users rising from 355 in January 2024 to 703,265 in June 2026, queries per active user climbing from 18 to 39.5 a month, and month-over-month retention of 70% to 90% (Becker's summary). Abridge took the other road, signing multi-year content deals with NEJM Group and the AMA itself on 4/15, claiming on 8/17 that more than 300 enterprise health systems had adopted its decision support since April with queries per clinician tripling in two months, then locking the workflow with its 10/8 University of Texas System enterprise agreement. Wedged between them sits Suki's $90 million Series E on 10/14, where founder Punit Soni supplied the day's footnote: "Clinical documentation was never the destination. It was the entry point."

01 — Top Stories

Eight items, one through-line: the clinical evidence layer is being assigned an owner — license it yourself, outsource it, or become someone else's infrastructure.
Peer-reviewed · Usage OpenEvidenceJAMAMass General Brigham10/15

271,000,000 queries, 952,008 verified clinicians: OpenEvidence's scale reaches JAMA — on data the company handed over itself

What

On October 15 JAMA published "Clinician Use of an Artificial Intelligence–Enabled Clinical Information Platform" (DOI 10.1001/jama.2026.21868). Working from query logs between January 2024 and June 2026, it counts 271,000,000 queries from 952,008 US clinicians with verified NPIs, 556,200 of them physicians. Monthly active users grew from 355 to 703,265; queries per active user per month rose from 18 to 39.5; month-over-month retention ran about 70% to 90%. Treatment and management accounted for 33.1% of physician queries and diagnosis for 13.9%. Out-of-specialty queries ranged from 35% among oncologists and hematologists to 88% among emergency physicians. Early-career physicians are 24.3% of the physician workforce but produced 61.8% of physician queries, and nurse practitioners and physician assistants averaged 44.5 queries per active user-month against 37.1 for physicians (Becker's Hospital Review, Scimex embargo note).

Why it matters

Until now OpenEvidence's scale existed only in company releases and investor memos — even the systematic review published in npj Digital Medicine on 8/12 could only call the "40% of US physicians daily" claim "reportedly" true. Now there is a citable range. The shape of the behaviour matters more than the total: 88% of emergency physicians' queries fall outside their own specialty, and early-career physicians supply 61.8% of physician queries on 24.3% of the workforce. What the product actually consumes is the real-time uncertainty that sits beyond specialty boundaries — precisely the terrain a UpToDate-style subscription is most expensive to staff and hardest to defend with single-specialty depth. An intensity of 39.5 queries a month means this is no longer a lookup tool but something folded into the rhythm of deciding.

Discount this

OpenEvidence provided the data and contributed to the analysis, and several co-authors were company employees during the study. The authors themselves describe the counts as lower bounds, because the data covers only queries made through the platform's main question interface. This study measures use and nothing else — no diagnostic accuracy, no patient outcomes, no comparison group. High retention and high intensity can be evidence of clinical value or evidence of habit formation, and this design cannot tell the two apart.

Integration · Coopetition NablaOpenEvidenceClinical questions10/15

Nabla, a rival ambient scribe, plugs OpenEvidence into its own panel: 125,000 clinicians, 190 health systems, evidence layer outsourced

What

At 8:00 am ET on October 15, Nabla announced an integration with OpenEvidence. Clinicians can open a Clinical questions panel beside the note and ask about the patient in front of them; Nabla passes OpenEvidence the patient's demographics, visit diagnosis, current medications, provider-entered context and the generated note, and answers come back with citations to peer-reviewed literature. Nabla states the context is anonymized and no protected health information is involved. The numbers: Nabla has over 125,000 clinicians across 190 health systems and provider groups, integrates with all major EHRs, and supports more than 35 languages.

Why it matters

This is the hinge of today's report. Nabla and Abridge face the same question: what goes in the box after the note is written. Abridge licensed UpToDate, NEJM and JAMA and built decision support as its own product; Nabla conceded that the evidence layer already has a winner and wired OpenEvidence in as infrastructure. For OpenEvidence this is the first public instance of its becoming a layer inside someone else's product rather than a site physicians open themselves — and the embedded layer is usually the one that eventually collects. Worth noting: OpenEvidence supplied no quote for the release. Every word in it is Nabla's.

Discount this

This is both companies' own release, with no third-party verification. "Anonymized, no PHI" is Nabla's own characterization, and the fields being passed include demographics, diagnosis, medications and the entire generated note — a combination whose re-identification risk is not zero, with no account of the de-identification method or who audits it. The 125,000 and 190 figures are self-reported. The only clinical appraisal in the release is one neurologist's impression that "what once took hours of research now takes minutes," which is a feeling, not a measurement.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products are solving the same problem — what a clinician needs in the second of deciding — but one is walking from the search box into the workflow and the other from the microphone into the evidence base. The contrast is not in the feature list; it is in who controls the denominator: attention, or conversations.

OpenEvidence

Free, ad-supported clinical question-answering platform · OpenEvidence (US, Miami)

Function and position. Free to US clinicians with a verified NPI, answering clinical questions from peer-reviewed literature with citations. Revenue comes from contextual advertising and sponsored content — Contrary Research's breakdown records the company's position that the information system and the ad display system are "fully unconnected" and that advertisements "shall not be considered an endorsement," alongside CME credit modules since April 2025; the market being addressed is the roughly $20 billion US pharma spent on physician marketing as of 2019. Credibility comes from licensing rather than models: the JAMA Network strategic content agreement, the NEJM Group partnership, and the Behind the Knife surgical library added on 10/14. It is expanding in three directions at once: down into the Epic workflow, sideways into becoming a layer in other companies' products, and up into developing its own cancer drugs.

  • Strength : intensity and retention now have peer-reviewed numbers behind them — the JAMA study of 10/15 gives 703,265 monthly actives, 39.5 queries per active user per month and 70% to 90% month-over-month retention. These are not top-of-funnel marketing figures; they describe a habit.
  • Strength : being free removes the per-physician licensing ceiling and lets it absorb an entire clinical team at once — Northwestern opened single sign-on to nurses and pharmacists, and in the JAMA data NPs and PAs already run 44.5 queries a month against physicians' 37.1. A UpToDate-style subscription has to charge per seat on that same ground.
  • Concern : the evidence stops at use. The npj Digital Medicine systematic review of 8/12 found only eleven peer-reviewed studies in clinical or simulated clinical settings, concluding that performance is strongest in guideline-based situations and more variable in complex ones, and that the platform "often reinforced existing clinical decisions rather than changing them" — a sharp finding for a product that claims to improve decisions. The review also flags small samples, methodological heterogeneity and a constantly changing platform, and calls for prospective real-world studies.
  • Concern : a structural tension between the business model and the product's interest. Pharma pays, clinicians use, patients are affected, and the ad shares a screen with the answer. Contrary lists exactly this risk, plus a regulatory one: if the platform is seen as directing clinical decisions it could fall under FDA device oversight. Neither today's Northwestern deployment nor the Nabla integration says anything about how advertising is handled inside a health system or a third-party product — currently the largest information gap of the two.

Abridge AI（Clinical Decision Support）

Enterprise subscription, decision support entered through ambient capture · Abridge (US, Pittsburgh)

Function and position. It starts from ambient documentation, but the 8/17 expansion turned it into a patient-aware evidence assistant inside the workflow, on desktop and mobile, deployed through the security, governance and EHR integrations a health system already runs for Abridge's documentation — the decisive difference from opening another website. The content is a licensing portfolio it bought itself: Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate, NEJM and the JAMA Network signed on 4/15 (eleven specialty journals plus JAMA Network Open), plus the American Diabetes Association, AAFP, Neurology and the Journal of Clinical Oncology, among others. Health systems pay; the unit is conversations and seats, not attention.

  • Strength : an unusually steep adoption curve. The 8/17 figures: more than 300 enterprise health systems adopting decision support since April, serving over 250 million patients, monthly actives above half of eligible Abridge clinicians, and queries per clinician tripling in two months. Shiv Rao's line is that "adoption of decision support has outpaced anything we've shipped before." UPMC's CMIO reports that more than 60% of its Abridge users found it by self-discovery.
  • Strength : the level at which it is bought, and its channels. Best in KLAS for Ambient AI in both 2025 and 2026, a seat on a $775.7 million VA enterprise contract on 9/22 (now available across VA medical centers), and the UT System enterprise agreement on 10/8 — the layer a built-in Epic feature finds hardest to route around: governance approvals and integration cost already paid for.
  • Concern : squeezed between cost structure and price ceiling. Its evidence layer is purchased, licensing fees are a running cost, and a competitor gives the same layer away free. Epic's AI Charting now ships inside the EHR and Epic holds 42% of the acute hospital EHR market and 55% of beds; once documentation is treated as an EHR built-in, Abridge's subscription has to be carried entirely by the box after the note. Its $5.3 billion valuation (June 2025) prices the latter, not the former.
  • Concern : there is no external usage dataset equivalent to today's JAMA study, and no clinical outcome data. Every adoption figure — 300 systems, 250 million patients, tripled queries, 100 million conversations — is self-reported, and successive releases define "health systems" inconsistently (April's 250 described total customers; August's 300-plus described decision-support adopters). "Monthly actives above half of eligible clinicians" comes without a denominator, and "queries tripled" without an absolute baseline.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Today's fight is over who owns the evidence layer and who pays for it, and Taiwan's answer to both differs completely from the US — which is where both the risk and the opportunity are being underestimated.

(1) An ad-supported free model is close to unreproducible in Taiwan, so "free OpenEvidence" will not automatically become a free resource for Taiwanese clinicians. OpenEvidence's economics rest on US pharma's spending on physician marketing — about $20 billion as of 2019, per Contrary Research. Taiwan's prescription-drug marketing is constrained by the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act and related rules, and the market is not of the same order of magnitude. The same product entering Taiwan therefore has to either charge a subscription (back to UpToDate's price structure) or be paid for by hospitals or the state. The route OpenEvidence took with Anthropic on 9/24, offering free access across roughly 100 low- and middle-income countries, neither covers Taiwan nor makes it a counterparty. The question worth asking is which platform Taiwanese clinicians are using right now, and whether their institutions know.

(2) Taiwan is building data interoperability while today's fight is over evidence embeddability — and if the two do not meet, domestic vendors are left with the last mile only. Health Minister 石崇良's next-generation smart healthcare roadmap, set out on 9/15, centers on the FHIR exchange standard, with a "FHIR Box" at each medical center so hospitals can standardize and exchange data securely without replacing their existing systems. The timeline: key medical data across all medical centers connected by the end of this year, nationwide hospital linkage pushed through by the end of next year, alongside a planned NT$10 billion strategic investment paired with regulatory guidance and an NHI sandbox. This is correct and necessary plumbing, but what it solves is whether data can move. What Abridge and OpenEvidence are competing over is the layer above: patient context plus authoritative evidence, on the same screen in the second of deciding. The FHIR Box gives Taiwanese vendors a path to Nabla's position — feeding context into somebody's evidence layer — without addressing the fact that Taiwan has no UpToDate- or NEJM-scale domestic evidence base to license. Which is exactly what Abridge keeps paying for and what OpenEvidence spent a year's fundraising buying.

(3) Taiwan already has a better mechanism than the US for answering whether AI actually works — but its capacity is out of proportion to today's commercial speed. Among the three AI centers the Ministry of Health and Welfare launched on October 7, 2024, the Clinical AI Validation Center works with the TFDA, assembling a hospital consortium in advance to help vendors validate models and improve accuracy in Taiwanese populations, while the AI Impact Research Center works with the National Health Insurance Administration to build cross-system, cross-tier trial mechanisms that give NHI reimbursement pricing a scientific basis. That design meets today's central problem head-on: the JAMA study measures use, not outcomes, whereas Taiwan's mechanism ties payment to benefit assessment from the start. The problem is scale — 30 hospitals submitted 48 proposals and 16 hospitals with 19 proposals made the final cut. Against the order of magnitude of the NT$48.9 billion, five-year Healthy Taiwan Deep Cultivation Plan, the investment on the validation and impact-research side remains far smaller than the pace of commercial deployment. Suki's single $90 million round today — roughly NT$2.8 billion — exceeds the full-term budget of many domestic programs, and it is the latter that decides whether Taiwan should pay for any of this.
