Wednesday · Regulation & Policy

In one week the US turned clinical AI software into a payment category and started asking how to judge an autonomous surgical robot — CMS creates SaMS and status indicator O1, moves 36 codes to New Technology APCs and pulls 14 algorithmic laboratory codes off the CLFS, while FDA opens consultation on benefit-risk for autonomous and teleoperated robots and ONC deletes the AI model-card disclosure

Wednesday, November 11, 2026 · Covering Nov 4–11 · Taipei time

Today's regulatory story is not about permission; it is about payment. On November 4 CMS released three CY2027 final rules at once — the Physician Fee Schedule, hospital outpatient (OPPS/ASC) and home health — with the OPPS rule appearing in the Federal Register on November 6. The least-covered passage, and the one that matters most to clinical AI, is where CMS renames its long-standing "Software as a Service" to Software as a Medical Service (SaMS), creates status indicator O1, and reassigns 36 HCPCS codes to New Technology APCs — Reed Smith reads it as an interim policy, and CMS's own words in the rule are "an interim first step". In the same week, on November 6, FDA's CDRH issued a benefit-risk discussion paper on robotic devices with autonomous or remote teleoperation capabilities, while on October 26 ONC's HTI-5 final rule had just deleted the AI model-card disclosure requirement. The payment door opens as the disclosure door narrows — that opposing pair is what to watch today.

01 — Top Stories

Five items in two groups: the first three are the US pulling AI software into its payment and classification machinery, the last two are the different road Europe is taking — and the same government loosening oversight instead
Reimbursement CMSSaMS / O111/04

Medicare gives clinical AI software a payment category of its own: SaMS, status indicator O1, and 36 codes moved to New Technology APCs

What

The CY2027 OPPS/ASC final rule and the PFS final rule together rename "Software as a Service" to Software as a Medical Service (SaMS), on the ground that the old label also covers general cloud computing. The definition stands: software-based technologies that support clinical decision making through algorithmic analysis, including those with clinical or diagnostic functionality. A new status indicator O1 pays separately and is not subject to multiple-procedure discounting, with the same payment specifications as status S; CMS expressly rejected the "T"-type alternative that would have applied discounting. Reed Smith, 2026-11-06 flags one newly hard condition: a non-laboratory SaMS analysis must have FDA marketing authorisation to be paid by Medicare. Thirty-six HCPCS codes are designated SaMS; those previously paid under clinical APCs move to New Technology APCs at rates deliberately pinned to CY2026. A proposal-stage breakdown (meddeviceguide, citing 91 FR 41734) puts 21 of the 36 as moves out of clinical APCs (status S); codes already in New Technology APCs keep their APC and simply switch to O1. Effective January 1, 2027.

Why it matters

For years the bottleneck medical-AI companies named was not FDA but the bill. CMS sets out three reasons in the rule why clinical APCs fit software badly: software carries no physical material cost on hospital cost reports; hospitals acquire it through varied commercial models that cost-to-charge ratios rarely capture; and its cost gets diluted across the many claims in a broad clinical APC, depressing median costs and producing underpayment. Routing all SaMS into New Technology APCs is an admission that software's cost structure differs from that of surgical hardware and needs its own channel. It is a structural step — but it only settles how a service may be paid if covered. It does not establish Medicare coverage, and coverage and medical-necessity requirements still apply.

Reimbursement CMSCLFS → OPPS11/04

Fourteen pure-algorithm laboratory codes are pulled off the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule — and patients start owing coinsurance

What

This is the most concrete passage of the day, and the easiest to miss. CMS moves 14 CPT codes off the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule (CLFS) and pays them under OPPS as "other diagnostic tests", all carrying status O1; the proposal had 10 codes, the final rule has 14 (OPPS final rule Table 85). A code-by-code reading by Discoveries in Health Policy (Nov 2026) lists: 81417 (exome re-evaluation) → APC 1505 ($301–400 band, standard payment $350.50); 0510U → APC 1506 ($401–500, $450.50); 0512U, 0513U, 0414U, 0418U, 0220U, 0376U, 1106T, 1107T, 1097T, 1063T, 1064T → APC 1509 ($701–800, $750.50); 81427 (genome re-evaluation) → APC 1522 ($2,001–2,500, $2,250.50). Against the proposal: 0511U and 81416 stay on the CLFS after CMS accepted that their descriptors include wet-lab work; 0208U (deleted in 2022) was dropped; 81417 and 81427 were added; and 1063T/1064T moved up from the proposed $301–400 band to APC 1509 at $750.50. On the PFS side CMS abandoned the proposed contractor pricing, switching to national pricing crosswalked to the OPPS rates (PFS final rule Table A-D8, the same 14 codes, status A).

Why it matters

Three knock-on effects matter more than the dollar figures. (1) Patients start paying. CLFS items carry no Part B deductible or coinsurance; OPPS items do. On the $2,250.50 genome re-evaluation, roughly $450 (about 20%) lands on the patient. (2) The CLIA barrier loosens. CMS's logic is that once the data exists, a pure algorithmic analysis does not necessarily require a CLIA laboratory, so it is a diagnostic test rather than a laboratory test — removing an entry barrier laboratory incumbents long relied on. (3) The 14-day laboratory date-of-service rule (42 CFR 414.510) no longer applies, freeing these services from laboratory billing's timing constraint. One detail the coverage missed: CMS concedes these 14 codes "should have been included" in the proposed Addendum B at status C, and were not.

FDA CDRHSurgical robots11/06

FDA asks the question for the first time: how should a surgical robot's autonomy be judged — discussion paper, Jan 5 deadline, Dec 2-3 workshop

What

On November 6 CDRH published Evaluating Benefit-Risk in Robotic Medical Devices with Autonomous or Remote Teleoperation Capabilities as a discussion paper and request for feedback, docket FDA-2026-N-13135, with comments due January 5, 2027. It covers four topics: terminology, benefit and risk of autonomous capabilities, benefit and risk of remote teleoperation, and risk mitigations. The scope is explicit — "Both current and potential future robotic medical devices are in the scope" — and American Health Intel (2026-11-08) adds that it reaches diagnostic, therapeutic and rehabilitative tasks, surgical and non-surgical alike. FDA is careful about its status: "This paper is intended for discussion purposes only and does not represent draft or final guidance." Holland & Knight's November 10 policy digest also records a public hybrid workshop on December 2-3, 2026 (an earlier FDA calendar listing showed only December 2). We did not download the 8.5 MB full PDF, so the autonomy levels and FDA's specific question list go unreported here; FDA's web version also does not mention AI/machine learning, cybersecurity or latency.

Why it matters

The paper's value lies in the question it asks, not the answer it gives — it gives none. FDA's existing logic for robotic devices assumes a clinician operating the instrument. Once the instrument decides the next cut, or the operator sits hundreds of kilometres away, the basis of the benefit-risk calculation changes: how much latency is unsafe, how autonomy should be graded, at what layer human oversight must sit, what the fallback is on failure. Listing "terminology" first among the four topics is itself an admission that a shared vocabulary does not yet exist. For manufacturers the practical point is that this is the window to shape definitions before rules harden — and it closes on January 5.

EU AI ActEHDS10/11–11/06

Two European clocks: the AI Act's high-risk duties for medical devices slip to 2028, while three EHDS implementing regulations are already in force

What

First, pin down the date most often misstated this year. The Digital Omnibus on AI is now Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, and it amends the AI Act's timing rather than its structure: the European Parliament voted on June 16, 2026, the Council adopted on June 29, publication in the Official Journal followed on July 24 and entry into force on July 27. The line that matters most for health: high-risk obligations for Annex I products — those covered by EU product law, medical devices among them — move from August 2, 2027 to August 2, 2028. Annex III slips from August 2, 2026 to December 2, 2027, and national regulatory sandboxes under Article 57 must be operational by August 2, 2027. Article 50's transparency obligations still apply from August 2, 2026, with a grace period to December 2, 2026 for machine-readable marking, and only for systems placed on the market before August 2, 2026. The other clock runs faster: three EHDS implementing regulations entered into force on October 11-12 — 2026/2083 (the MyHealth@EU cross-border infrastructure, applying March 26, 2027), 2026/2099 (cross-border identification and authentication, applying March 26, 2027, with member states to notify national healthcare attribute sets by March 26, 2028) and 2026/2098 (minimum metadata for secondary use, built on HealthDCAT-AP, applying March 26, 2029). At the notified-body end, at the RAPS AI in Regulatory conference in Barcelona on November 6, TÜV AI.Lab's Kerstin Kaspar said plainly that "Human oversight is super important" and that validation alone does not eliminate risk; Edwards Lifesciences regulatory director Simona Jerabkova said "We are not relying fully on the AI"; and MedQAIR's Leon Doorn noted that IEC 63450, on verification and testing of AI-enabled medical devices, is expected next year.

Why it matters

Europe has handed device makers an extra year, to August 2028 — but the year is not empty. The EHDS plumbing is being laid in the same period, and notified bodies' review habits are forming now. The November 6 conference shows the direction: reviewers increasingly ask not only "is this AI model validated" but "is the AI you use inside your quality system validated, how is it monitored, who owns its mistakes, and what triggers revalidation". Doorn's proposal is to scale testing depth to process risk — unscripted testing may suffice for low-risk uses, while a high-risk use such as generating instructions for use calls for scripted testing, on the basis of ISO 13485:2016. For Taiwanese manufacturers aiming at the EU, the practical reading is that what was postponed is the compliance date, not the preparation period. Discount this: the AI Act dates and article numbers cited here come from a third-party summary page rather than the Official Journal, and that page itself warns they should be checked against the OJ text of Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; the EHDS article never mentions AI, so whether "secondary use" covers AI training cannot be inferred from it.

Counter-current ONC HTI-5CMS WISeR10/15–10/26

The same government's other hand: HTI-5 deletes the AI model-card disclosure, while a CMS deputy administrator says its own AI prior authorisation has "a lot of things … not working"

What

Two items pointing the same way. (1) ONC's HTI-5 final rule (October 26) removes 14 health IT certification criteria, revises 7 and leaves 39 unchanged — including removal of the model-card disclosure requirement attached to the HTI-1 Decision Support Intervention (DSI) criterion. The information-blocking proposals were not finalised; HHS is still weighing comments and they may return in the anticipated HTI-6 rulemaking, expected to address API capabilities and AI-enabled interoperability. (2) WISeR — CMS's AI-assisted prior authorisation pilot in Original Medicare across six states (Arizona, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas and Washington, about 6.4 million Medicare beneficiaries) — drew this from CMS Deputy Administrator Kimberly Brandt at a conference in Santa Fe, New Mexico, on October 15: "There are a lot of things about WISeR that are not working." The pilot began in January 2026 (in practice January 15, delayed by technical problems) and is scheduled to run to 2031, covering electrical nerve stimulators, epidural steroid injections for pain, cervical spinal fusion, certain knee procedures and, in some states, skin substitutes for chronic leg wounds; emergency services are excluded. GAO determined on May 12, 2026 that WISeR meets the Administrative Procedure Act definition of a rule, which can trigger the Congressional Review Act's expedited disapproval procedure.

Why it matters

This item is where today converges. In a single week the US did three things pointing in different directions: opened a payment channel for AI software (SaMS), started asking how autonomous robots should be judged (the FDA discussion paper), and withdrew the disclosure requirement that let hospitals understand the AI they hold (HTI-5). The consequence of the third lands on hospitals: once the model card is no longer a certification requirement, the burden of local validation shifts from the developer to whoever buys and runs the system. WISeR shows the downstream of the same logic — where AI sits inside a payment decision, the government made itself the first buyer to hit the mine. One slice of Texas data showed an initial approval rate of about 62%, rising to 84% after physician review — meaning more than half of that roughly 38% of initial denials were reversed by a human. Discount this: Brandt did not say which parts are failing and the quote reaches us via Nextgov/FCW; CMS has published no full public evaluation; and the Texas figures cover one state over a limited period, with the 38% being our own inference from the 62% rather than a denial rate any source states.

02 — Product Analysis

No product launched today, but a batch of existing products got price tags for the first time. Two with the sharpest contrast: one CMS moved into the new channel with a number attached, one CMS expressly refused to move — and the difference is not technical, it is which payment history each one grew out of

Optellum Lung Cancer Prediction (CPT 0721T / 0722T)

AI quantification of malignancy risk in lung nodules · Optellum (Oxford, UK) · already in a New Technology APC, now switched to O1

Function and position. It takes CT imaging and outputs a malignancy probability score for a lung nodule, moving the resect-or-watch decision from a radiologist's impression toward quantified risk stratification. Buyers are hospitals along the lung-cancer screening and thoracic surgery pathway. Within SaMS it belongs to the group that was already in a New Technology APC and therefore only changes status indicator; the proposal-stage table places 0721T/0722T in APC 1508 at a standard payment of $650.50.

  • Strength : it is the cleanest beneficiary shape under the new regime — no relocation, no re-anchoring of rates, just a swap from the old status indicator to O1, and O1 is expressly exempt from multiple-procedure discounting. In lung screening, where several imaging services are often billed on one encounter, not being discounted is itself a material difference.
  • Concern : the price is a band, not a price. APC 1508's $650.50 is the midpoint of the $601–700 band and bears no relation to the product's cost or value; the analysis notes CMS never explains how a new code's initial band will be chosen, leaving later entrants in a system with no pricing anchor.
  • Concern : O1 only settles how a service is paid if covered. Reed Smith makes the point explicitly: assignment to a New Technology APC with O1 does not establish Medicare coverage, and coverage and medical necessity still have to be met. We also have not verified whether 0721T/0722T still sits at $650.50 in the final Addendum B.

AI coronary plaque quantification (CPT 75577) vs FFR-CT (CPT 75580)

Both are AI post-processing of cardiac CT · one goes into a New Technology APC, the other CMS keeps in a clinical APC

Function and position. Both take the same cardiac CT as input: plaque quantification outputs the burden and composition of atherosclerosis, FFR-CT outputs a haemodynamic estimate of functional stenosis. Technically both are imaging-AI post-processing; clinically they answer different questions — how much bad material is there, versus is it obstructing flow. The proposal-stage table places 75577 (AI-QCT plaque analysis) in APC 1511 at a standard payment of $950.50; in the final rule, CMS rejected comments asking it to treat 75580 (FFR-CT) and 92229 (autonomous retinal imaging) like the other codes — 75580 stays in a clinical APC, while 92229 ends up in APC 1502 at status O1.

  • Strength : this pair is the most explanatory material of the day, because it proves SaMS's boundary is not a technical definition. Two products, both AI post-processing of cardiac CT, treated differently; the author's conclusion is that the category reflects "historical payment silos more than a coherent technology definition". Knowing that, a manufacturer planning a new CPT application sees the real variable: which fee schedule the code historically travelled on, not how good the model is.
  • Concern : we cannot verify which companies' products correspond to these codes. The sources describe product categories only ("AI-QCT plaque analysis", "coronary CT fractional flow reserve analysis"), and one analysis states for itself that it "names no companies". This daily covered Cleerly's AI-QCT randomised trial on November 9 and Heartflow's registry study on November 10, but mapping a product to a CPT code needs separate verification and we do not make that link here.
  • Concern : the $950.50 for 75577 is a proposal-stage figure. The final rule's claim is that rates are pinned to CY2026, but we did not reconcile the final Addendum B code by code, so treat the number as indicative rather than settled.

Putting the two panels together. SaMS is not a technology category but a reclassification: it relocates a batch of products from an old fee schedule to a new channel while deliberately holding the amounts still. What actually changes is three things — no multiple-procedure discounting, no dilution inside a low-volume clinical APC, and, on the laboratory side, the loss of CLIA's shelter in exchange for patient coinsurance. For manufacturers the sum to do today is not how much the rate rose, but which historical track their code sits on.

This is the most honest price signal in the table: 0972T asked for $2,750.50 and got $250.50, a gap of roughly eleven times; 1041T asked $1,150.50 and got $150.50. Closing the section in one sentence: today's competitive structure is not about whose model is better, but about whose code sits on the right historical track. SaMS's boundary is set by three things — whether you hold FDA device status (which put CureMatch's 0794T out), which fee schedule the code historically travelled (which kept FFR-CT in a clinical APC), and whether the descriptor contains a physical step (which kept 0511U and 81416 on the CLFS). None of the three is technical merit. And the elevenfold gap between the $2,750.50 asked for 0972T and the $250.50 granted gives the real price level of this new category today.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan stands in the gap between a guideline and a binding rule — and today the US showed what the far side looks like

(1) On the original timetable, Taiwan's draft AI medical rules are due about now. On May 29, 2026 the Ministry of Health and Welfare issued the Guidelines for the Application of Generative Artificial Intelligence by Medical Institutions (letter no. 1151663164) — administrative guidance, not legally binding: patients must be told the purpose and limits when generative AI interacts with them or records audio or video; vendor contracts must disclose the large language model used; use must respect the Personal Data Protection Act's purpose limitation and the Medical Care Act's rules on records; a function touching diagnosis, treatment or prevention needs a classification determination from the FDA; and, crucially, "wherever clinical judgement, patient safety or the medical record is involved, a suitably qualified health professional must bear final confirmation and responsibility". At the August 6 founding of the Legislative Yuan Hou Sheng Association's smart healthcare committee, MOHW information division director Li Chien-chang said binding rules would be drafted on the seven principles of the Artificial Intelligence Basic Act — transparency, privacy, accountability, safety, equity, sustainability and human autonomy — defining which conduct constitutes harm, with a draft within three months. Three months lands in November — now. We have not verified whether the draft appeared in November; the three-month timetable was an August 6 forecast, not a completed fact.

(2) The most usable lesson today is where a payment code, once created, pushes responsibility. Taiwan's gap is plain: the FDA has authorised a fair number of AI devices, yet National Health Insurance has no dedicated payment category for AI software — almost exactly where CMS stood before CY2027. And CMS's fix created three problems Taiwan will meet sooner or later. First, software was pulled out of clinical APCs because it carries no physical material cost on cost reports and its commercial models resist capture by cost-to-charge ratios — Taiwan's NHI point-value system has the identical difficulty, with nowhere to put the cost of AI software. Second, CMS made FDA device status the ticket to payment (non-laboratory SaMS needs marketing authorisation, non-device CDS is out), turning a TFDA classification determination into a precondition for payment, where Taiwan today merely suggests vendors seek one. Third, moving laboratory algorithms off the CLFS starts charging patients coinsurance; if Taiwan creates codes for AI, whether they attract co-payment or a self-pay differential is the same question that has to be answered first. We have no figures for NHI payment of AI devices by count or value, so this is a qualitative description.

(3) On data infrastructure, Taiwan runs ahead of the EU. The EU's three EHDS implementing regulations entered into force on October 11-12, but MyHealth@EU and cross-border authentication do not apply until March 26, 2027, and the secondary-use metadata framework not until March 26, 2029. In Taiwan, MOHW expects all 27 medical centres connected to its next-generation EMR platform by the end of 2026 (Chang Gung, Mackay and Chung Shan have completed pilots), with some 200 to 300 regional and district hospitals following through 2027; a dual consent mechanism is due by March 2027 — the first consent for cross-hospital access, the second for de-identified data in public-interest AI research. Medical centres serve some 80 to 90 per cent of patients and seriously ill patients concentrate there, so record sharing for major illness is in place by year end. Li Chien-chang's account of the path is worth noting: replacing every hospital's EMR would cost too much and a national standard would take about a decade to land, so the government built its own exchange platform (FHIR Box) for hospitals to connect to; he contrasts the roughly 80 per cent of US hospitals on Epic with Taiwan's dozens of different systems to explain why interoperability is the local bottleneck. This progress comes from an October 19 United Daily News report and states expectations.

(4) The real choice: whether the rules copy the American template or the British one. The American path fits AI software into existing payment and classification machinery while loosening disclosure (HTI-5): entry and billing thresholds fall, and local validation shifts to hospitals. The British path rewrites the regulatory timeline, accepting that risk mostly materialises after launch — on October 6 the UK government accepted in full all 44 recommendations of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, re-themed MHRA's AI Airlock Phase 3 around post-market surveillance with the first cohort selected in November, draft change-management guidance in December 2026, a consultation on qualification and classification in 2027 and a full roadmap by spring 2027, so long-run maintenance obligations rise (note that no new rules are yet in force — this is a policy commitment and a timetable). Taiwan's conditions favour the British shape — high concentration in medical centres, a single payer, a nationwide record-sharing channel by year end. Those are assets that pay off under continuous monitoring, not one-off review. Treating FHIR Box as infrastructure for post-market performance monitoring, not merely a pipe for retrieving records, is worth putting into the draft discussion now. This point is this report's own analysis and represents no official position.

    Penn's Jessica Fishman and John L. Their proposed standard is behavioral benchmarking. It is an essay, not a policy event — but it is exactly the ground left unowned once HTI-5 deletes the disclosure requirement.
