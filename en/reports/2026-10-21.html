Wednesday · Regulation & Policy

The FDA's generative-AI device docket closed at midnight on 19 October, and two letters filed the same day framed the fight: hospitals say developers must own it, industry says do not turn a risk framework into a classification tool

Wednesday, October 21, 2026 · Covering Oct 6–20 · Taipei time

Four jurisdictions answered the same question four different ways this fortnight. In the United States, the FDA's generative-AI device discussion paper (published 18 August 2026, Docket FDA-2026-N-7874) closed to comment on 19 October, with the American Hospital Association and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce filing the same day and pulling in opposite directions; meanwhile CDRH's FY2027 guidance agenda is down to 17 documents, five of them software and AI. Britain accepted all 44 Commission recommendations and carried the agenda to AdvaMed on 20 October. The EU used Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 to defer high-risk duties for AI embedded in medical devices to 2 August 2028. And the American states went around the product entirely to regulate its use — California signed four laws and vetoed two.

01 — Top Stories

Not one of today's items is a model clearing approval. All eight circle the same question: when the model keeps changing, does a one-off premarket review still count as regulation.
Lead AHAFDA CDRH10/19

On the day the docket closed, the AHA posted the post-market monitoring bill back to the developers

What

On 19 October 2026 the American Hospital Association filed comments on Docket FDA-2026-N-7874, addressed to CDRH Director Michelle Tarver and signed by Ashley Thompson, Senior Vice President for Public Policy Analysis and Development. The AHA says it speaks for nearly 5,000 member hospitals and health systems, more than 270,000 affiliated physicians, 2 million nurses and caregivers and 43,000 healthcare leaders. Its core claim is one sentence: primary responsibility for premarket and post-deployment monitoring belongs to developers, because hospitals lack the information needed to assess independently how these tools perform, and black-box models hide the flaws that degrade output accuracy. The figures in the letter: over 96% of AI-enabled devices clear through 510(k); the AHA's own Health IT supplemental survey finds 74% of hospitals need multiple teams — senior leaders, department heads, IT — to evaluate predictive AI; and a cited Nature study puts mis-triage for certain patients needing emergency care at 52% and over-triage of non-emergent cases at 64% (AHA, 19 Oct 2026; Becker's Hospital Review).

The asks

Five concrete recommendations: (1) rework the FDA's risk heuristic to add reversibility, downstream safeguards, output traceability and likelihood of error, and update or drop the "activity level" variable, since informational tools can also carry serious risk; (2) write labelling guidance jointly with the Federal Trade Commission, separating informational from action-taking devices and medical from non-medical ones, and stating how much clinician oversight a tool carries; (3) fold premarket and post-deployment activity into a total product lifecycle approach; (4) state clearly that generative-AI clinical decision support and administrative tools fall outside FDA oversight; (5) put primary monitoring duty on developers. The AHA also backs keeping the AI transparency criteria in ONC's certified health IT programme, opposing their removal in the proposed HTI-5 rule, and asks for cross-agency help for rural, critical access and safety-net hospitals (AHA).

Why it matters

The argument is not whether to regulate but on whose ledger the cost of regulating lands. One of the four paths floated in the FDA's discussion paper is lighter premarket review paired with heavier post-market monitoring — and post-market monitoring happens, in practice, on hospital servers, watched by hospital IT staff. The AHA is pricing that path in advance: if the FDA picks it, the monitoring duty has to be written onto developers in regulatory text, or 5,000 hospitals inherit an unbudgeted job by default. It is also why the AHA simultaneously opposes HTI-5's deletion of model cards — that is currently the only institutionalised window hospitals have into a model.

Discount this

The AHA is the hospitals' lobby, and "developers own it" is its interest position stated as principle. The 74% comes from the AHA's own supplemental survey, with no sample size or sampling method in the release; the 52% and 64% triage figures reach us through the AHA's summary of a Nature study, and this report has not obtained the paper, so neither the design nor the scope has been checked.

Counterpoint U.S. ChamberPCCP10/19

The same day, the U.S. Chamber asked that the two-axis risk framework shape evidence expectations only — never classification or pathway

What

On 19 October 2026 the U.S. Chamber of Commerce filed on the same docket, opening with the line that a framework "poorly calibrated, in either direction, risks delaying patient access to beneficial tools." Its asks: use the two-axis risk framework for risk management and evidence expectations only, not to set device classification, predicates or premarket pathway; publish objective criteria for placing a device on each axis, with worked examples; do not treat patient-facing tools as higher risk by default, since risk should track the actual path from output to clinical action, including human review and urgency; set evaluation on task-specific performance under a defined intended use rather than general "competency"; and build benchmarks with AAMI, IEEE, HL7 and ISO "rather than developing evaluation criteria unilaterally" (U.S. Chamber of Commerce, 19 Oct 2026).

The sharpest ask

Change control. The Chamber wants the FDA to say how prompt changes fit inside a Predetermined Change Control Plan and to publish what counts as a significant change requiring a new submission — the letter states plainly that "prompt optimization" is not categorically minor. It wants risk-based guidance on when an upstream foundation-model update forces re-evaluation or resubmission, and a clear statement of the limits of FDA authority over foundation-model developers who build no finished device. Any foundation-model information programme should be voluntary and narrowly scoped, with an FDA commitment that non-submission will not be scored as a deficiency, plus confidentiality protection including against FOIA disclosure. On agentic AI the Chamber calls a formal framework premature: hold a public workshop first, coordinate with ONC and CMS, and back an HHS-run voluntary National Clinical AI Sandbox with state opt-in (U.S. Chamber).

Why it matters

Lay the two letters side by side and both press on the same fulcrum: post-market monitoring. The AHA wants it made a statutory duty of developers; the Chamber wants it treated as the consideration paid for a lighter premarket burden, with the contents of a qualifying monitoring plan published in advance. The difference is where responsibility and cost land. What actually decides the outcome is a third thing — whether the FDA is willing to write "monitoring plan" as an enforceable regulatory requirement rather than a credit earned at review.

Discount this

The Chamber's page runs to roughly 143,000 characters and this report read about the first 100,000, leaving some 43,000 unread; the account above covers only what was read. The Chamber is also the industry's lobby, and its ask that patient-facing tools not be presumed higher risk bears directly on how hard consumer-facing generative products get regulated.

Guidance agenda CDRHFY202710/01

CDRH's FY2027 guidance list shrinks to 17 documents, five of them software and AI — a shorter list leaning harder on AI

What

CDRH published its proposed guidance lists on 1 October 2026, the first day of FY2027; Steven J. Gonzalez and Adrienne R. Lenz of Hyman, Phelps & McNamara took them apart on the FDA Law Blog on 18 October 2026. Across the three lists — A-list (intended for publication), B-list (as resources permit) and Under Construction — there are 17 documents: five software/AI, eight cross-cutting, four device-specific. The annual list has shrunk three years running: 29 in FY2025, 20 in FY2026, 17 in FY2027, and output has shrunk with it — CDRH has issued roughly 20 guidances so far in 2026 against an average near 40 a year in 2022–2024. Comments on the proposed lists are open through 30 November 2026 (FDA Law Blog, 18 Oct 2026).

The five software/AI items

(1) "Marketing Submission and Lifecycle Management Recommendations for Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions" (final); (2) "Generative Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Conversational Devices for Mental Disorders: Evidentiary Recommendations" (draft); (3) "Policy for Device Software Functions" (draft, retitled from the version that also covered mobile medical applications); (4) "Postmarket Management of Cybersecurity in Medical Devices" (draft); (5) "Risk Assessment and Evidentiary Considerations for Generative Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Devices" (Under Construction). One more AI-adjacent final sits on the cross-cutting list: "Predetermined Change Control Plans for Medical Devices" (FDA Law Blog).

Why it matters

This list is the most practical read on where the 19 October comment pile is headed: the FDA has already put "Risk Assessment and Evidentiary Considerations for Generative AI-Enabled Devices" under construction, the mental-disorders conversational device evidence paper in draft, and lifecycle management for AI device software functions in final. The discussion paper is not guidance, but the work is on the schedule. That the list fell from 29 to 17 while actual output halved means the timing remains unknown — which is exactly what the Chamber was aiming at when it asked the FDA to publish a timeline for authoritative guidance.

The 5/8/4 split and the 29→20→17 comparison come from the law firm's blog analysis; this report has not checked them item by item against CDRH's own list page, and the post itself does not break out separate A-list and B-list counts.

UK MHRAAdvaMed10/20

The MHRA takes lifecycle regulation and mutual reliance to AdvaMed in the US, and will station an FDA liaison in Washington

What

The UK's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency trailed on 19 October 2026 that Jessica Paulsen, its Deputy Director for AI and Software, would set out its approach to software and AI-enabled devices at AdvaMed on Tuesday 20 October: more flexible lifecycle regulation, regulatory sandboxes, closer international collaboration. Under the MHRA 2030 Strategy that means a modern, adaptive framework for AI as a medical device, a new MHRA Centre for AI and Digital Health, better device data and traceability, and expanded reliance and recognition routes with trusted regulators including the FDA. Paulsen's line: "AI is changing medical technology, and regulation must keep pace." (GOV.UK, 19 Oct 2026)

The machinery

The MHRA–FDA Pioneer Programme, announced in June 2026, creates reciprocal liaison roles at both agencies; the MHRA is recruiting a Regional Deputy Director of US Partnerships based in Washington, D.C. to serve as its liaison officer to the FDA. Both agencies keep making independent decisions, but the MHRA says openly that it wants to explore greater use of international reliance routes, including for devices already approved by the FDA. On the sandbox side, AI Airlock has already been used to test the lifecycle approach, and new regional partnerships with NHS London and Manchester University NHS Foundation Trust will test technologies in live care settings (GOV.UK).

Why it matters

With the FDA still taking comment and the EU having just pushed high-risk duties back a year, the MHRA chose to sell "we have already worked this out" on the American medtech industry's home turf. For manufacturers the real inducement is not the sandbox but reliance: if a device already approved by the FDA can enter the UK through an international reliance route, Britain stops being a third market to run and becomes an extension of the first. It is one of the few structural cards the UK can still play post-Brexit.

Discount this

This is the MHRA's own release, describing a developing approach and a 2030 ambition rather than rules in force; neither the Centre for AI and Digital Health nor the expanded reliance routes comes with a legislative timetable yet, and this report has no transcript of what Paulsen actually said at AdvaMed.

UK AI Airlock44 recommendations10/06

The UK accepts all 44 recommendations: AI devices to be watched across their whole working life, with draft change-management guidance in December

What

On 6 October 2026 the UK government accepted in full all 44 recommendations of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, and launched the next phase of the AI Airlock sandbox the same day. The Commission — an independent, non-statutory advisory body led by NHS doctors, set up by the MHRA in September 2025, chaired by Professor Alastair Denniston with Professor Henrietta Hughes, England's Patient Safety Commissioner, as deputy — published its 44 recommendations on 10 September 2026, on the back of a year-long UK-wide evidence programme involving more than 12,000 people. The government response spans ten priority areas, overseen by a newly established Programme Board. Its central finding: AI-enabled devices should be monitored across their whole working life rather than assessed once, because the current rules were built for static products while AI device performance varies by setting, workflow, user and surrounding organisation (GOV.UK, 6 Oct 2026; MedTech Dive).

Timeline

22 Oct 2026: AI Airlock Phase 3 applicant webinar at 10:00. November 2026: first wave of innovators selected, this cohort focused on post-market surveillance and lifecycle regulation. December 2026: MHRA draft guidance on managing changes to AI-enabled devices as they adapt over time. Start of 2027: consultation on how to qualify and classify AI-enabled devices. Spring 2027: full implementation roadmap covering all 44 recommendations. AI Airlock has three years of additional government funding. MHRA Chief Executive Lawrence Tallon called the Commission's work "a clear and ambitious blueprint," aimed at "making sure patients can benefit from safe, trusted AI more quickly"; Scotland will set out its formal response later (GOV.UK).

Why it matters

What the AHA asked the FDA to do, Britain has already signed up to: move monitoring from one premarket moment to the whole working life. The difference is that Britain also absorbs the cost into the public system — three years of sandbox funding, NHS site partnerships, staged authorisation pathways all put the state behind early-deployment risk. The US has no equivalent, which is why it ends up as hospitals and industry passing the duty back and forth. The detail worth watching is staged authorisation: letting promising tools into NHS use earlier under close supervision while real-world evidence accrues is, logically, a sandbox promoted to a standard route.

Discount this

"Accepted in full" is a policy commitment, not rules in force: the MHRA has put the substantive consultation in 2027 and the full roadmap in spring 2027. The GOV.UK page also notes that the number of priority areas was corrected from nine to ten.

EU AI ActDigital Omnibus2026/1744

The Digital Omnibus pushes high-risk duties for AI inside medical devices from 2 Aug 2027 to 2 Aug 2028 — while the transparency duties move not one day

What

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was proposed by the Commission on 19 November 2025 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. The line that matters most for devices amends Article 113 of the AI Act: for Annex I embedded high-risk systems — which cover AI in devices under the MDR and IVDR — the Chapter III, Sections 1–3 obligations now apply from 2 August 2028 instead of 2 August 2027, a full year's deferral; Annex III standalone high-risk systems move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. The deferred duties include data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency to users, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity. Legacy public-authority systems have until 2 August 2030, and the deadline for national regulatory sandboxes is extended to 2 August 2027 (artificialintelligenceact.eu; COSM).

What did not move

The MDR and IVDR stay in Annex I Section A, so AI-enabled devices remain high-risk: the Commission's proposal in COM(2025) 1023 of 16 December 2025 to move them to Section B was not adopted. The Article 6(1) classification logic from MDCG 2025-6 is unchanged, and devices going through notified body assessment are generally still high-risk. The Article 50 transparency duties still apply from 2 August 2026, with a watermarking grace period to 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market before that date. Article 5 prohibitions and the GPAI rules are unchanged, with AI Office enforcement over GPAI models starting 2 August 2026, and a new Article 5 prohibition on AI-generated child sexual abuse material and non-consensual intimate imagery takes effect 2 December 2026 (not device-specific). Separately the safety-component definition is narrowed, so functions that merely assist users or optimise performance are no longer automatically high-risk, and a simplified registration duty for systems claiming the Article 6(3) exemption is reinstated; the Commission may use implementing acts to limit overlapping requirements where sectoral law already imposes equivalents — those acts have not been issued (COSM).

Why it matters

The gap between the two dates is itself the message. The deferral is blamed on late harmonised standards, not on softened duties — the EU is in effect conceding that, absent technical standards to work to, asking manufacturers to demonstrate compliance on data governance, robustness and human oversight is unenforceable. Set against Britain betting on sandboxes and post-market monitoring, and America still asking whether to regulate at all, the EU is the only one that has written the obligations into law and then found it has no measuring stick. The practical consequence for manufacturers: the high-risk package slips a year, but generative interactions facing patients or families — the Article 50 labelling duties — have to be in place by early December.

Discount this

Article numbers and dates come from consultancy and tracker write-ups, not the Official Journal text; artificialintelligenceact.eu dates the regulation "8 July 2026" while COSM gives 24 July 2026 as the OJ publication date, though both agree on entry into force on 27 July 2026, which is what this report uses. Check OJ L, 202601744 before relying on any of it.

US states CaliforniaAB 1979 / SB 50309/30

Washington regulates the product, the states regulate its use: California signs four laws, vetoes two, and locks responsibility back onto the licensed clinician

What

California signed four healthcare-AI bills at the end of September 2026. AB 1979 (signed 30 September 2026, effective 1 January 2027) requires health facilities, clinics and physicians to take "reasonable steps" to ensure a licensed provider exercises independent clinical judgment where care is informed by a clinical decision support system; AI may not independently perform functions reserved to licensed professionals, nor direct unlicensed staff to perform them, while administrative tools such as scheduling and appointment reminders are exempt. The law also treats a business offering a consumer "health care chatbot" as a healthcare provider for CMIA purposes only. Violations count as unprofessional conduct, including unlawful practice of medicine, enforced by the Medical Board of California and sister boards. SB 503 (effective 1 January 2027) requires developers and deployers of CDS systems to make reasonable efforts to identify and mitigate biased impacts on characteristics such as race, sex and age; developers must hand deployers documentation on intended uses, training data, performance and bias evaluation and monitoring, and deployers must monitor and mitigate (Holland & Knight, Oct 2026).

The other two, and the vetoes

SB 947 (effective 1 July 2027) governs employers' automated decision systems, which reaches hospitals and health systems as employers: software output alone cannot justify discipline or termination, a human must corroborate, and such systems may not be used to breach labour or civil rights law, infer protected status or retaliate. AB 1609 (signed 28 September 2026, effective 1 January 2027) is not healthcare-specific but bites: it covers large private businesses with over $500 million in gross annual national revenue, which must disclose chatbot use, may not present a bot as human, must make a good-faith effort to connect a customer to a human agent within 15 minutes during business hours (or schedule an appointment), and must post customer service contact details; hospital communications about providing, managing, paying for or operating healthcare services are exempt. Fines run to $5,000 for a first violation and $10,000 for each subsequent one, with no private right of action. The two vetoes: SB 903, which would have limited AI in psychotherapy to administrative and supplementary support, required informed consent and barred marketing chatbots as therapists, vetoed by Governor Newsom as too broad in scope; and AB 2575, which would have shielded clinicians who rely on or override CDS from retaliation and barred vendors from treating a failure to override as a superseding cause of harm, vetoed as impractical and outside the labour commissioner's medical expertise (Holland & Knight).

The collision

Writing in Forbes on 19 October 2026, emergency physician Jesse Pines puts the structure plainly: the FDA regulates the product and is loosening; the states regulate practice and are tightening. His figures: of 1,357 AI devices cleared or approved through early December 2025, only 34 (2.5%) had been tested in prospective trials and just three studied using patient-centred outcomes; and in January 2026 the FDA loosened its clinical decision support software guidance, letting tools that recommend a single diagnosis or treatment, where the clinician can review the reasoning, avoid device requirements. The states run the other way: Texas requires practitioners using AI for diagnosis to review AI-generated records and disclose the use to patients; Utah's regulatory sandbox lets the AI system Doctronic gradually take over renewing chronic-condition prescriptions, though the state Medical Licensing Board has asked for it to be suspended; and in Pennsylvania a bipartisan bill from state representatives Arvind Venkat and Joe Hogan would require disclosure of AI use in clinical and coverage decisions plus compliance statements on bias and privacy. A December 2025 executive order directed the attorney general to stand up an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws, but Pines notes an executive order cannot rewrite state statutes, and Congress had already stripped a proposed ten-year moratorium on state AI laws out of a budget bill (Forbes, 19 Oct 2026).

Discount this

The Forbes piece is a columnist's argument, and the 1,357 / 34 / 2.5% figures are his citation of a census this report has not obtained. Effective dates and penalties for the California bills come from a law firm's analysis; check the enacted text for the detail. The Holland & Knight piece does not cover utilization review or clinician-title provisions.

China NMPAAI 醫療器械09/14–09/15

China's NMPA fills the gap with two drafts — a horizontal framework plus vertical clinical evaluation — while its new GMP inspection regime, 200 items deep, starts 1 November

What

China's National Medical Products Administration issued a draft "Guideline on Artificial Intelligence Medical Devices" on 14 September 2026 and a draft "Guideline on Clinical Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence Multi-Disease Auxiliary Decision-Making Medical Device" on 15 September 2026. Industry analysis reads them as layered: a horizontal framework covering the AI lifecycle and algorithms, plus vertical clinical evaluation rules for complex AI products. In the same window, the "Guideline for Inspection under the Medical Device Good Manufacturing Practices" was issued on 15 September 2026 and takes effect 1 November 2026, breaking the new GMP into 200 inspection items — 39 critical, 88 major, 73 general; the revised GMP itself was published in November 2025 and took effect 1 October 2026, with an accompanying GMP/GVP suite shifting post-market oversight toward continuous inspection. A further 130 Class II device draft guidelines were published on 1 September 2026 (16 IVD, 55 non-active, 59 active), with feedback closing 8 October 2026 (China Med Device, NMPA Roundup October 2026). At policy level, the NMPA issued its "Implementation Opinions on 'Artificial Intelligence + Drug Regulation'" in August 2026 (NMPA English).

Why it matters

Of the four jurisdictions, only China has put out both AI-specific guidance and an enforceable inspection checklist in the same season. America is asking whether to regulate, the EU concedes it lacks standards, Britain is betting on a sandbox and a roadmap; the NMPA's move is framework first, then checklist — and a 200-item scheme with 39 critical findings is, in substance, lifecycle regulation turned into auditable actions. For exporters that also means the compliance bar in China becomes concretely measurable after 1 November, which is more than the other three can currently offer.

Discount this

The roundup does not list the specific requirements or comment deadlines of the two AI guidelines; this report has not checked the NMPA's own notices clause by clause, nor confirmed whether either has been finalised. This item rests on a consultancy's monthly summary, a secondary source.

02 — Product Analysis

A regulation day offers no new model to dissect, but two products sit squarely on different jurisdictional fault lines: one squeezed out of a market by a device definition, the other let into one by a single state's sandbox.

Ash — Slingshot AI

Consumer LLM mental-health product · Slingshot AI (US)

Function and position. Ash is a direct-to-consumer LLM mental-health app that its maker positions as a "wellbeing" product rather than a medical device. Slingshot AI has raised $93 million, with investors including Andreessen Horowitz. On 21 January 2026 the company said it was withdrawing Ash from the United Kingdom because the app might conflict with the country's medical device rules, and that Ash would not be available there after 23 January; CEO Daniel Reid Cahn emailed users that "there isn't a clear regulatory pathway for wellbeing products like ours," and the company said it was in talks with the government about a remedy (STAT News, 21 Jan 2026).

  • Strength : in the US it lands precisely in the gap today's argument leaves open. The FDA loosened CDS guidance in January 2026, the Chamber is arguing that patient-facing tools should not be presumed higher risk (U.S. Chamber, 19 Oct 2026), and California's SB 903 was vetoed, shutting off a dedicated AI-psychotherapy statute for now — so consumer products still have room to operate.
  • Concern : "not a device" is a claim, not a status. Britain has already demonstrated what happens when the regulator disagrees with the classification — the product leaves. And the FDA has put "Generative AI-Enabled Conversational Devices for Mental Disorders: Evidentiary Recommendations" on its FY2027 draft list (FDA Law Blog, 18 Oct 2026), while California's AB 1979 treats consumer health-care chatbot businesses as providers for CMIA purposes. Both are closing in. The STAT report also gives no user numbers, so the revenue impact of the UK exit cannot be assessed here.

Doctronic — Utah AI Policy Sandbox

AI prescription-renewal system inside a state regulatory sandbox · Utah, US

Function and position. Under the Utah Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy's regulatory sandbox, Doctronic processes 30-, 60- and 90-day renewals of medications already prescribed by licensed physicians. The system cannot issue new prescriptions, handle controlled or addictive substances, or change a treatment plan. It remains in phase one, in which a licensed practitioner must approve every renewal before it is issued; the state says each phase carries "extensive identity and prescription verification protocols" (Utah News Dispatch, 28 Apr 2026).

  • Strength : it is the first state-level sandbox in the US to push AI into prescription renewal, and the sandbox is exactly what everyone is asking for today — the Chamber wants a National Clinical AI Sandbox from the FDA (U.S. Chamber), while Britain has taken AI Airlock to a third phase with three more years of funding (GOV.UK, 6 Oct 2026). The state also reports no serious safety incidents so far, and says medical professionals reviewed the pilot before launch with many of their suggested adjustments built in.
  • Concern : the sandbox's authority comes from the executive branch while licensure's comes from the medical board — and Utah's Medical Licensing Board has written to the Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy demanding immediate suspension, on the grounds that it should have been consulted before implementation rather than after go-live. The board argues refills require physician reassessment for dose adjustment, side-effect monitoring, contraindications and new drug interactions, and wrote that "we must not allow AI or other financial motivations to override this obligation." A third-party red-teaming report found "potential vulnerabilities under adversarial usage," with no count given. The state declined to suspend, citing phase one's per-refill physician review and saying it may modify or cancel the programme if safety benchmarks are not met (Utah News Dispatch; STAT News, 24 Apr 2026).

Both cases point at the same gap. Ash shows that calling yourself "not a device" offers no resistance when someone else holds the classification power; Doctronic shows that holding a sandbox permission offers no resistance when someone else holds the licensure power. Everyone is talking about post-market monitoring and sandboxes today, but both presuppose an answer to a prior question: who gets to decide what the thing is. America has not settled that, and it is the root of the FDA-versus-states split.

American Hospital Association
The hospitals' voice, speaking for the deployment end		 Says it represents nearly 5,000 hospitals and health systems, over 270,000 affiliated physicians and 2 million nurses and caregivers; wrote to CDRH Director Michelle Tarver on 19 October arguing that developers own post-market monitoring, citing 96% via 510(k) and 74% of hospitals needing multiple governance teams (AHA, 19 Oct 2026). Today's competition is not company against company but jurisdiction against jurisdiction. All four major markets agree AI devices need continuous watching; they disagree only on who pays, who classifies and who penalises. Britain gathers all three into one agency, with the MHRA running sandbox, monitoring and reliance together; China turns it into 200 inspection items; the EU has written the law and admits it has no measuring stick; and America splits in half between a federal government regulating the product and states regulating practice — and the crack is exactly where Ash and Doctronic are standing.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan is not behind in this round so much as running three lines in parallel that have never been tied together

(1) On guidance, Taiwan has already done half of what the AHA is asking the FDA to do. On 29 May 2026 the Ministry of Health and Welfare issued its Guideline on the Use of Generative AI in Medical Institutions (letter No. 衛部醫字第1151663164號), covering public and private hospitals and clinics preparing to adopt or already using generative AI across record-drafting assistance, clinical decision support, administrative documents and patient communication, and expressly leaving out AI agent systems with autonomous decision-making for now. It sets out six risk categories (foundation-model bias, external data source risks, hallucination, security attacks such as prompt injection and data leakage, overreliance eroding clinical judgment, and outages of external models or cloud services), five core principles and nine provisions across three lifecycle phases, and requires vendor contract management to include disclosure of the large language model used, licensed clinicians to bear final responsibility for clinical judgment and records, and patients to be told the purpose and limits whenever generative AI interacts with them or records audio or video (Lee and Li newsletter, 26 Jun 2026). The missing half is the hardest line in the AHA letter: the guideline is administrative advice, not a mandate, and it assigns no primary monitoring duty to developers.

(2) The EU's extra year is real for Taiwanese exporters, but it is not a blanket postponement. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 pushes the obligations for Annex I embedded high-risk systems — AI devices under the MDR and IVDR among them — to 2 August 2028, buying a year to assemble evidence on data governance, technical documentation, logging and human oversight. But the Article 50 transparency duties have applied since 2 August 2026, and the watermarking grace period for systems already on the market expires on 2 December 2026. For a Taiwanese manufacturer shipping generative features that interact directly with patients or families in the EU, the first thing to fix is labelling, not technical documentation — and that is under seven weeks away (COSM). Worth watching too: the Commission's COM(2025) 1023 proposal to move the MDR and IVDR from Annex I Section A to Section B was not adopted, so AI-enabled devices stay high-risk in the EU and the classification logic of MDCG 2025-6 is unchanged.

(3) Evidence, reimbursement and registration are all moving, but none is tied to the others the way the MHRA has tied them. The Ministry has been building an AI impact research centre system since 2024, formally launched at the Taiwan Clinical AI Impact Forum 2025 on 20 November 2025 by Li Chien-chang, Director of the Ministry's Information Technology Office; five medical centres — Taipei Veterans General, Taichung Veterans General, Tri-Service General, National Cheng Kung University Hospital and National Taiwan University Hospital — serve as demonstration sites running AI clinical trials, with local data expected to reach the NHI HTA evaluation centre and then the joint drafting committee in about three years. Li put the crux well: once something is covered by the NHI it almost never leaves, so the early design has to be careful (United Daily News, 20 Nov 2025). On the registration side, the TFDA's Smart Medical Device Project Office was set up on 7 May 2021 alongside the Medical Devices Act, offering a single window and lifecycle-management counselling for AI and machine-learning devices and drafting AI device policy guidance (MOHW). Set against the MHRA holding sandbox, post-market monitoring and international reliance inside one agency under one 44-point roadmap, Taiwan's three lines are each sound — but the joint between them, whether evidence generated in a pilot can feed the NHI committee directly and whether it can simplify registration in turn, is not institutionalised. That joint is precisely what all four jurisdictions were competing over today.
