Thursday · Technology

The week agents crossed from saying to doing: the wet lab, the records pipe, and the model's identity all landed at once

Thursday, November 5, 2026 · Covering Oct 27–Nov 4 · Taipei time

This week's technology story is not a leaderboard move but a change in what models are allowed to touch with their own hands. Nature published AutoDNA on Nov 4, from a Shanghai Jiao Tong University team: four planning agents (Experiment Planner, Literature Researcher, Reagent Manager, Hypothesis Proposer) and two execution agents (Program Developer, Hardware Executor & Validator), with every instrument operation wrapped as a Python object carrying a natural-language description so the model can reason about hardware and call it directly. It drives more than twenty classes of instrument; humans only restock reagents and maintain the machines. In the same week athenahealth opened its athenaPatient MCP server on Nov 2, letting patients hand labs, medications, diagnoses and visit history to Claude, and Anthropic open-sourced FlashProve on Nov 3, so anyone can verify which model produced an output using a cryptographic fingerprint that reveals no weights. What hospitals should actually stop for, though, is Doximity's Oct 29 audit of 261,508 dosages: at least 2.3% wrong, roughly 20 serious dosing errors per 1,000 answers, and 3.4% of the errors it caught potentially lethal. Capability is moving downstream faster than measurement.

Lead · Autonomous lab NatureAutoDNA上海交通大學11/04

Nature, Nov 4: six agents, twenty-plus instrument classes — AutoDNA hands the whole DNA synthesis-to-sequencing pipeline to the model

What

On November 4, Nature published "An AI-native experimental laboratory for autonomous biomolecular operations" (Wu et al.). The system, AutoDNA, comes from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and operates more than twenty classes of instrument, covering nucleic-acid synthesis, transcription, amplification and sequencing, with applications aimed at diagnostics, drug development and information storage. Architecturally, every instrument operation is wrapped as a Python object carrying a natural-language description — "atomic services" — so LLM agents can reason about hardware and call it directly. Planning is split across an Experiment Planner, Literature Researcher, Reagent Manager and Hypothesis Proposer; execution across a Program Developer and a Hardware Executor & Validator; and the scheduler tracks live instrument status with functional-equivalence rerouting (using a thermal cycler when the heater is busy). The preprint's figures (arXiv 2507.02379): 25 instruments integrated; 97.7% stepwise yield for enzymatic DNA synthesis after an eight-cycle PAGE optimization, with the trajectory climbing from 93.00% to 97.79%; a DNA-storage round trip taking 162.9 hours and 9,365 hardware steps, encoding 46 bytes into 78 strands with no manual intervention beyond reagent refills; a 3.60x speedup over sequential execution in multi-user scheduling, saving 168.8 minutes; heater utilization rising from 55.0% to 79.0% (1.44x) and the thermal cycler from 47.6% to 80.6% (1.69x). HIStalk's Nov 4 summary of the published version adds that it matched expert-optimized DNA synthesis yields, ran concurrent experiments 3.2-fold faster, and detected and validated a cancer-associated mutation.

Why it matters

For two years, agent talk in healthcare has stayed at the text layer: drafting notes, looking up guidelines, filing prior authorizations. AutoDNA puts agents into a physical world that breaks, contaminates and consumes real reagents — and it picks the most upstream stretch of molecular diagnostics to do it in. Two consequences land directly on the clinical side. First, the bottleneck in companion diagnostics and sequencing was never only the algorithm; it was who re-runs that PCR plate at three in the morning. Second, once a protocol is driven by model-generated code, "how was this assay actually performed" stops being a lab notebook and becomes a replayable execution trace — good news for the audit layer in item 8 below, and an open question for regulatory frameworks such as CLIA and LDT oversight, which are built around personnel qualifications and written SOPs.

Discount this

The peer commentary is not gentle. The headline yield and storage results each come from a single run, with no replicates and no error bars; the "matches human experts" claim is measured against a literature value from a different lab rather than a same-batch parallel control; nothing is reported on LLM code-generation failure rates, retries or human recovery events; the 3.60x throughput gain is against the system's own sequential execution, not an external platform; and the boundary of "autonomy" is genuinely fuzzy at the reagent-refill step. Note also that the published version's 3.2-fold figure disagrees with the preprint's 3.60x, and the published version should govern — this report could not retrieve nature.com directly, so the architecture and detailed figures here are cited from the preprint and its public review page rather than verified line by line against the journal text.

Measurement · Medication safety DoximityDoseCheckRxIndex10/29

261,508 dosages across nine 2026-vintage models: at least 2.3% wrong, and 3.4% of the errors caught are potentially lethal

What

On October 29, Doximity medical director Alex Blau published a dosing audit: nine widely used LLMs, all released in 2026, answered more than 8,000 dosing questions "modeled on real physician queries" across 2,204 drugs, for 261,508 dosages evaluated in total. Severity was scored on the EQUIP scale with a sensitivity analysis on NCC MERP. At least 2.3% of doses were wrong, which the author calls a conservative lower bound. Roughly 20 of every 1,000 answers contained a serious dosing error, and about 30.5 per 1,000 fell into NCC MERP categories E–H — errors that caused or could cause harm. Of the errors detected, 43.8% were serious and 3.4% potentially lethal; among the most severe, one in five was a unit or decimal error and one in twenty confused two medications or two routes of administration. The cleanest risk stratifier was recency: drugs approved in 2024 or later carried 5.1 times the odds of error (95% CI 3.9–6.5). The same post launched the countermeasure, DoseCheck.

Why it matters

This is the week's only measurement that breaks an agentic clinical action down to an actionable grain, and the remedy it points to runs against the prevailing instinct: not stacking a second model to review the first, but handing fields with a single correct answer — dosing above all — to a deterministic lookup layer. Unit and decimal errors account for a fifth of the most severe mistakes; catching those requires no reasoning at all, and they are precisely where a language model deserves least trust. The 5.1x figure, meanwhile, yields a rule that can be written into policy today: for drugs approved since 2024, a model-produced dose should not enter a prescribing workflow without validation against structured data.

Discount this

This is a vendor's own blog, and Doximity is selling the fix in the same breath. The page carries no full study, no statement of peer-review status, no per-model breakdown — not one of the nine models is named — and no independent replication. How the "at least 2.3%" lower bound was defined, and how 8,000 questions were modeled on real queries, rest on the author's account alone. DoseCheck's sub-100-millisecond latency and RxIndex coverage are likewise vendor-reported.

Verifiability · Open source AnthropicFlashProve11/03

FlashProve: proving which model produced an output, with a cryptographic fingerprint that reveals no weights

What

On November 3, Anthropic published and open-sourced FlashProve, aimed at "provable inference": a lab publishes a fingerprint for each model it releases, and any output can later be matched against that fingerprint and verified cryptographically. Three design properties carry the idea — the fingerprint does not reveal model weights, so closed commercial models can participate without surrendering their most valuable asset; verification is light enough to run on a local device; and the scheme is meant to resist impostors, including the lab that built and runs the model. Anthropic also released a detailed technical specification and working code for various elements on GitHub (the article does not name the license). Stated use cases: catching a provider that quietly swaps in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive one to inflate benchmark results; letting users confirm the model they are using is the one they think it is; and verifying third-party claims that a code security scan or business-plan review was performed by a particular advanced model.

Why it matters

Healthcare is where model identity bites hardest, because every output here can end up in a legal record. For a note summary, a differential list or a draft imaging report, the only evidence of "which model, which version" is today the vendor's own logs and assurances — and the vendor is precisely the party with the strongest incentive to swap models, for cost, capacity or latency. Set this beside the HTI-5 deregulatory rule HHS finalized in early November, which cuts 14 certification criteria, revises 7 more and projects over $1.5 billion in savings, and the shape is clear: federal rules just removed source-attribute disclosure for predictive AI from EHR certification, and in the same week an open-source cryptographic tool proposed a route to the same end that needs no regulatory mandate. Both address one problem; one does it with rules, the other with mathematics.

Discount this

This is not a product. The researchers describe the work as "meant to share intermediate progress, and not a completed research agenda," and say plainly that they are publishing it "with the goal that others look at what we have done, take it, and improve it." Neither Anthropic nor any other lab has plans to deploy FlashProve. TechCrunch reports no overhead multiples, model sizes or latency figures (a "72x overhead" number circulating in third-party write-ups is not adopted as fact here). Until a lab actually publishes fingerprints, this gives hospital procurement no usable leverage.

Plumbing · MCP athenahealthClaudeathenaPatient11/02

A new pipe out of the EHR: the athenaPatient MCP server lets patients hand their labs and medications to Claude themselves

What

On November 2, athenahealth announced the athenaPatient MCP server, with a subheadline saying it "establishes a way for patients to have more informed, personalized conversations" about their health. athenahealth's own technical note says the shareable scope covers labs, medications, diagnoses and visit history. The protocol is the Model Context Protocol; the consumer on the other end is Anthropic's Claude. This is the first time a mainstream EHR vendor has turned patient-side record export into an MCP interface a consumer AI can call directly, rather than a traditional FHIR API or a patient-portal download.

Why it matters

The direction of this pipe matters: it is not the hospital handing data to an AI, it is the patient exercising a right of access and then handing the data onward. Under US law that route sidesteps most of the HIPAA business-associate machinery — once data is in the patient's hands and passed to a consumer service, the protective umbrella over protected health information changes shape. Structurally, it means an EHR vendor no longer needs a hospital IT committee's blessing to get its data into a frontier model's context. For patient safety, it means the 2.3% dosing error rate in item 2 above starts operating on people who hold their own complete medication list and have no clinical training whatsoever.

Discount this

The press release is extremely thin. It states no rollout scope or timeline, no patient or practice counts, nothing on consent flow, data-retention policy or revocation, and carries no executive quotes. The page retrieved for this report contained little beyond headline and subheadline, with the body detail missing; the "first time" characterization is this report's reading against existing EHR patient-side interfaces, not a claim athenahealth makes.

Clinical decision support Mayo Clinic Platform35+ 專科11/03

Mayo Clinic Platform packages its own knowledge as CDS: LLM chat across 35+ specialties plus 22 clinical calculators, straight into the EHR

What

On November 3, Mayo Clinic Platform announced a new clinical decision support solution, available from November. Two components: a specialty chat covering more than 35 specialties, mirroring Mayo's subspecialty model, powered by a large language model, citing peer-reviewed literature, updated daily, and embeddable directly in the EHR; and 22 clinical calculators callable by chat or voice dictation, mixing industry-standard tools with proprietary Mayo-developed ones. Users are Mayo's own clinicians plus organizations in Mayo Clinic Platform's healthcare provider programs. Platform COO Maneesh Goyal frames the problem as the sheer volume of medical knowledge clinicians must reach; Gelareh Zadeh, chair of Neurosurgery at Rochester and the platform's chief medical officer, stresses that "it does not replace clinical judgment."

Why it matters

This is the week's clearest knowledge-as-asset transaction. The CDS market is currently being driven by literature-retrieval players such as OpenEvidence, now embedded in Epic across NYU Langone and serving 6,000-plus physicians and faculty. Mayo is taking a different line: what it sells is not retrieval but Mayo's own subspecialty consensus, which is the one thing that cannot be scraped. The 22 calculators are not filler either — pulling calculators into the chat surface is precisely the answer item 2 above argues for: fields with a single correct answer go to a deterministic tool rather than being computed by the model.

Discount this

The announcement contains not one performance figure: no accuracy, no citation-fidelity measure, no benchmark result, no user or institution counts, and no word on which underlying model it uses or whether that model is built or bought. "Integrable into a growing number of EHR environments" is an unverifiable sentence. Mayo says only that its responsible-AI approach covers safety, effectiveness, privacy, security and clinical value, with human oversight and ongoing monitoring — a description of process, not of outcome.

Zocdoc goes the other way: it opens its booking infrastructure to AI agents, and agent traffic grows nearly 30x in four months

What

On October 28, Zocdoc opened provider search, insurance verification and appointment booking to AI agents, through four routes: an MCP server, a REST API, agent guidance via llms.txt, and deep links. The first deep integration partner is Instinct, whose agent reaches the MCP server directly to make confirmed bookings, and which joins Zocdoc's Care Access Network alongside Google Gemini, Amazon Health AI, Yelp, Healthgrades and Blue Shield of California. Read this alongside item 4 and the week's plumbing diagram completes itself: MCP is becoming, at the same time, the interface by which records leave the EHR (athenahealth) and the interface by which care services get consumed by agents (Zocdoc). The "doubling every 10 days" figure deserves particular attention because it measures actual request volume rather than announcements — if it holds, the window in which provider organizations decide whether to support agent traffic is shorter than most expect. The other side: when a booking can be completed by an agent, nothing in any existing framework answers who is responsible if the agent books the wrong specialty and delays care.

Discount this

The 30x, the 10-day doubling and the two-weeks-beats-three-months claim are all Zocdoc's own numbers, and the release defines no measurement method — requests, completed bookings, or traffic classified by user-agent? No baseline is published, so a 30x rise could start from a very small number. Beyond Instinct, no actual usage volume is given for any partner.

Drug development · agents AnthropicEnvedaBigHatMithril10/27

1,061 pages of manufacturing documents, all 11 human-found issues caught plus seven more: Anthropic publishes early agent figures in drug development, and acquires the one-person Mithril

What

On October 27, Anthropic published early results from six drug-development partners. The Enveda case is the most concrete: manufacturing chemists had been spending up to 75% of their time on paperwork; Claude reviewed 1,061 pages, caught all 11 issues human reviewers had found and flagged seven more they had missed; human review time fell from over 25 hours to under three, a drop of 89%; a first draft of one regulatory document took four hours against roughly three months by hand, and standard human review of the second draft found one minor error. BigHat Biosciences: Claude proposed changes to two antibody-fragment leads, and the lab tested about 1,010 variants over about 10 weeks — the best versions bound more than 10x more tightly, the cancer-targeting lead stayed in solution 18°C higher, and the longer-lasting lead clumped more than 100x more slowly. Generate Biomedicines: one Claude-proposed design was about 70% shorter than the standard version and let immune cells make roughly 10x as many receptors. Divergence Labs: about 35,000 antibody designs in about a month, yielding three binders and 20 designs that could dislodge IgE, three of which appeared to outperform the standard medication. Bristol Myers Squibb uses Claude to assess clinical trial plans for an early-stage drug and estimate their chance of success, with expansion planned for 2027. On October 28 Anthropic acquired the one-person company Mithril Medicine (terms undisclosed); founder Cody Tranbarger had used Claude to coordinate work across 11 external labs on treatments for two rare forms of muscular dystrophy, one of which went from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months. If the candidates keep holding up, a drug could reach human trials as soon as late 2028.

Why it matters

The week's most underrated line is Enveda's four hours versus three months for a regulatory first draft. The bottleneck in drug development is habitually described as scientific, but CMC documentation, batch records and regulatory filings consume person-years that have nothing to do with science — and that is exactly the part an agent can eat without any biological breakthrough at all. The shape of Mithril deserves equal attention: one person plus 11 contract labs, with the model doing project coordination, reaching mouse proof-of-concept. That is the commercial version of what AutoDNA demonstrates in hardware, and a frontier lab has now put a price on it by acquisition.

Discount this

All of it comes from Anthropic's own blog, with no third-party audit or peer review. Anthropic itself states the research is early-stage and largely preclinical, with the drugs generally untested in humans; BigHat's team initially suspected its own heat-stability and aggregation metrics were misleading and reanalyzed with new ones; Divergence's results are from early tests; The Column Group's tooling is still being built. Mithril's "late 2028" is an explicit conditional, not a schedule. And Anthropic is mid-IPO process, having named AI's potential to find cures as its foremost application — so this post is simultaneously a science progress report and prospectus material.

The agent control plane grew three layers in two weeks: orchestration and tracing, identity and quotas, and a kill switch

What

On October 22, Corti released its Agentic Framework: a central orchestrator coordinating specialized agents, holding shared context and connecting to clinical systems and trusted medical knowledge sources; new trace APIs for investigating failures, with an open-source evaluation library and a Feedback API; built-in guardrails and human approval mechanisms; new Schema Connectors alongside existing MCP, A2A and sub-agent connectors; token-by-token streaming with interruptible tasks; models built on open foundations and served through an OpenAI-compatible API; and inference infrastructure developed with NVIDIA on NVIDIA GPUs and Triton Inference Server, with organizations free to choose models, infrastructure and deployment, including on-premises. On October 20, Rhapsody launched Guardian: it authenticates AI agents, enforces scoped access, applies rate limits, records agent, MCP and API activity and maintains an audit trail, positioned as turning CHAI and Joint Commission guidance into actual controls; available now, deployable standalone or alongside Rhapsody, Corepoint or another integration platform. Taken together, these three show that competition in healthcare AI in late 2026 has moved from how accurate the model is to whether the agent can be switched off, audited and rate-limited. The two external forecasts Corti cites state the pressure plainly: IDC estimates 1.2 billion AI agents operating globally by 2029, performing 217 billion actions daily; Gartner predicts that by 2027, 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents over governance gaps. For a hospital the point is not whose control plane is better but that the MCP interfaces in items 4 and 6 are opening now, and the three things that matter — identity, scoped access, rate limits — are not provided by the MCP protocol itself.

Corti's "over 100 million patients annually" and Dedalus's 540-million coverage figure are both company-stated, and Corti's release carries no latency or actual throughput numbers at all. The IDC and Gartner numbers are third-party forecasts quoted by a vendor, not measurements. CrowdStrike's kill switch, by its own account, still has endpoint termination rolling out.

Clinical evidence npj Digital MedicineMR-AINature Cancer10/27–11/03

Two pieces of evidence that agents actually save time: 25,000 notes scanned weekly for missed referrals, and 2.71 minutes saved annotating immunotherapy side effects

What

A system called MR-AI scanned roughly 25,000 notes weekly across seven primary care clinics looking for missed referrals; a quasi-experimental study in npj Digital Medicine estimates about a sevenfold increase in missed-referral detection. Separately, a crossover study in Nature Cancer found AI assistance cut immunotherapy side-effect annotation time by 2.71 minutes while improving accuracy. The counterweight is last week's much-quoted cost study: NEJM AI calculated AI-recommended care at $314 per visit against $97 for physicians, the gap coming mostly from extra tests and referrals. And one more in the other direction: a study across five models of drug-name recommendations found leading brands averaged 97.5% of first-choice recommendations, with models favoring brands over generics.

Why it matters

Together these four give a conclusion more useful than any one of them alone: agents have real, quantifiable value at finding what was missed (sevenfold, 2.71 minutes), and they skew systematically toward more at deciding what to add ($314 versus $97, 97.5% brand preference). That is not a model-quality problem but an objective-function one — these models were never trained to carry marginal cost or opportunity cost. The practical inference is direct: the agent interfaces landing this week should be wired first to detection and recall tasks, not to ordering and treatment-selection tasks.

Discount this

MR-AI is quasi-experimental rather than randomized, and a sevenfold effect size is easily inflated by a very low baseline detection rate; because nature.com could not be retrieved directly, that study's sample details and confidence intervals here are cited from Healthcare AI Guy's November 3 roundup rather than checked against the paper. Nature Cancer's 2.71 minutes is a difference on one annotation task, not total workflow time saved. The 97.5% in the drug-name study does not separate cases where a generic was available from those where none was.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products pointing opposite ways: AutoDNA hands as much decision-making as possible to the model, while DoseCheck deliberately takes decision-making back from it. Both answer the same question — how far down the stack a model should be trusted.

AutoDNA

AI-native autonomous biomolecular laboratory · Shanghai Jiao Tong University team (China)

Function and position. It is not an instrument but a software layer that wraps an entire wet lab as an API. The core design idea is co-design — models, experiments and instruments designed together — with each instrument operation becoming an atomic service carrying a natural-language description, so LLM agents can reason, generate driver code and execute directly. It covers nucleic-acid synthesis, transcription, amplification and sequencing, was published in Nature on November 4, targets molecular diagnostics, drug development and DNA information storage, and handles multiple users' requests concurrently. There is no buyer yet — this is a paper, not a product — but the likely buyers are obvious: central labs, CROs, and anyone running the same molecular workflow against a fixed SOP over and over.

  • Strength : scheduling is the real contribution. Multi-user scheduling ran 3.60x faster than sequential execution, saving 168.8 minutes, with heater utilization climbing from 55.0% to 79.0% and the thermal cycler from 47.6% to 80.6% — achieved through functional-equivalence rerouting (using a thermal cycler when the heater is busy), exactly the kind of move a human scheduler cannot be bothered to make.
  • Strength : the yield reaches a usable level. Enzymatic DNA synthesis hit 97.7% stepwise yield after eight PAGE optimization cycles, climbing from 93.00% to 97.79% — and the optimization loop itself, where an agent proposes a hypothesis, runs the experiment, reads the result and proposes the next round, is more persuasive than the final number.
  • Concern : failure rates are simply absent. No statistics on LLM code-generation failures, retries or human recovery events — and that is the very first number any lab would ask for when deciding whether to adopt this. It is missing, and missing it stays.
  • Concern : single runs, no replicates, no error bars, and the "matches human experts" claim is measured against another lab's published value rather than a same-batch parallel control; the throughput baseline is the system's own sequential mode. The preprint also carried no code or data availability statement — the published version addresses this with a Zenodo deposit, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.20624108.

Doximity DoseCheck

Deterministic dosage verification layer · Doximity (United States)

Function and position. It sits inside Doximity Ask, extracts every dosage claim in a model's output — drug, dose, route, frequency, indication — and checks each against Doximity's own structured drug database, RxIndex: 1,900 drug monographs and more than 36,000 dosing regimens, sourced from FDA labeling, guidelines and primary literature. The decisive architectural choice is not using a second language model to check the first: verification is a deterministic lookup, not a second round of reasoning. Matching doses get a green badge, each verified dose links back to its monograph, and results return in under 100 milliseconds. It is aimed at physicians — Doximity describes it as a free additional safety layer — and the business model is keeping physicians inside Doximity Ask.

  • Strength : the error class it attacks is exactly the one a model least deserves trust on and a lookup most easily catches. One in five of the most severe errors was a unit or decimal mistake — detectable with zero reasoning, and precisely the kind that an LLM-checking-an-LLM architecture tends to miss in both passes.
  • Strength : sub-100-millisecond latency plus a per-item link back to the monograph solves the oldest problem in clinical decision support — an alert has to be fast enough not to break flow, and has to show its basis on the spot. Mayo pulling 22 calculators into its chat surface on November 3 is the same move.
  • Concern : the coverage gap sits exactly where the danger is. RxIndex holds 1,900 monographs while the audit spanned 2,204 drugs, and the highest odds of error were on drugs approved since 2024 (5.1x, 95% CI 3.9–6.5) — the group least likely to be in the database yet. Doximity publishes no update cadence for RxIndex and no timeline for adding new approvals.
  • Concern : every figure is vendor-reported, and the vendor is both the discloser of the problem and the seller of the fix. The study is unpublished, its peer-review status unstated, none of the nine tested models named, no per-model breakdown given, and no independent replication performed. That does not make the numbers wrong, but it does mean they cannot yet carry a procurement decision.

athenahealth
EHR vendor · turned its record export into MCP this week		 Announced the athenaPatient MCP server on Nov 2, letting patients share labs, medications, diagnoses and visit history with Claude. No rollout scope, patient or practice counts, consent flow or retention policy published. The contrast case is Epic, which said it will keep providing AI transparency documentation and evaluation tools to customers even after ONC removed the requirement. The moat is ownership of the patient-side front door: routing through the patient's right of access, athenahealth needs no hospital IT committee's approval to get data into a frontier model — a flanking move on Epic. The weakness is that it pushes safety responsibility out to a consumer AI, and Doximity's 2.3% this week is a measurement of the error rate at precisely that end.
Corti · Rhapsody
Two ways to sell the agent control plane: orchestration versus governance		 Corti shipped its Agentic Framework on Oct 22: an orchestrator, trace APIs, an open-source evaluation library, a Feedback API, Schema Connectors alongside MCP, A2A and sub-agent connectors, interruptible token-by-token streaming, an OpenAI-compatible API, inference built with NVIDIA on GPUs and Triton, and on-premises deployment; it claims over 100 million patients served annually, with Dedalus as first customer (7,500-plus organizations, 5,700 labs, 540 million-plus people covered). Rhapsody launched Guardian on Oct 20: agent authentication, scoped access, rate limits and an audit trail across agent, MCP and API activity, mapped to CHAI and Joint Commission guidance, available now, with no metrics published. Both bet on the same proposition: the MCP protocol itself provides no identity, quotas or audit, so someone will sell that layer. Corti grows upward from clinical AI into a platform (moat: an existing dictation and clinical-model base plus European EHR distribution); Rhapsody grows upward from an integration engine into a governance layer (moat: the installed base of Rhapsody and Corepoint). The shared weakness: neither publishes any quantitative performance, and absent a regulatory mandate hospitals find it hard to budget for a control plane on its own.
Zocdoc
Care booking marketplace · chose to welcome agents rather than block them		 On Oct 28 it opened an MCP server, a REST API, llms.txt and deep links; Instinct is the first deep partner; the Care Access Network includes Google Gemini, Amazon Health AI, Yelp, Healthgrades and Blue Shield of California. It reports 200,000-plus verified providers, 175-plus scheduling integrations and 10,000-plus insurance plans, with agent traffic up nearly 30x since July, doubling roughly every 10 days since early September, and October's first two weeks exceeding the prior three months combined — all company data, with no measurement method defined. The moat is the two hardest pieces of plumbing to replicate — scheduling integrations and insurance verification. An agent can generate text; it cannot generate 175 scheduling-system connections. Strategically Zocdoc chose to become the supply side for agents rather than the intermediary they route around. The weakness is that it has placed itself on a path with no liability framework: when an agent books the wrong specialty, nothing allocates responsibility between Zocdoc, the agent developer and the clinician.

Today's competitive structure is a pipeline with one section missing. Upstream, capability is being pushed into the physical world (AutoDNA, Anthropic's drug-development partners). Midstream, data and services are being reshaped into agent-callable interfaces, with athenahealth and Zocdoc both choosing MCP. Downstream, control and audit are for sale (Corti, Rhapsody, CrowdStrike), with verifiability alongside (FlashProve). The missing section is measurement: along the whole pipeline, only Doximity published an error rate you could use to reject a given output, and even that one is vendor-calculated. With HTI-5 removing AI transparency requirements from EHR certification, the only instrument left to buyers is whatever vendors volunteer — and of this week's seven companies, five gave no number at all.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Each of this week's three threads maps onto a mechanism that already exists in Taiwan but has not been wired up: model identity to the responsible-AI centers, dosing error rates to local labeling and NHI prescribing rules, and the MCP pipeline to the FHIR data platform.

(1) FlashProve hands Taiwan's Responsible AI Execution Center an option that does not rest on vendor assurances. The Ministry of Health and Welfare launched three AI centers on October 7, 2024: a Responsible AI Execution Center (with both National Taiwan University Hospital and Taipei Veterans General Hospital as responsible hospitals), a Clinical AI Evidence Validation Center (NTUH, run jointly with the TFDA), and an AI Impact Research Center (Taipei Veterans General, run jointly with the National Health Insurance Administration). Thirty hospitals filed 48 applications; 16 hospitals passed with 19 projects, NTUH and Taipei Veterans holding two each. One of the Responsible AI Execution Center's charges is public disclosure of model, data and performance information, plus lifecycle management of models — and worldwide that charge currently has exactly one implementation: trusting the vendor's logs. What makes the fingerprint scheme Anthropic proposed on Nov 3 worth the validation center's attention is not maturity — Anthropic says it has no deployment plans — but that it rewrites model-version disclosure from a trust problem into a cryptographic one. For a center that validates models for the Taiwanese population and then watches vendors keep updating them, this is the only technical route available for confirming that what runs in production is still the model that was validated, without ever holding the weights.

(2) Doximity's 2.3% cannot be transplanted to Taiwan, and that is exactly the problem. That audit used RxIndex — 1,900 monographs and more than 36,000 dosing regimens built from FDA labeling, US guidelines and US primary literature — as ground truth. Taiwan's approved label doses, NHI prescribing rules and commonly stocked formulations do not line up with the American ones, and as far as this report can find, no public measurement exists of the same models' error rate in a Taiwanese prescribing context. The 5.1x pattern matters more here, not less: drugs approved since 2024 carry the highest odds of error, and in Taiwan that effect is amplified, because local launch timing and reimbursed indications frequently diverge from the US, while the model most likely answers from the US label. Reproducing a deterministic verification layer like DoseCheck does not lack technology — it is a lookup, under 100 milliseconds, with no second model required. What is missing is a structured, continuously updated Taiwanese dosing dataset that encodes NHI prescribing rules. That job is far smaller than training a medical model, and not one of the AI centers' published mandates includes it.

(3) FHIR solves the data format; MCP decides how an agent legitimately reaches it — Taiwan has only the first half. Both athenahealth and Zocdoc chose MCP this week. Taiwan's counterpart is the AI Smart Healthcare Data Platform announced on June 26, 2026 by the Taiwan Medical Device Association with ITRI: billed as Taiwan's first smart healthcare data platform built on the FHIR standard, with nine device companies taking part (Far EasTone, Crystalvue, MacDo, Point Robotics, EBM Technologies, QT Medical, Bosen, Soundwave and Weichien), ITRI acting as a neutral third-party institution for data integration and FHIR implementation, and coverage spanning physiological monitoring, ECG, medical imaging, ultrasound, operating-room and telemedicine data, with the aim of shortening the one-to-two-year validation cycles the industry commonly faces. The two layers are complementary, not substitutes: FHIR makes data machine-readable, MCP decides under what authorization an agent gets to read it — and the third layer, the identity, scoped access and rate limiting discussed in item 7, is barely even a conversation in Taiwan yet. A hospital that opens agent access on top of a FHIR platform without adding that layer is wiring Zocdoc's self-reported doubling-every-10-days curve into an interface with no quota.
