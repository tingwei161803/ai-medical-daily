Saturday · General AI

Agents are already acting inside systems nobody authorised. The machinery to clean up after them has only just broken ground — and hospitals are not on the list

Saturday, October 10, 2026 · Covering Oct 3–10 · Taipei time

Not one of this week's big general-AI stories was a benchmark score. On 8 Oct, at SecTor 2026 in Toronto, Zenity Labs disclosed AgentCorruption: starting from a single public-facing agent, researchers reached every Bedrock AgentCore agent in the same AWS account and region, read private conversations and long-term memories across users and sessions, pulled third-party credentials out of Secrets Manager, and planted malicious memories so that a user keeps talking to an agent that quietly answers to the attacker. The chain was reported to AWS on 25 December 2025; going public took nine and a half months. The same day, Anthropic launched its Cyber Mission, whose 11 founding partners cover grid, water, transport and government OT — and not one hospital. Also that day, CrowdStrike tied an AI pentest toolchain to data theft at Shinhan Bank and Yegaram Savings Bank in South Korea. And the industry figures Axios reached on 9 Oct put the window for the next major AI incident at six to twelve months. Lay those four over healthcare and the most uncomfortable number comes from hospitals themselves: in Imprivata's survey of 250 officials, published 15 Sept, 72% of health organisations have AI tools or agents running with no formal IT approval.

01 — Top Stories

Eight stories in two groups: the first five are agent trust boundaries breaking in practice, the last three are what actually got cheap this week
Agent security AWSBedrock AgentCoreZenity Labs10/08

One public-facing agent, every agent in the account prised open: AgentCorruption demonstrates in full that there is no boundary between agents

What

On 8 Oct at SecTor 2026, Zenity Labs disclosed AgentCorruption, a chain of flaws in Amazon Bedrock AgentCore. AgentCore let an agent reach the Instance Metadata Service and retrieve credentials for its underlying machine, and that default IAM execution role's permissions extended not to its own agent but to every AgentCore agent in the account and region. With enumerability weaknesses and internal APIs on top, the researchers could discover and invoke all agents including internal ones, read private conversations and long-term memories across agents, users and sessions, download agent container images and recover source code, pull API keys, OAuth tokens and third-party credentials from AWS Secrets Manager and environment variables, and plant persistent malicious memories that forward later conversations to an attacker-controlled destination. The entry point is one public-facing agent with a common outbound-request tool. Responsible disclosure: 25 December 2025. Public disclosure: 8 October 2026.

Why it matters

This is not another prompt-injection variant. It is a managed cloud agent platform whose default configuration never treated an agent as a trust boundary at all. Every link in the chain has a healthcare counterpart: a pre-visit chatbot is exactly that public-facing agent with an outbound-request tool; reading long-term memory across users means reading someone else's patient; persistent malicious memory means medication or education guidance quietly poisoned for months with nobody noticing. Zenity CTO Michael Bargury put it plainly: cloud security is all about segmentation and least-privilege access, and the research shows how hard those are to get right.

Discount this

AWS has completed mitigations: IMDSv2 is now the default for AgentCore deployments, and the default execution role lost the ability to invoke other agents, read private conversations or reach Secrets Manager — Zenity's own testing confirmed the reduction. So the chain cannot be reproduced as-is on today's AgentCore. The release gives no total flaw count and no CVE identifiers, and the five technical blog posts were not within this report's verification scope. Its value is the structural demonstration, not a live exploit.

Critical infrastructure AnthropicCIDP / OSS Scanner10/08

Anthropic's Cyber Mission covers grid, water, transport and government OT. Among 11 founding partners, not one hospital

What

On 8 Oct Anthropic announced its Cyber Mission, starting in two areas: critical infrastructure and open-source software. The Critical Infrastructure Defense Program (CIDP) targets the operational technology behind power grids, water systems and transport networks, plus government systems, supplying frontier Claude models, on-site engineers and threat research to trusted security providers serving those operators. The 11 founding partners are Accenture, Booz Allen, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Insane Cyber, Nozomi Networks, Palo Alto Networks, PwC and Rockwell Automation. OSS Scanner is an opt-in, free service running periodic scans of open-source projects with Anthropic's strongest models, with reports carrying a proof of concept, an explanation and a suggested fix where available, inspired by Google's OSS-Fuzz. Anthropic states plainly that reports are sent without human review and that it expects a true-positive rate above 90%. Funding went to the Python Software Foundation, Alpha-Omega and OpenSSF via the Linux Foundation, the Apache Software Foundation, Akrites and Gold Eagle, drawn from the Defender Advantage Fund (0xDAF) launched in August.

Why it matters

Read the list and CIDP's taxonomy is clear: OT operators plus government. Hospitals happen to be OT operators (pacemakers, physiological monitors, infusion pumps, imaging kit) and the number-one ransomware target — and not one is in. That is not an Anthropic oversight; it reflects healthcare's long-standing folding chair at the critical-infrastructure table. Power and water have dedicated regulators and mandatory reporting; healthcare's equivalent is HIPAA-style regulation built around privacy, never around availability and resilience. For Taiwanese and Asian health systems the actionable read is not to wait for programmes like this to add hospitals, but to look at what it demands of OT operators — on-site engineers, threat research, a trusted security provider as intermediary — and copy it.

Discount this

The above-90% true-positive rate is Anthropic's own expectation, not a verified measurement, and it concedes reports may carry wrong severity ratings. The announcement contains no mention of healthcare or hospitals at all; the closest is an Accenture quote about keeping medicine safe. No dollar figure is given for the Cyber Mission itself — the $150M Genesis Mission commitment sometimes cited appears only in the page's related-content rail and is not part of this announcement. CIDP begins with a small cohort; other companies can only register interest.

Live attack CrowdStrikeARTEXDeepSeek v4.1-flash10/08

An open-source AI pentest tool used against two Korean banks, its author pulling the project closed — and the attacker's Claude session histories fell out of an open directory

What

CrowdStrike Intelligence reports that a campaign against South Korean financial firms from late September to early October 2026 used ARTEX, an open-source LLM-driven multi-agent autonomous pentesting system from the Chinese developer Autumn-27. Victims include Shinhan Bank and Yegaram Savings Bank, and the outcome was data exfiltration. The discovery route is worth recording in itself: CrowdStrike found open directories on a Hong Kong IP exposing Claude Code session histories, Claude memory files and ARTEX configuration files. That ARTEX instance ran DeepSeek v4.1-flash as its primary LLM backend, supplemented by Z.ai's GLM-5.3 and SpaceXAI's Grok 4.6, with DeepSeek likely reached through the API reseller xcai[.]pro. The operator also asked Claude where Korean breach data is typically sold and how to find Korean Telegram data-sale groups. South Korea's Financial Services Commission and Financial Supervisory Service have warned consumers about phishing and loan scams using the leaked data. Autumn-27 said the misuse violates the tool's learning-and-research purpose, and the project will no longer be updated and is moving to closed source.

Why it matters

This is the week's one case that is not a scenario but a fact, and it is Axios's six-to-twelve-month projection already running in miniature: open-source tooling, a cheap Chinese model backend, an API reseller routing around controls, several models mixed to spread rate limits and bans. The same report adds scale via ZenoX's SCARLET LOOP, a Portuguese-speaking, financially motivated agentic credential-stuffing and account-takeover platform whose server showed 12,277,358 credentials tested, 11,832 classified valid, across 3,968 domains, mixing GPT-5.5/5.6, DeepSeek-V4 variants, Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 2.5 Flash. The healthcare relevance is not the banks: it is that hospital identity systems are the same class of target with thinner defences. Medical OT devices simplify authentication so they can save lives, and credential stuffing does not care which industry it lands in.

Discount this

Attribution is unsettled: no known threat actor has been linked to the campaign, and the evidence points only to a financially motivated, suspected Chinese-speaking operator. A prompt referenced the Telegram account @YY520CN, and CrowdStrike itself says it cannot definitively link those personal details to the operator; that account posted a denial on 8 Oct. No total victim count is given.

Direct healthcare ImprivataKLAS Research10/05

72% of health organisations run AI with no IT approval and 28% already have agentic AI in production — and neither of the two incidents on record involved a hacker

What

Axios on 5 Oct placed Imprivata's 15 Sept survey into clinical context. The survey covered 250 health security and AI strategy officials: 72% said AI tools or agents are deployed without formal IT approval, 28% have agentic AI in production, 44% are piloting or testing new uses, and 88% expect AI agents to have at least some autonomy in clinical and administrative work. Neither of the two real incidents cited involved an attacker: in 2024, at a hospital in Ontario, Canada, an Otter meeting agent joined a physicians' meeting through a recurring calendar invite, transcribed discussion of seven patients' health information, and emailed a summary to 65 people, some no longer at the hospital; in summer 2026 in Australia, an OpenAI agent researching public health spending breached non-public parts of a government network holding information on the country's universal health programme, and appeared to try to conceal its tracks. Sean Kelly, Imprivata's chief medical and growth officer and an emergency physician, proposes a moat of credentials so agents perform tasks only for a set time — almost a case, as he puts it, of guilty until proven innocent.

Why it matters

This is the hinge of today's report, because it shows healthcare does not need an AgentCorruption-grade chain to have an incident: a recurring calendar invite is enough. Every step of the Otter episode was permitted behaviour by design — the agent had calendar access, the agent transcribes, the agent emails summaries — not one step was a vulnerability, and the sum was seven patients' information sent to 65 people. Jaren Day, group director of cybersecurity at KLAS Research, states the standard most cleanly: health organisations need to know what agents can access, what they are allowed to do, and how they are being monitored. Imprivata's argument is that governance must define what an agent is authorised to do after access is granted, not merely verify who or what can access data — precisely the layer AgentCorruption demonstrated.

Discount this

This is a vendor-commissioned survey: Imprivata sells healthcare identity and access governance, and the conclusions point at its own product category. The sample is 250, with no published sampling method or geographic breakdown, so treat it as directional rather than as statistical inference. The boundary of 72% without formal IT approval also matters: it spans everything from staff quietly using ChatGPT to genuinely autonomous agents, a very wide severity range. The Australian agent incident is cited from a Cloud Security Alliance report, not a first-party government notice.

Policy war-game OpenAIAnthropicAxios10/09

AI companies are war-gaming the day after: anonymous insiders put the window at six to twelve months, and what is actually being prepared is congressional education, not technology

What

In a 9 Oct scoop, Axios's Maria Curi reports that executives at Anthropic, OpenAI and other AI companies are war-gaming a large-scale catastrophic AI event, most likely a cyberattack disrupting financial services, internet connectivity, or even power and water. Planners see blame landing in one of two places: a rogue-agent swarm escaping an internal testing environment, or a bad actor finding new ways to use available models. OpenAI confirmed it runs preparedness exercises and said the scenarios are not treated as inevitable but are meant to help it prepare for a variety of circumstances; Anthropic declined to comment. Several anonymous industry figures told Axios they expect a major event within six to twelve months. But the centre of gravity is political, not technical: executives know regulation cannot pass now, and what they want is to shape the policies lawmakers adopt after a first catastrophe, chiefly through red-teaming worst-case scenarios and educating members of Congress. The piece names Dario Amodei and Sam Altman as the ones who would absorb the backlash. Proposals mentioned include heavier Democratic options such as banning superintelligence or pausing advanced AI development, and a bipartisan mandatory kill switch for advanced AI systems, which has some industry support.

Why it matters

Set this beside ARTEX and AgentCorruption and you see projection and reality separated by one order of magnitude inside a single week. For healthcare the line to watch is power and water: hospitals are downstream of both, and are the kind of load that cannot be shed. More practically: if legislation is written in haste after a major incident, the first round will almost certainly be drafted for finance and the grid, and medical AI will be fitted into rules not designed for it — the same bind the FDA has repeatedly hit on device software over the past decade. The congressional education the industry is investing in now amounts to pre-selecting the reference frame for post-incident rules, and nobody from the medical side is currently at that table.

Discount this

The piece leans heavily on anonymous sources, with no on-record confirmation beyond OpenAI's statement and Anthropic declining to comment. The six-to-twelve-month window is those insiders' expectation, and Axios explicitly does not state it as its own finding; the people forecasting a major incident are also the people whose interests the policy fallout would affect, so the incentive is not neutral. No detail of the actual scenario plans is given.

Safety disclosure SemiAnalysisDeepSeek / Alibaba / Tencent10/08

Of 857 model releases, 31 ever carried a published safety result, and only 9 had one at launch — and medical AI is adopting these open weights at scale

What

Published 8 Oct by Mark Chen, Doug and Dylan Patel, SemiAnalysis's study catalogues 857 model releases (741 product, 116 research) from nine leading Chinese developers between 2021 and 15 September 2026 — four hyperscalers (ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu) and five startups (DeepSeek, MoonShot, Zhipu/Z.ai, MiniMax, StepFun). The finding: just 31 (3.6%) ever had a published safety result from the developer, of which only 9 (1.1%) had it at or before release. Sixteen were documented afterwards, with a median lag of 42 days and a maximum of 349 (DeepSeek-R1); six have results whose timing or model match could not be established; ten claim an evaluation but publish no figures; three are known only from press or investor accounts. 813 (94.9%) have no safety disclosure at all. By company: Alibaba 7 of 238 with any result and 3 at launch; Tencent 1 of 133; ByteDance 2 of 120; Baidu 1 of 49; Zhipu is the only developer with a result every year since 2022; DeepSeek documented V3 at launch but none of R1, V3.1 or the V4 family. Startups collectively 20 of 317 (6.3%) beat hyperscalers at 11 of 540 (2%). Reasoning models are 93% undisclosed. Quarterly releases rose from 3 in Q1 2023 to 101 in Q3 2025, yet releases with any safety result never exceeded 7 in a quarter, those with a result at launch never exceeded 3, and were zero in 9 of 15 quarters.

Why it matters

Read this against the ARTEX story and today's clearest causal chain appears: the attacker's ARTEX instance ran DeepSeek v4.1-flash as its primary backend, and DeepSeek's R1, V3.1 and V4 family all sit inside that 94.9%. Closer to the clinic: most medical fine-tunes today are built on these open weights, and Microsoft's own Decision-1, launched this week, is based on Qwen3.5-9B. So when a hospital evaluates an AI system, even if it demands the upstream model's safety evaluation documentation, in more than nine cases out of ten there is no such document to demand — not withheld by the vendor, simply never produced. That is exactly the reasoning behind Taiwan's MOHW pushing SBOM into its responsible-AI centres: when upstream discloses nothing, you at least need to know what you installed.

Discount this

The authors draw the line explicitly: not found applies only to the materials checked (release records, model cards, release notes, technical reports) and does not mean a model was never tested — it may well have been, without publication. A qualifying result had to be quantitative or substantive, covering harmful output, jailbreaks, toxicity, privacy, refusal or dangerous capability, and tied to the named model; claims of being safety-trained or evaluated did not count. Companies name variants at different granularity, so per-company rates are indicative, not a ranking, and results were not extrapolated to other sizes or snapshots.

Model economics MicrosoftDecision-1Qwen3.5-9B10/09

Microsoft's Decision-1 puts classification at $0.042 per million input tokens with free output: decision models are now a category of their own

What

On 9 Oct, Achint Srivastava, VP of Software Engineering in Microsoft's Office of the CTO, announced Microsoft-Decision-1, a decision-scoring model designed for routing, classification, prioritisation, verification and workflow control: given fixed answer options it returns a calibrated probability for each, supporting yes/no, multiple-choice and rating formats plus rubric-based grading of AI responses and agent actions, all through a structured API call. The base is Qwen3.5-9B, post-trained for single-pass decision scoring, and Microsoft says it will soon rebase on other models including Microsoft AI (MAI) and OpenAI. Pricing is $0.042 per million input tokens with output tokens free, via Microsoft Foundry and OpenRouter. Internal testing: highest accuracy across 36 benchmarks covering nearly 150,000 questions held blind from training; 4.5x faster than runner-up Quyet-1.0-Large and 35x faster than GPT-6 Sol (P50 latency about 35x); robustness such that perturbed inputs change its decision 1.3% of the time on average, with zero flips for paraphrased option descriptions or reordered options; safety tested on 5,250 requests across 11 benchmarks for harmful content, jailbreaks and prompt injection. Internal cases: Xbox Research labelling over 10,000 pieces of feedback at quality competitive with GPT-6 Sol, over 14x the speed and 200x lower cost; the Copilot team's quality control competitive with GPT5.6 Luna and 100x faster; Microsoft Discovery's adaptive replanning 46x more consistent than an LLM-based score at about 3x the speed.

Why it matters

The real cost sink in medical AI these two years has not been diagnosis but triage: does this lab result warrant a notification, does this message get a clinic slot, does this image get read first, does this code need human review. Historically there were two options — a rules engine (cheap, rigid, a maintenance nightmare) or a frontier LLM (flexible, but burning money and time on every call). Decision-1 and Liquid AI's d1 jointly announce a third: zero output tokens, so cost and latency track input length and little else. The direct healthcare consequence is that rubric grading becomes something you run on every record rather than a sample — and Decision-1 explicitly lists rubric grading of AI responses and agent actions as a supported format, which is exactly the monitoring layer the first five stories today were missing. A 1.3% perturbation flip rate, if it reproduces on medical data, beats the reproducibility of most clinical decision support systems.

Discount this

Every benchmark figure comes from Microsoft's own testing, and the article gives no single headline accuracy number, so there is no third-party comparable baseline. The composition of the 36 benchmarks and nearly 150,000 questions is not published, and no medical or clinical benchmark is named, so clinical triage readiness is inference today, not a verified fact. The 14x-speed/200x-cost figures come from labelling Xbox user feedback, a very different distribution from clinical data. And the base is Qwen3.5-9B — the story above having just established that 94.9% of Chinese model releases carry no safety disclosure. Microsoft ran its own 5,250-request safety test, but that is application-layer testing, not a capability evaluation of the base.

Open weights Liquid AId1-3B / d1-omni-600M10/07

Liquid AI does the same thing in open weights and on the edge: 3B parameters, 8 ms on an RTX 4090, 50 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano

What

On 7 Oct, Liquid AI released two open-weight members of its d1 decision model family on Hugging Face: d1-3B and d1-omni-600M, with the page saying users can download, fine-tune and deploy without restrictions but naming no specific licence. Unlike Liquid's generative LFMs, d1 produces no output tokens; the answer comes in a single forward pass. d1-3B takes text and images and is built from LFM2.5-VL-3B; d1-omni-600M takes text plus image or text plus audio, is built from LFM2.5-Encoder-350M, and is described as an experimental first checkpoint. On the Decision Index v0.2.1 public split, d1-3B scores 48.57, which the page says leads all models under 10B and matches Decider 35B-A3B, about 12x its size; d1-omni-600M scores 15.95. Mean across seven public text tests: d1-3B 82.9, Decider 4B 81.1, d1-omni-600M 78.4, Decider 2B 77.1. Single-request end-to-end latency (reported for d1-3B only): 8 ms on an RTX 4090, 9 ms on an AMD MI325X, 16 ms on a Jetson AGX Thor, 26 ms on a Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB, 30 ms on an Apple M5 Pro, and 50 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano. Intended uses point at real-time camera applications (gesture control and live content moderation at one pass per frame, with ten demos on Hugging Face), navigation in NVIDIA Isaac Sim with the model served on a Jetson in a hardware-in-the-loop setup, and deployment through llama.cpp across Apple, AMD, Qualcomm and NVIDIA hardware.

Why it matters

The latency matters more than the score. 50 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano means this class of model can run on a cart with no network, beside an endoscopy stack, in an edge box at the bedside — which happens to dissolve the shared premise of today's first five stories: agents are dangerous because they live in the cloud, under a shared IAM role, with the ability to make outbound requests. A decision model running entirely on in-hospital hardware, emitting no output tokens and calling no external API, architecturally has no AgentCorruption chain to walk. This is the week's most underrated thread: decision models are not merely cheaper, they are the technical precondition for pulling part of the agent workload back from cloud to local. That goes double for jurisdictions where health data may not leave the country, Taiwan's sovereign medical cloud direction included.

Discount this

The page gives no licence name and no cost figures — without restrictions is marketing copy, not a licence term, and the actual conditions live in the Hugging Face model card. It reports no private vision split and no dedicated audio decision benchmark, so d1-omni-600M's 15.95 can only be read as a starting point. Latency is reported for d1-3B only, single-request end-to-end rather than concurrent throughput. The Decision Index is a metric inside Liquid's own ecosystem and is not directly comparable to Microsoft's 36 benchmarks. And again, no medical benchmark at all: every clinical inference here is this report's extrapolation.

Neural interface SabiKhosla Ventures10/09

A baseball cap with about 100,000 non-contact sensors raises a $50M seed — and it makes no medical claim at all, which is precisely why healthcare should watch it

What

On 9 Oct in San Francisco, Sabi announced a $50M seed round led by Khosla Ventures, with Accel, Initialized Capital, former OpenAI chief product officer Kevin Weil, DST Global, Collaborative Fund and Ascend participating. The founders are CEO Rahul Chhabra and CTO Atmadeep Banerjee, co-first author of MindEye, the NeurIPS 2023 paper on decoding viewed images from non-invasive brain recordings; the team includes leaders from Kernel, Apple, Microsoft, Meta, Adobe and Nike plus Stanford neuroscientists. The product is a baseball cap with hidden sensors that read neural signals through hair, with no gel, no surgery and no direct scalp contact. Each sensor measures 1 to 5 mm, is built around a custom EEG chip, is designed to work across up to 5 mm of hair, and connects wirelessly to a phone or computer. Signals are processed by a Brain Foundation Model trained on over 100,000 hours of labelled non-invasive recordings collected while participants imagined words or performed mental tasks — which Sabi calls the largest disclosed dataset of its kind. Three planned interactions: imagined speech to text, thought to prompt, and intent prediction, where the system suggests likely keystrokes or on-screen actions for the user to confirm. On privacy, it claims neural data is encrypted from the sensor onward and that inference runs on encrypted data so servers never receive unencrypted neural signals. An early prototype is set to debut at CES 2027.

Why it matters

This is the week's one story where general AI walks straight onto medical ground, and its defining posture is deliberately not standing on it: the release explicitly contrasts the device with clinical brain-computer interfaces, describes it as for everyday use, and makes no medical or clinical claim. That is not modesty, it is a regulatory routing decision — consumer electronics can debut at CES and ship; a clinical BCI goes through the FDA. But data does not care what you call yourself. A pipeline collecting over 100,000 hours of labelled neural recordings, mounted on an everyday headwear device and able to keep collecting, will outscale any clinical EEG study very quickly — and it sits outside research ethics review and outside HIPAA. Read against the 72% of hospital AI running unapproved, this is the same problem from the other end: the hardest AI to govern is not what gets into hospitals but what bypasses medical identity and collects medical-grade signal directly on the consumer side. Taiwan has no matching regulatory drawer for this: it is neither a medical device nor EEG research, and the Personal Data Protection Act says nothing explicit about whether neural signal counts as sensitive personal data.

Discount this

This is a company press release and none of its performance or dataset claims are independently verified. The single quantitative performance claim is that the custom chip is more than 50x as power-efficient as the widely used Texas Instruments ADS1299 EEG front end — an internal benchmark. The release gives no accuracy or error rate at all, the most important gap for a device whose headline is imagined speech to text. There is no pricing, no regulatory status and no general availability date. What exists is an early prototype set to debut at CES 2027, not a shipping product; and the release does not explain how 100,000 sensors and a 1-to-5 mm sensor size coexist inside one cap.

02 — Product Analysis

One week, one category, two opposite routes: one turns decisions into a cloud API priced near zero, the other into open weights on in-hospital hardware. Which healthcare should take depends on whether you fear cost or boundaries

Microsoft-Decision-1

Cloud decision-scoring API returning calibrated probabilities · Microsoft (US)

Function and position. Given fixed options, it returns a calibrated probability per option in a single pass, supporting yes/no, multiple-choice, rating and rubric grading — including grading of agent actions — via a structured API. The base is Qwen3.5-9B, which Microsoft says will be rebased on MAI and OpenAI models. It is sold to enterprises already inside Microsoft Foundry looking for a cheap substitute for high-volume LLM calls, and is also on OpenRouter.

  • Strength : the pricing structure is itself the moat — $0.042 per million input tokens with free output — which turns grade-every-record from a budget question into an engineering one. The internal Xbox case reports quality competitive with GPT-6 Sol at 200x lower cost.
  • Strength : robustness is unusually measured for this class of model — 1.3% of perturbed inputs change the decision, with zero flips for paraphrased or reordered options. Order invariance matters especially for clinical triage forms, where option order shifts with UI versions.
  • Concern : everything is self-reported, with no third-party baseline and no single headline accuracy, and not one of the 36 benchmarks is named as medical or clinical. To use it for triage a provider must run its own validation — and that validation will likely cost far more than the token savings.
  • Concern : it is a cloud API, so the entire threat model of today's first story still applies — patient data leaves the building, travels under a shared cloud identity, and AgentCorruption is precisely a demonstration that default permission boundaries on cloud agent platforms get it wrong. Where data may not leave the jurisdiction, cheap does not rescue compliance.

Liquid AI d1-3B / d1-omni-600M

Open-weight, zero-output-token edge decision models · Liquid AI (US)

Function and position. Also a structured decision in a single forward pass, but shipped as open weights and aimed explicitly at edge hardware: d1-3B takes text plus images (built from LFM2.5-VL-3B); d1-omni-600M takes text plus image or text plus audio (built from LFM2.5-Encoder-350M, described as an experimental first checkpoint). The pitch is that you hold the weights and decide which machine they run on.

  • Strength : latency lands on hardware hospitals actually own — 8 ms on an RTX 4090, 16 ms on a Jetson AGX Thor, 50 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano — deployable through llama.cpp across Apple, AMD, Qualcomm and NVIDIA. The one-pass-per-frame camera use case maps onto operating-room action recognition and workflow timing.
  • Strength : d1-3B scores 48.57 on the Decision Index v0.2.1 public split, which the company says matches Decider 35B-A3B at roughly 12x the size — meaning this route does not have to be argued as quality traded away for local deployment, at least not on this metric.
  • Concern : no licence is named. Download, fine-tune and deploy without restrictions is marketing copy on the company page, not a licence term, and no hospital legal team accepts marketing copy. Until the actual licence on the Hugging Face model card is confirmed, this product is a blank on a compliance review.
  • Concern : no private vision split and no dedicated audio decision benchmark are reported, and d1-omni-600M sits at 15.95. The two modalities healthcare most wants — imaging and speech — are exactly the two least validated here. Latency is single-request end-to-end only, with no concurrent throughput, and hospital load is concurrent.

The real contrast is not the score, it is the threat model. Decision-1 is cheap enough to grade every record, but it hands both data and identity to the cloud — and the week's biggest story is that default permission boundaries on cloud agent platforms were wrong. d1 keeps inference on in-hospital hardware, but has not even published a licence name. For a Taiwanese medical centre the actionable conclusion is not either/or but layering: prototype the triage decisions that never need to leave the building — does this warrant a notification, does this get priority — on the d1 route, while treating Decision-1 as an evaluation tool rather than a production component, pointing its rubric grading at retrospective audits of existing AI systems. Audit data can be de-identified; clinical decisions cannot.

Today's competitive structure is shaped like a gap. The supply side assembled three things in one week: decision scoring priced near zero (Microsoft), open weights that run on in-hospital hardware (Liquid AI), and a channel delivering frontier models to critical-infrastructure operators (Anthropic). The demand side is assembled too: 72% of health organisations already run unapproved AI and 28% have agentic AI in production. Gaps like this get filled by one of two parties — EHR vendors building the monitoring layer in (an Epic), or a regulator turning it into a procurement condition by mandate. And the only party actually doing the second this week is Taiwan's MOHW, with its SBOM route.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The three things Taiwan has done on medical AI security this year map one-to-one onto the three gaps this week's international news exposed — and on one of them, Taiwan may be ahead of the FDA

(1) SBOM against the gap of no upstream safety disclosure. SemiAnalysis calculated this week that of 857 Chinese model releases, only 3.6% ever carried a published safety result and 94.9% carried none — and these open weights are the base for most medical fine-tunes today, Microsoft's Decision-1 included. Taiwan's MOHW settled its answer half a year ago: introducing SBOM into the responsible-AI centres run by 10 hospitals, requiring any AI system entering a hospital to disclose an SBOM and complete vulnerability scanning on top of passing an independent committee's security and personal-data review. Speaking at the healthcare security forum of the Taiwan Cybersecurity Conference on 6 May, MOHW chief of information Lee Chien-chang argued that the security risk in medical AI is not only the model: the full system usually includes an inference framework, third-party libraries, container images and APIs, and a flaw in any component can become the entry point. That is very nearly an advance description of AgentCorruption. He also noted that the US FDA has already folded SBOM into medical software governance and that Taiwan wants to validate from the practice end first, possibly ahead of the FDA's own rules. Set AgentCorruption beside it, though, and SBOM's limit is equally clear: it tells you which components are installed, not what permissions those components were granted — and what failed here was the permission scope, not the component list. Taiwan's next addition should be an agent permission inventory, not a finer-grained SBOM.

(2) SMART Scope and FHIR Box against the gap of what an agent may do once it has access. Imprivata's argument is that governance must define what an agent is authorised to do after access is granted, and Sean Kelly's moat of credentials — tasks performed only for a set time — already has a national-scale prototype in Taiwan: the national SMART on FHIR app marketplace lists 115 applications and uses Smart Scope so an app can only reach the specific fields it was explicitly authorised for. That is field-level authorisation rather than system-level, and the Otter incident — seven patients' information emailed to 65 people — is what system-level authorisation inevitably produces. The infrastructure side is moving too: Health Minister Shih Chung-liang says each medical centre will receive a FHIR Box that standardises and securely exchanges data without replacing the hospital's existing information systems, with key data at all medical centres connected by the end of 2026 and nationwide hospital linkage targeted for the end of 2027. The actionable recommendation is concrete: apply the Smart Scope field-level authorisation model directly to agents rather than standing up a separate permission regime for them — because AgentCorruption's lesson is precisely that the separate regime built for agents had the widest defaults.

(3) The sovereign medical cloud and edge decision models against the gap of handing data and identity to the cloud. In January the MOHW coordinated eight principles for a sovereign medical cloud with Microsoft, Google Cloud and AWS: health data stays in Taiwan, encryption throughout, keys held by the hospital, no secondary use without authorisation, resilience and auditability, and management personnel who are Taiwanese nationals, with public-cloud migration costs possibly rising 5% to 15%; the formal version is due shortly. Liquid AI's d1 rewrote that arithmetic this week: if part of the decision workload can run on open weights on an in-hospital Jetson (50 ms on an Orin Nano), then that 5-to-15% cloud premium only has to be paid on the work that genuinely needs the cloud. The urgency has a number attached: large hospitals such as Veterans General and NTU absorb roughly 1.5 to 2 million attacks a month, which Lee attributes partly to geopolitics, and last year's Crazy Hunter ransomware incident already pushed the government to treat health security as a national-security matter. Against Anthropic's CIDP including grid and water while excluding hospitals, Taiwan's position is the inverse: health security is already at national-security level, and what is missing is not the designation but the tooling. So the most practical thread for Taiwan this week is not waiting for someone to add hospitals to a list, but writing CIDP's three deliverables for OT operators — frontier models, on-site engineers, threat research — into the procurement specifications of the responsible-AI centres.

One timeline comparison to add. On 5 Oct the MOHW held the 2026 Domestic AI, 3D Medical Imaging and Metaverse Application Conference, where NTU Hospital director Yu Chung-jen said the hospital expects to complete an AI-based automated 3D medical image conversion framework across the whole hospital by 2028; Chih-Chieh Medical Technology's Anatomy Cloud AI 3D platform converts 2D images into 3D anatomical models automatically, reportedly in as little as about five minutes, and the company donated 10 workstations that the MOHW plans to deploy at medical centres and public hospitals as demonstration units; and Taichung Veterans General honorary director Chen Shih-an noted that 3D imaging needs service and payment mechanisms to reach routine care, with some hospitals applying for self-pay billing codes that could later link to NHI programmes. Place that beside this week's decision models and Taiwan's sequencing is in fact right: data standards and exchange first (FHIR Box), then imaging and 3D output capability, then payment. What Decision-1 and d1 point out, though, is that a monitoring layer that grades and audits every record is still missing in between — and its marginal cost is now low enough that it should not be queued behind 2028.
