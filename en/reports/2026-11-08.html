Seven days, one question asked seven ways: who certifies that it works

Sunday, November 8, 2026 · Covering Nov 2–8 · Taipei time

Lay the week flat and the unrelated stories turn out to be standing over the same hole. On November 5 METR published its privileged-access assessment of Anthropic: as of October 2026, the rate of capability gain on well-specified tasks is about 2x the March 2024–October 2025 baseline (1.9x on the AECI measure, 95% CI 1.5–2.8x), METR attributes roughly 80% of it to AI itself, and it finds no credible bottleneck in algorithms, training compute, experiment compute or data over the next 12 months — while Anthropic redacted most of what METR called the decisive evidence. The same day the Wall Street Journal, citing three large investors planning to take part in the IPO, reported the $1.5T valuation may come down toward $1T; the S&P 500 fell 4.4% and the Nasdaq 100 6.3% that day, both the worst since April 2025, with the Philadelphia semiconductor index down 12.3% and NVIDIA down 9.2%. And the amended S-1 filed November 2 put the books on the table: $17.0B of Q3 revenue against $1.4B a year earlier, a $104.0B nine-month net loss of which $94.3B is a non-cash charge tied to convertible notes, and about $895.4B in signed commitments, $69.8B of it falling due within 12 months of September 30, 2026. On the evidence side the same week returned three readings that fight each other: Doximity's audit of 261,508 doses found at least 2.3% wrong and roughly 20 answers per 1,000 carrying a severe dosing error; Stony Brook's 60 stays of 7–21 days had physicians preferring the AI-written discharge summary in 95% of cases; and ONC's HTI-5, finalized November 3, moved the other way and deleted source-attribute disclosure for predictive AI. Only one genuinely new primary document this week addresses the hole itself: Google's multi-method evaluation framework for health AI, published November 4 (arXiv:2611.05127).

Story of the week METRAnthropic11/05

METR: the rate of capability gain is about 2x its level 18 months ago, roughly 80% of it AI-driven — and the decisive evidence is redacted

What

On November 5 METR published its privileged-access assessment of AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic: as of October 2026 the rate of capability gain on well-specified tasks is about 2x the March 2024–October 2025 baseline, 1.9x on the AECI measure (95% CI 1.5–2.8x), with the annualized trend rising from +15 to +29 points per year and the breakpoint placed at October 7, 2025. METR estimates about 80% of that acceleration comes from AI itself, and says it can find no credible bottleneck over the next 12 months across algorithms, training compute, experiment compute or data. Anthropic redacted most of what METR identified as the decisive evidence.

Why it matters

Validation in medicine runs on a scale of years: prospective trials, IRB review, multi-site external validation, FDA clearance. If the generational turnover of models has genuinely doubled, the model that finished validation and the model actually in use come apart permanently — which is not an implementation problem but a mismatch of institutional time constants. Every document this week on liability, payment and certification implicitly assumes a model can be frozen long enough to be inspected.

Discount this

The "about 80% from AI" figure is METR's estimate, not a measurement, and the 1.5–2.8x confidence interval is itself wide; the supporting evidence METR called decisive was redacted by the party being assessed, so the report's auditability falls short of the strength of its conclusion.

Regulation ONCAMACMS11/03–11/06

The same week Washington loosened one hand and raised the price with the other: HTI-5 deleted 14 certification criteria, the AMA pushed the duty of care onto hospitals, and CMS's 2027 OPPS final rule landed on November 6

What

On November 3 HHS finalized ONC's HTI-5 deregulatory rule: of 60 certification criteria, 14 are removed and 7 revised, among them source-attribute disclosure for predictive AI; HHS estimates savings above $1.5B, effective December 28. The same day the AMA's clinical-AI liability framework stated plainly that warnings and disclaimers are "not enough," that a hospital's duty of care covers selection, validation, training and ongoing monitoring, and that developers and deployers share it. Senator Cassidy's PATIENT AI Act discussion draft would have HHS build a Medicare coverage framework, payment methods and billing codes for AI services and have CMMI test an autonomous-AI payment model, with comments due December 1. On November 6 CMS published CMS-1850-F, the 2027 OPPS final rule, tying 340B drug payment to acquisition cost and expanding site-neutral payment for imaging.

Why it matters

Stack the three documents and the direction is unmistakable: the federal government no longer promises that certified AI is safe, yet the AMA and tort law still require someone to answer for it. The difference lands on the hospital's AI governance committee — the party most directly exposed to story one, where validation cannot keep pace with the models. Meanwhile the OPPS expansion of site-neutral payment thins the marginal revenue on outpatient imaging, so hospitals absorb a new monitoring duty in the same year their revenue narrows.

Safety Doximity261,508 doses10/29

The week's number that should stop a hospital: nine 2026 models, 2,204 drugs, 261,508 doses, at least 2.3% wrong

What

Doximity's dosing audit, published October 29: nine 2026-vintage models, 2,204 drugs, 261,508 doses, at least 2.3% wrong; roughly 20 answers per 1,000 carry a severe dosing error and about 30.5 fall in NCC MERP categories E–H; of the errors caught, 43.8% were severe and 3.4% potentially lethal; drugs approved after 2024 carried 5.1x the odds of an error (95% CI 3.9–6.5).

Why it matters

The audit points the opposite way from this week's Stony Brook discharge-summary study, and both are right: an LLM beats a tired resident at rewriting information it has been given, and misses systematically when it has to recall a precise number — the newer the drug, the worse, which is a direct consequence of how training data is distributed in time. A hospital's deployment question is therefore not whether the model is good enough but whether the task is rewriting or recall.

Discount this

This is research published by Doximity itself, and in the same week its earnings called AI Search an "eight-figure bookings product" — the audit's finding favors the sourced-and-grounded positioning it sells, so treat it as an interested party's own account.

New this week Google for HealtharXiv:2611.0512711/04

Google publishes a multi-method framework for evaluating health AI: three methods stacked, with thresholds set by clinical risk rather than a universal cutoff

What

On November 4 Sarah Montgomery-Taylor, clinical evaluation lead at Google for Health, published a framework for evaluating general-purpose generative AI in health (whitepaper at arXiv:2611.05127, with Elizabeth McGlynn and Dana Gelb Safran). It stacks three methods: closed-question benchmarks such as multiple choice serve only as a baseline, AI autoraters score open-ended answers against written rubrics, and expert human review including red-teaming handles complex cases and safety boundaries. Procedurally it requires mapping a "Critical User Journey" first — who uses the system, what they are trying to achieve, which steps they pass through, and how it is likely to be misused — before test cases are chosen; each application is assessed on accuracy, safety (weighing both severity and likelihood of harm) and usefulness; passing thresholds are set gate by gate according to that application's clinical risk rather than a universal cutoff; and evaluation runs before release through shadow trials and clinical pilots and continues as post-deployment monitoring. The authors note two physicians can diverge by 30–40% or more on a rating task, so rater variability has to be controlled for.

Why it matters

Every other document this week reports a measurement; only this one asks how to measure. It takes on METR's time-constant problem head-on by breaking evaluation into continuing gates rather than a one-time certification, which in principle can run at the pace of model turnover. It also fills the gap ONC left: once the federal disclosure requirement is deleted, what a hospital needs is exactly an evaluation method it can write into a procurement contract. But it comes from a model vendor, not a regulator and not a third party — who audits the audit method is a question this week still leaves unanswered.

New this week Stony BrookPenn Medicine11/02

Two clinical readings where AI came out ahead: physicians preferred the AI discharge summary in 95% of 60 long stays, and Percival mined 400,000 CTs for diseases the scan was never ordered to find

What

On November 2 Stony Brook described its discharge-summary study, published in npj Digital Medicine: for 60 internal medicine stays of 7 to 21 days at its own hospital, LLM-generated discharge summaries were paired against human-authored ones and assessed by hospitalists and primary care physicians, who preferred the AI version in 95% of cases and scored it higher on overall quality, readability, accuracy and completeness, with fewer missing clinical details; primary care physicians found it easier to explain to patients and caregivers and more useful for follow-up. Both kinds of summary contained errors, but reviewers found no statistically significant difference in the estimated severity or likelihood of harm. Lead author Tyler Osborne is a computer science PhD candidate; Rachel Wong and Fusheng Wang are co-corresponding authors. At the other end, Becker's reported on October 30 on Penn Medicine's Percival, also in npj Digital Medicine: trained on more than 400,000 CT images from nearly 52,000 patients in the Penn Medicine BioBank, it runs in the background and surfaces signs of disease the scan was not ordered to look for, including atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease, outperforming two other AI imaging models across 279 conditions on chest CT and 230 on abdomen/pelvis CT, with the widest margins in lung cancer and acute lymphoid leukemia.

Why it matters

Together these two studies locate AI in medicine more precisely than any benchmark: its edge lies in recomposing information already in hand into something more readable (the discharge summary) and in opportunistic screening of data already collected but never read for that purpose (incidental findings on CT) — neither of which asks the model to know a fact it was not given. This week's Doximity dosing audit is the exact converse.

Discount this

Stony Brook is a single hospital, 60 cases, with the model unnamed, and the authors themselves stress that "physician review and verification remain essential"; Percival is still in preclinical testing, its broader findings have not been validated on data from outside Penn Medicine, the team hopes to begin clinical testing only in early 2027, and the Becker's write-up reports no accuracy metric at all.

02 — Product Analysis

The week's two most instructive new things sit at opposite ends of the spectrum: one pushes distribution back to before licensure, the other mines data deeper than the reason it was ordered for.

OpenEvidence Learn

Free medical education platform · OpenEvidence (US) · 2026-11-04

Function and position. Four modules: Test Prep (more than 50,000 board-style questions across USMLE, COMLEX and specialty and subspecialty exams), Preceptor (case-based Socratic tutoring through a simulated patient), Flashcards (generated from the question bank, missed questions or a chosen topic, reviewed at spaced intervals) and CME (credit for clinical questions the user has already asked inside OpenEvidence, with certificates held in one place). All four draw on the same peer-reviewed literature and guidelines behind its clinical answers. It is entirely free, requiring only registration; at launch Johns Hopkins School of Medicine and Penn's Perelman School of Medicine were named as the schools inviting trainees and attendings to test it and give feedback, per Unite.AI.

  • Strength : it pushes the distribution channel back to before licensure. A student who practiced clinical reasoning in Preceptor before Step 1 will not be comparing tools as an attending — the same habit moat UpToDate once built, except OpenEvidence has driven the cost of acquisition to zero. Its valuation case (a $250M raise at a $15B valuation) rested on the user base rather than revenue in the first place.
  • Strength : the CME design is shrewd — rather than asking anyone to take a course, it converts the lookup behavior physicians already perform into credit, raising retention and data quality at the same time.
  • Concern : the revenue comes from pharmaceutical advertising, and educational content is where recommendation bias is hardest to notice. The Nature Health analysis of 883,403 answers found five models' first recommendation converging on a single brand name 97.5% of the time, a dominant product above 90% share in 96 of 123 scenarios, and branded drugs recommended at 20.5x the odds of generics. In a point-of-care answer that is a clinical problem; in a resident's study material it becomes a generation's prescribing habits. The Learn announcement does not describe how advertising and educational content are separated.
  • Concern : there is no published audit of Test Prep's answer accuracy, no statement that Preceptor's clinical reasoning was validated by medical educators, and no description of an error-reporting channel. Measured against the three layers Google's framework asks for this same week, the public material on Learn does not report even the first of them.

Percival（Penn Medicine）

Opportunistic CT screening model · Penn Medicine (US) · published in npj Digital Medicine

Function and position. Trained on more than 400,000 CT images from nearly 52,000 patients in the Penn Medicine BioBank (which holds data on over 350,000 people gathered across two decades), it runs in the background and extracts, from scans already completed, signs of disease and future risk the scan was never ordered to look for — including atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease, which CT does not normally diagnose. Senior author Walter Witschey is an associate professor of radiology and associate vice chair of research IT at Penn; co-author Charles Kahn is a professor of radiology, per Becker's.

  • Strength : the marginal cost is close to zero. The patient has already been through the scanner, the dose has already been given, the images already exist — Percival needs only compute. In the same year that site-neutral payment for outpatient imaging expands (CMS-1850-F, published November 6) and revenue per scan thins, extracting more information from the same image is one of the few value propositions that stands up without a new payment code.
  • Strength : the coverage is genuinely broad — 279 conditions on chest CT and 230 on abdomen/pelvis CT, and it beat two other AI imaging models across that breadth, most widely on lung cancer and acute lymphoid leukemia. Most imaging AI is a single-indication point solution; Percival takes the foundation-model route.
  • Concern : there are no accuracy figures. The Becker's write-up states outright that no specific accuracy metric is given, and "beat two other models" cannot be assessed without AUC, sensitivity, specificity and a calibration curve. The whole risk of opportunistic screening lies in the downstream cascade of tests that false positives set off — exactly the side a ranking cannot show.
  • Concern : external validity is unknown. The broader findings have not been validated outside Penn Medicine, and the training set comes from a single biobank, which may differ from other health systems in population composition, scanner models and imaging protocols. The team hopes to begin clinical testing only in early 2027; for now it remains preclinical.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The US spent this week deleting a disclosure duty that Taiwan put into force six months ago — a rare case where the timing runs in Taiwan's favor.

(1) Taiwan's disclosure duty predates the US one by six months and points the opposite way. On May 29, 2026, the Ministry of Health and Welfare issued its Guidance on the Use of Generative AI in Medical Institutions (ref. 衛部醫字第1151663164號), requiring an institution to notify patients in advance where a generative AI tool has recording functionality, and to disclose proactively, where an AI tool interacts directly with members of the public, that the person is interacting with AI. Set against ONC's HTI-5, finalized November 3, which deletes source-attribute disclosure for predictive AI, the two jurisdictions are moving in opposite directions. For Taiwanese vendors selling into the US this means building to the Taiwanese guidance as a baseline is not wasted work: disclosure has been demoted there from a certification requirement to a differentiator, but after the AMA pushed the duty of care onto hospitals the same week, hospitals will still ask for it at procurement.

(2) What lands in Taiwan is language and workflow, not models — and this week offered a clean sample. On November 6 Dajia Lee General Hospital deployed three AI systems under the Healthy Taiwan deep-cultivation program: a lobby guidance robot, a ward health-education robot, and a generative AI voice handover-record system built by Yi Chen Intelligence (CEO Hsu Yung-chang). What matters is not the model but three things: support for Mandarin, Taiwanese and English, with voice input able to mix Mandarin and Taiwanese and handle Hakka, and Indonesian and Vietnamese being added for migrant workers; a hospital-specific terminology database calibrated against common terms supplied by nurses, built in about three months; and standalone operation — the system runs without connecting to the electronic medical record. The hospital's and vendor's own figures are a handover record falling from 5 minutes to 1, at least 20 minutes saved on handover, health education for a new patient falling from 26 minutes to 20, and an 80.7% drop in the rate at which nursing staff request support. These are self-reported and not independently audited, but the shape itself shows where Taiwan's comparative advantage lies: many languages, a low integration threshold, a tight fit to workflow — not competing on models against $895.4B of commitments.

(3) The interoperability timetable collides with this week's validation gap. At a Kaohsiung Medical University forum on June 27, Minister of Health and Welfare Shih Chung-liang said Taiwan's medical centers are expected to achieve interoperable patient records by the end of 2026, with regional hospitals following in 2027 and district hospitals in 2028; at the same event Minister without Portfolio Chen Shih-chung set out the Healthy Taiwan deep-cultivation program's five-year NT$48.9 billion budget for hospital work on precision medicine and telehealth, as reported by United Daily News. Lay that timetable over this week's signals and the problem is clear: the moment interoperability is complete, Taiwanese hospitals acquire a large body of longitudinal data readable by AI — exactly the raw material an opportunistic screening model like Percival needs, and exactly the infrastructure that the "continuing post-deployment monitoring" in Google's framework most depends on. But Taiwan has no certification criteria corresponding to HTI-5 and no framework like the AMA's allocating the duty of care between developer and deployer. The three AI centers the Ministry launched with 16 hospitals in October 2024 address precisely the three pain points of deployment, clearance and reimbursement — and the US experience this week suggests the third will be the slowest: Cassidy's PATIENT AI Act is still only a discussion draft, with comments open until December 1.

05 — Further Reading

Five primary documents from the week worth keeping and reading slowly. The criterion: it changes your estimate of something, rather than adding one more item of news.
  1. Anthropic AI R&D Acceleration Assessment — METR (2026-11-05)

    Read it not for the 2x but to watch how an external assessor words things when it has been let in and then had the decisive evidence redacted. Its methodology appendix is the most honest public account of how to measure AI accelerating AI — including the places it admits it cannot measure well.

  2. A multi-method framework for evaluating generative AI in health — Google for Health (arXiv:2611.05127, 2026-11-04)

    The week's only document asking how to measure. For a hospital AI governance committee, the Critical User Journey and the risk-tiered, gate-by-gate thresholds can go straight into a procurement specification; the passage on two physicians diverging by 30–40% on a rating task is the warning any team planning its own internal evaluation should read first.

  3. Over 2% of LLM doses are wrong — some can be lethal — Doximity Engineering (2026-10-29)

    The scale — nine models, 2,204 drugs, 261,508 doses — makes this the most citable audit of LLM medication safety available. The figure to remember is not 2.3% but the 5.1x odds of error on drugs approved after 2024: it means the error rate is not a fixed property of a model but a function drifting with the time distribution of training data, so last year's validation does not hold this year. Note that the publisher is an interested party.

  4. Conversational diagnostic AI in ambulatory primary care: a prospective feasibility study — The Lancet (2026-10-08)

    Published before this week, but it remains the only prospective study of a patient-facing diagnostic AI in the real world, and it rewards a weekend read. Google's AMIE let patients at a Beth Israel Deaconess urgent primary care clinic chat with it before their appointment: the final diagnosis fell within AMIE's top seven possibilities in 90% of cases, its top three in 75% and its single most likely in 56%; physicians said the summary helped them prepare in 75% of cases and may have shaped their approach in 57%. But it is single-center, uncontrolled, text-only and skewed young, the authors explicitly disclaim quantitative efficacy claims, and one interaction was rated somewhat harmful by a provider because lymphoma appeared on the list of possible diagnoses and alarmed the patient. Unite.AI's summary is a serviceable entry point.

  5. Brand-name convergence in LLM drug recommendations — Nature Health (2026-10-29)

    The scale — 883,403 answers — makes this the hardest evidence to sidestep in any discussion of clinical AI funded by pharmaceutical advertising: five models' first recommendation converged on a single brand name 97.5% of the time, a dominant product held above 90% share in 96 of 123 scenarios, branded drugs were recommended at 20.5x the odds of generics, and a drug with its own Wikipedia entry at 3.00x. With OpenEvidence launching a free education platform this week, the study's scope widens from clinical decisions to medical training.
