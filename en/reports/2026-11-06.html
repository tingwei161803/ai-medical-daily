Friday · Product & Company Deep Dive

Two answers to "who pays" land in the same week: hospitals buy agent-hours, pharma buys the physician's attention

Friday, November 6, 2026 · Covering Oct 27 – Nov 5 · Taipei time

Friday is deep-dive day, and this week set medical AI's two revenue lines side by side. On November 2 Hippocratic AI shipped Polaris 6 Ultra, wrapping a 671B generative core in 30 specialist supervisors and 142 production monitors for a 7.4-trillion-parameter constellation, on $444M raised and 250 million voice interactions — and it bills hospitals by the agent-hour. On October 27, Vera Health closed a $28.5M Series A with twelve employees, 800,000 clinicians in 190 countries, free for good. In the same lane the $15B OpenEvidence sends the bill to pharma instead — roughly $300M annualized by July on Sacra's estimate, CPMs above $1,000, only about 5% of inventory sold. Then on October 29, 883,403 answers in Nature Health pinned that revenue line to the wall: across five models the first recommendation converged 97.5% onto a single brand name, with originator drugs carrying 20.5x the odds of a generic. Today is not about whose model is better. It is about where each company's money comes from, and which way that money bends the advice.

Voice agents Hippocratic AIPolaris 6 Ultra11/02

Polaris 6 Ultra: 7.4 trillion parameters spent on not mishearing a drug name

What

Hippocratic AI announced Polaris 6 Ultra from Menlo Park on November 2. The architecture is a 671B-parameter generative core plus 30 specialist supervisor models and 142 continuously running production monitors — 7.4 trillion parameters of constellation, against 5.0 trillion in Polaris 5 (the company's own release is dated Oct 31). Time to first sentence is 278 ms, against 0.71–0.94 seconds for the four fastest commercial providers on byte-identical prompts, and end-to-end speed is 1.3x the previous generation. Its in-house medical TTS pronounced 90% of 629 drug names correctly, versus 75% for a leading commercial TTS. The hardest numbers come from 2,778 clinician-reviewed production calls: misheard medications or symptoms fell from 1.19 to 0.20 per hundred calls, misheard numbers, names and contact details from 0.57 to 0.00, hallucinated words on noise-only audio from 24% to 0.6%, and television speech attributed to the patient from 64.8% to under 3%. Training drew on 323 million real patient conversations.

Why it matters

Note what kind of numbers these are. They are not diagnostic accuracy; they are call quality. Attributing a television voice to the patient, or hallucinating words out of room noise, are the failure modes that actually hurt people on a voice line — and most vendor releases over the past year have not reported them at all. That Hippocratic published an ugly 64.8% baseline is the most useful thing in this announcement: it moves the argument from how clever the model is to whether, in a room with the TV on, the call records the right dose.

Discount this

The "18 points above base, 11 points above the best frontier model" figures come from Hippocratic's own internal healthcare-intelligence benchmark, with no third-party audit and no published item set. The "0.00% minor-harm rate" is likewise in-house clinician output testing. The release says only that pricing is newly flexible and lower; it gives no per-hour rate, and names no customer, referring only to unnamed "health systems" among its investors. Even the supervisor count appears as both 30 and 31 within the same document.

Decision support Mayo Clinic PlatformCDS11/03

Mayo built its own in-EHR decision support: 35 subspecialty chats and 22 calculators

What

On November 3 Mayo Clinic Platform commercially launched an AI clinical decision support solution built from two pieces: an LLM-powered clinical chat spanning 35-plus subspecialties that answers natural-language questions with citations to peer-reviewed literature, and 22 risk and diagnostic calculators reachable by text or voice dictation. The knowledge base ingests new trial results, revised guidelines and drug safety warnings daily; the calculators mix industry-standard risk stratification algorithms with proprietary tools developed and validated by Mayo investigators. It is available to Mayo's own clinicians and to outside organizations in Mayo Clinic Platform's provider programs. Mayo's framing is that clinicians should not have to leave the EHR to consult several references.

Why it matters

This is a third revenue line beside today's two: the health system builds it itself, with its brand and its own validated algorithms as the moat — no ads, no agent-hours. For OpenEvidence, Vera Health and UpToDate, the Mayo name is the one input they cannot copy: the same literature, under a different signature. Worth noting that Mayo is opening it to outside organizations in its platform programs, which means this ends up as a product sold to other hospitals, not merely an internal tool.

Discount this

Public coverage carries no pricing, no list of supported EHR vendors, no count of outside organizations live, and not one clinical outcome or accuracy figure — only three scale numbers: 35-plus subspecialties, 22 calculators, daily updates. For a tool pitched as replacing several reference lookups, nothing is disclosed about error rate or citation fidelity.

Research Nature HealthGPT-5.4 / Claude / Gemini / Llama / MedGemma10/29

883,403 answers: AI's first drug pick converges 97.5% onto one brand

What

A team from Osaka Metropolitan University and MD Anderson published "Large language models show product name convergence in drug recommendations" in Nature Health. The design crossed 123 condition-and-active-ingredient pairs with 1,440 sociodemographic profiles into 177,120 synthetic clinical scenarios, put them to GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Flash, Llama 4 and MedGemma asking for up to three FDA-approved brand names each, collected 883,403 answers containing at least one recommendation, and had four physicians verify names and approval status. Each pair had 5.5 approved products available on average — yet the single most frequent product took 97.5% of first recommendations, 96 of the 123 pairs had a dominant product above 90%, and inter-model agreement reached Fleiss' κ = 0.895. Against US prescribing data for 2019–2023, the most-prescribed product matched the AI's first pick only 29.7% of the time, and 17.0% weighted by prescription volume. The direction of the bias is unambiguous: originator brands carried 20.5x the odds of a generic, products with higher Google search volume 1.70x, and products with their own Wikipedia entry 3.00x. In a cost simulation, 38 of 51 ingredients with complete pricing (74.5%) came out more expensive under the AI's recommendation distribution.

Why it matters

This bolts directly onto today's theme: it shows that even with no advertising at all, models skew systematically toward originator brands purely because of how loudly a brand name appears in web text. The 3.00x for having a Wikipedia entry is the one to sit with — that is not clinical evidence, it is editing activity. Which puts an ad-funded platform like OpenEvidence in an awkward spot: it can honestly say advertisers do not touch the answers, while this paper shows you never needed to touch the answers — touching the corpus is enough. And pharma marketing has always been in the business of touching the corpus.

Discount this

Everything here is synthetic: no real patients, no real prescribing behavior. The eye-catching "$55.6M a year, a 1,019% increase" is a projection conditional on physicians prescribing according to the AI's recommendation distribution — not money anyone has spent, and the conditional must travel with the figure. Pricing is US-based and does not transfer to Taiwan's single payer. Different model versions or prompts could move the results. The figures here are taken from Chinese-language secondary coverage; the primary paper page could not be retrieved in full during this run.

Funding Vera HealthThreshold Ventures10/27

Twelve people, no enterprise marketing budget, 800,000 clinicians in 190 countries

What

Vera Health announced a $28.5M Series A on October 27, led by Threshold Ventures with Mantis VC and SV Angel, and existing backers Y Combinator and Gradient increasing their stakes, for $32M in total capitalization. The company reports more than 800,000 clinicians across 190 countries, up from a few thousand active clinicians a year earlier — about one in four practicing physicians in Spain and Italy, one in seven in France. It has been the most-downloaded clinical app worldwide on both the Apple App Store and Google Play every month since June 2026, and logged more September downloads than OpenEvidence and Doximity combined. Headcount is twelve, the enterprise marketing budget is zero, and growth has come almost entirely from peer referral.

Why it matters

The architectural difference is the point: Vera retrieves and grades evidence before generating an answer, rather than generating text and attaching citations after. It indexes more than 60 million peer-reviewed publications, guidelines, consensus statements and FDA/EMA drug labels, scores study design, sample size, cohort power, recency, journal impact factor and citation velocity, and then color-codes each claim by evidence strength, flagging thin evidence in red. That makes uncertainty a UI element rather than a disclaimer. The enterprise tier, Vera Spaces, lets hospitals upload their own order sets, formularies and protocols to be prioritized alongside global literature — the worked example is antimicrobial dosing tuned to a hospital's own antibiogram.

Discount this

Every user figure is company-reported with no third-party audit, and "most downloaded" measures downloads, not active use — a twelve-person team serving 800,000 physicians is a ratio that invites a question about daily actives. Valuation, revenue and Vera Spaces pricing are all undisclosed. With no advertising and no published enterprise revenue, who subsidizes a permanent free tier for clinicians is an open question.

Deployment OpenEvidenceNYU LangoneEpic10/29

OpenEvidence moves inside NYU Langone's Epic chart, covering 12.4 million visits

What

On October 29, OpenEvidence and NYU Langone Health announced that "Patient Aware Clinical Intelligence" is embedded in the system's Epic workflow: clinicians ask in natural language inside Epic and get peer-reviewed answers carrying the patient's context. The footprint covers seven inpatient locations, several hundred outpatient sites and the Family Health Centers, and extends to trainees at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and NYU Grossman Long Island School of Medicine. The scale figures: 6,000-plus physicians and faculty, 2,000-plus residents and fellows, over 54,000 employees, 12.4 million outpatient visits a year, over 421,000 ED visits, plus more than 100,000 patients and about 600,000 visits at the Family Health Centers. Founder Daniel Nadler's line is that "the world's medical literature is now in the room for every one of the 12 million visits".

Why it matters

The weight of this item sits entirely in the words "Patient Aware." OpenEvidence began as a literature Q&A tool that never touched the chart, so what pharma bought with an ad slot was a physician's attention. Once it can read the chart, the same slot buys knowledge of which kind of patient that physician is working through right now. Mount Sinai and Sutter Health run the same Epic embedding, and Sacra notes those health-system deployments run on the same ad-supported model. This is the line in today's report most deserving of a follow-up question.

Discount this

The release says nothing about which patient data the tool reads, how it is retained or shared, who can access it, or what security and privacy safeguards apply; the only privacy link is NYU Langone's website privacy policy, and the release does not say whether that policy covers this tool. Contract and financial terms are undisclosed, and there is no clinical validation beyond vendor claims. Even the site count appears as both "more than 400" and "more than 330" in the same document. "A majority of US physicians use it daily" and "more physicians than all other AI platforms combined" are company claims.

Governance EpicONCHTI-511/04

With the AI transparency requirement deleted, Epic says it is keeping it anyway

What

HIStalk reported on November 4 that Epic will keep supplying AI transparency documentation and evaluation tools to customers even though ONC has eliminated those requirements for certified EHRs, so health systems can still assess the fairness, validity and other attributes of its software. It is a direct response to HHS's final HTI-5 rule, which cut 14 certification criteria, revised 7 and projects over $1.5B in savings.

Why it matters

Voluntarily keeping a disclosure obligation that has just been deleted, from a position of 42% hospital market share, effectively resets the floor for everyone: the question other EHR and AI vendors now have to answer is not whether the rule requires it, but why Epic provides it and they do not. This is the quietest item of the week and possibly the most structural — when regulation lets go, the largest incumbent gets to define the standard. Transparency survives; the authority to define transparency moves from the regulator to the vendor.

Discount this

This is Epic's stated position in secondary coverage: no published documentation spec, no commitment on duration, and no word on which models or attributes the evaluation tools cover. A voluntary disclosure can be withdrawn at any time, and its content and format are Epic's to set, with no external audit. HTI-5 takes effect December 28, and its information-blocking provisions remain under review.

M&A AnthropicMithril Medicine10/27

A model company buys a one-person drugmaker: Anthropic acquires Mithril Medicine and publishes six partners' preclinical numbers

What

On October 27, Anthropic disclosed its acquisition of Mithril Medicine — a one-person company whose founder, Cody Tranbarger, used Claude to coordinate contracts, logistics and experiments across 11 external labs, taking a candidate treatment for a rare muscular dystrophy from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months; if it holds up, human trials could begin as early as late 2028. The same document lays out early data from six partners. Generate Biomedicines used Claude to redesign a CAR-T component about 70% shorter than standard, with immune cells expressing roughly 10x the receptors, killing tumor cells more efficiently and staying active longer in lab tests. Divergence Labs generated about 35,000 antibody designs in roughly a month, finding three that bind IgE and 20 that dislodge it, three of those better than the standard medication. BigHat Biosciences made and tested about 1,010 variants in roughly 10 weeks, with the best binding more than 10x more tightly, one lead staying in solution 18°C higher and another aggregating more than 100x more slowly. At Enveda, across 1,061 pages of documentation Claude caught all 11 issues human reviewers found and flagged seven they missed, cutting review time 89% from over 25 hours to under three, and drafting one regulatory document in four hours instead of about three months.

Why it matters

Placed in today's frame, this is a fourth revenue line: the model company sells neither subscriptions nor ads and simply owns the asset. OpenEvidence's September round was also reported as a quiet pivot into oncology drug development, strikingly parallel to Anthropic's move. Two companies starting from opposite ends — a general model and a physician search engine — arriving at the same conclusion: if your model genuinely compresses preclinical work by an order of magnitude, collecting a license fee is the least efficient way to monetize it. Enveda's 89% and its four hours against three months are the only regulatory-documentation figures in the whole document, and the ones pharma can adopt soonest.

Discount this

All of it is preclinical; none has reached humans. Every figure is reported by the partners' own lab or pilot work, and Anthropic itself stresses the work is early. The Column Group and Bristol Myers Squibb items carry no quantitative results at all. The acquisition price is undisclosed. "Human trials as early as late 2028" is a conditional, not a schedule.

Two breaches of the same shape in two weeks: staff pasted charts into their own ChatGPT

What

HIStalk reported on November 4 that at two Washington state mental health facilities, employees used personal ChatGPT accounts to draft clinical notes, potentially exposing the names, diagnoses and medication details of 16 patients. It is the second incident of this shape in two weeks: Johns Hopkins Bayview notified 3,182 patients on October 23 after an employee sent data to an unapproved AI tool, offering 12 months of credit monitoring.

Why it matters

Set these beside today's product analysis and the conclusion is blunt. When a hospital evaluates Polaris 6 Ultra's 142 production monitors or Vera Health's evidence-grading UI, the real comparator is not "use nothing" — it is "clinicians are already using their own free accounts." The data of 16 and 3,182 patients is already out, and neither event was a model failure; both were procurement moving slower than demand. The practical implication for buyers: deferring adoption is not the zero-risk option. It only moves the risk from an auditable vendor contract to an unauditable personal account.

Discount this

Both are cited from secondary coverage rather than the institutions' own notification letters, and the Washington state case says only "potentially exposed," with no confirmation that data was accessed or retained by anyone else. Sixteen patients is a very small number and cannot support a prevalence claim; no institution has published actual rates of this behavior.

02 — Product Analysis

Two new things this week, at opposite ends of one spectrum: one turns safety into 7.4 trillion parameters of infrastructure, the other turns uncertainty into a red flag on screen

Polaris 6 Ultra

Autonomous patient-facing voice care model · Hippocratic AI (US)

Function and position. Not a copilot — an agent that phones the patient: pre-op preparation, post-discharge follow-up, chronic disease management, medication confirmation, pre-work for Medicare annual wellness visits. Sold to health systems, priced by the agent-hour. More than 250 million patient voice interactions completed to date across 300-plus live clinical use cases, on $444M raised. Its technical signature is treating safety as architecture rather than post-processing: a 671B core surrounded by 30 specialist supervisors and 142 production monitors, plus mid-sentence reasoning — planning the next sentence while the current one still streams, which is how 278 ms to first sentence arrives without an audible pause, with the planning decisions logged to a clinician-facing Safety Portal.

  • Strength : it publishes the ugly baseline. Across 2,778 clinician-reviewed calls, television speech attributed to the patient fell from 64.8% to under 3%, and hallucinations on noise-only audio from 24% to 0.6% — in this industry, reporting a 64.8% is harder than reporting a 0.6%.
  • Strength : the ASR is conditioned on the chart. CASR v3 conditions on the patient's EHR and dialogue history, so it knows which medications this patient takes and can resolve a slurred syllable into the right drug name — something a general speech model structurally cannot do.
  • Concern : the headline claims are all unverifiable from outside. "Eleven points above the best frontier model" runs on an in-house benchmark with no published items; "0.00% minor-harm rate" is in-house testing. None of it has appeared in a peer-reviewed journal.
  • Concern : pricing is opaque. The release says only "lower, flexible pricing," without even a range. And the agent-hour is the hardest unit for a buyer to budget, because call volume is not a headcount seat — it rises as clinical teams get comfortable with it. With no price, there is no ROI calculation.
  • Concern : not one named customer. At 300-plus live use cases and 250 million interactions, no health system appears in the release willing to be named — which is itself a piece of information.

Vera Health（含 Vera Spaces）

Evidence-graded-first clinical search · Vera Health (US/global)

Function and position. Clinical Q&A for physicians, permanently free for licensed clinicians and trainees worldwide, with Vera Spaces as the enterprise tier sold to hospitals. It indexes over 60 million peer-reviewed publications, guidelines, consensus statements and FDA/EMA drug labels, and embeds official American College of Emergency Physicians policies and decision trees directly in results. The decisive technical choice is order of operations: retrieve first, score study design, sample size, cohort power, recency, journal impact factor and citation velocity first, then generate — rather than generate and attach citations after. It is HIPAA and GDPR compliant, SOC 2 Type II certified, and states it does not train on clinicians' queries.

  • Strength : uncertainty is visible. Color-coding claims by evidence strength and flagging thin evidence in red builds "how much should you trust this sentence" into the interface. Against Mayo and peers that say only that they cite peer-reviewed literature, the difference is that the latter leave the judgment to whoever opens the citation.
  • Strength : no advertising, so none of the conflict-of-interest line that today's Nature Health paper traces. It may still inherit corpus-driven brand bias — that is an industry-wide problem — but at least the money is not standing on the same side as the bias.
  • Strength : a completely different geography. About one in four practicing physicians in Spain and Italy, one in seven in France — while OpenEvidence's terms exclude the EU, EEA and UK outright. These are not two companies fighting over one pie; they are two markets.
  • Concern : the operating math is not yet demonstrated. Twelve employees and $32M of total capital have to carry inference costs for 800,000 users and continuous indexing of 60 million documents, and Vera Spaces discloses neither pricing nor customer count nor revenue. The sustainability of the free promise currently rests on a sentence, not a number.
  • Concern : the grading itself is unvalidated. Using journal impact factor and citation velocity as scoring inputs is a proxy for evidence quality, not evidence quality — high-impact journals also publish papers that get retracted. There is no study of how this scoring affects clinical outcomes.

Each fails differently: hourly billing fears rising volume, advertising fears papers like today's Nature Health, the giveaway fears falling behind on quality, the in-house build fears distribution, and asset ownership fears partner trust. Before choosing a vendor, ask what keeps it alive — because that decides which way it leans when the interests conflict.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The same technology grows a different shape here — because there is no DTC pharma ad market, and because the guidance already has a clause for it

(1) The ad model has no counterpart market in Taiwan, so "free" here has only two roads. OpenEvidence's entire business rests on the US permitting pharmaceutical companies to advertise prescription drugs to physicians at scale, with CPMs quoted above $1,000. Taiwan has no pharmaceutical advertising market of that size, which means "free for clinicians" can only be carried here by a hospital paying an enterprise license (the Vera Spaces or UpToDate road) or by public or society subsidy. That should change how Taiwanese hospitals evaluate these tools: go straight to enterprise terms rather than letting physicians adopt a free tier first and sorting out data governance later, because the data flows of a free tier have no corresponding advertising regulation here to constrain them.

(2) For an outbound patient-facing agent like Polaris, the first gate in Taiwan is a disclosure duty. The MOHW's Guidance on the Use of Generative AI by Medical Institutions, issued on May 29, 2026 under letter 1151663164, already requires that an AI tool speaking directly with the public proactively disclose that it is AI rather than waiting to be asked, and that patients be told in advance if recording is involved. That collides head-on with Polaris 6 Ultra's central pitch: it spends 7.4 trillion parameters making the conversation sound natural, complete with breaths and pacing, while Taiwan's guidance requires it to open by saying it is AI. Not a blocker — but the disclosure script, the human fallback path when a patient declines, and the recording notice all belong in the SOP before go-live, not after.

(3) What Taiwan is actually deploying is shift-handover documentation, not outbound patient calls. On October 22 the Dajia and Yuanli branches of the Lee's General Hospital system both went live with Yichen Intelligence's generative-AI voice handover record system, covering Mandarin, Taiwanese, Hakka, English and medical terminology, with real-time recognition, filler removal and speaker separation, then generative AI summarizing handover points and producing a care plan, alongside upgrades to nursing carts, the HIS and ward electronic whiteboards. Note why that choice is sound: the consequences of a handover error are contained, there is no direct patient interaction, no disclosure duty to handle — and multilingual recognition, Taiwanese and Hakka above all, is the real technical bar here. Polaris's 629-English-drug-name pronunciation test does not transfer at all. That is the window for Taiwanese vendors: not chasing 7.4 trillion parameters, but making clinical speech in Taiwanese and Hakka actually work.

(4) For Taiwan, the Nature Health paper matters through the single payer, not through cost. Its cost projection is built on US drug prices, and the coverage itself says it cannot be transferred to Taiwan's NHI. But the 20.5x odds favoring originator brands has nothing to do with pricing — it is corpus bias, and it travels into Taiwan with the model untouched. Under Taiwan's system that will not show up as an acquisition-cost explosion, because reimbursement rules and reference pricing already bound that. It will show up as a different friction: the AI recommends an item the NHI does not reimburse on those terms, generating more prior-review and post-hoc disallowance disputes. What has to be handled before go-live is not price but feeding NHI reimbursement rules and the hospital's own formulary into the retrieval layer — which is exactly why a Vera Spaces-style "upload your formulary and prioritize it" feature is the thing most worth paying for here.
