Saturday · General AI

The week the market repriced the frontier labs, four desktop agents arrived at once — and every scorecard with a third party on it came back bad

Saturday, November 21, 2026 · Covering Nov 13–21 · Taipei time

Today's slot is general AI, not medicine — but both ends point back to the same place. One end is price: Anthropic sold 980 million shares at $83 on Nov 19 to raise $81.3bn, and opened at $111.50, closed at $97.17 (+17%) for a cap just under $1.8tn — on a prospectus showing $33.2bn of nine-month revenue and a $104bn net loss over the same period. The other end is evidence: in the same week Google pushed Gemini into your Mac, an Apple job listing gave away that it is building a computer-use agent too, and Microsoft shipped seven MAI models at Ignite — not one safety or performance number among them from an outside party. The three records that do have a third party present read: Meta's Muse dropped a stranger's bank balance into a chat about the weather, ARTEX took the European Commission's payroll and medical reimbursements down for over ten days, and four big US banks moved every agent-caused loss onto the customer. Medicine was on the list the whole time: the FBI's ARTEX alert names healthcare among its ten target sectors.

Capital markets AnthropicNasdaq: ANT11/19

The largest IPO ever: $81.3bn, up 17% on day one, a cap just under $1.8tn — and a $104bn net loss in the same prospectus

What

Anthropic listed on the Nasdaq on Nov 19 under the ticker ANT. It priced at $83 a share, above the marketed $70–78 range, and upsized the offering from 825 million shares to 980 million shares for $81.3bn, or $107.3bn including a $26bn private placement. It opened at $111.50, 34% above the IPO price, and closed at $97.17, up 17%, for a market cap just under $1.8tn at the close; exercising the 147-million-share greenshoe would take total proceeds to $118bn. The same filing shows $33.2bn of revenue for the nine months to Sept 30, against $2.5bn a year earlier, and a $104bn net loss over that period — most of it, Fortune notes, from how the convertible notes were accounted for. CEO Dario Amodei said on the day that "this moment is bigger than any one company, or any one country."

Why it matters

This is not one company listing; it is the first time the whole sector has to answer questions quarter by quarter, line by line. Frontier-lab valuations used to rest on private rounds and venture narrative; from this day they rest on 10-Qs and earnings calls — and the first set of numbers shows both the prettiest revenue curve available ($2.5bn to $33.2bn, more than twelvefold) and the hardest income statement to explain. The implication for medical AI is direct: buyers finally have a public financial floor against which to judge a vendor's staying power, and the start-ups that signed multi-year hospital contracts over the past two years will now have their capital structures compared against a listed-company standard for the first time.

Discount this

"Largest ever" depends which figure you compare. Al Jazeera itself notes SpaceX's June debut raised $75bn but reached $85.7bn once underwriters exercised their overallotment — more than Anthropic's $81.3bn. The market cap also comes in two versions: Fortune's close-price figure is just under $1.8tn, but it notes a lower share-count basis gives roughly $1.3tn. And the broader market fell hard that day (Nasdaq down 550 points, Dow down 1,101), so attributing the first-day move is not clean.

Security European CommissionARTEXCERT-EU11/19

Medical reimbursements down for over ten days: the European Commission confirms an AI attack framework got into its personnel systems

What

The Commission confirmed the attack on Nov 19. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier said the first signs appeared on Oct 22 and personnel systems were disrupted for more than ten days, with payroll, leave requests, medical reimbursements and translation services among the services hit. POLITICO broke the story and reported that CERT-EU identified the attackers' use of ARTEX; Regnier called it an "AI-generated cyber campaign" without naming the tool or a motive. The Commission says it notified affected individuals, is preventively checking other systems and is in contact with CERT-EU and the European Data Protection Supervisor; it has no evidence of a broad staff data breach and knows of no ransom demand. ARTEX is an open-source AI hacking framework released in July 2026 that uses commercial models to automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation.

Why it matters

This is the week's only third-party record of what an AI agent actually cost someone, and it was not measured in a lab — it was measured inside an institution with its own CERT team, its own data-protection supervisor, and three disclosed incidents this year. What went down was not a peripheral service either: payroll and medical reimbursements. Health payment workflows are exactly the high-value, lightly monitored flank the FBI's FLASH alert says ARTEX goes after — partner portals and third-party integrations, which is most of what a hospital's information architecture is made of.

Discount this

The attribution to ARTEX comes from POLITICO relaying CERT-EU; the Commission never named the tool itself. "More than ten days" is the reporting's figure — the Commission did not say how long each service was down. And the attack began in late October and was only confirmed on Nov 19, so this is a delayed disclosure of an older incident, not a same-day event; Regnier's explanation was that publicising defensive measures undermines them, and that "there was no hiding whatsoever."

Security FBIARTEXHealthcare named10/13

FBI: 1,600-plus ARTEX consoles publicly exposed, some intrusions at privileged access in under three hours, healthcare on the ten-sector list

What

The FBI's FLASH alert went out on Oct 13 and names ten sectors: financial services, government, technology, defence, semiconductor, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, telecommunications and legal. Citing Censys research, it found over 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX console instances and says total deployments are likely far higher, including systems behind firewalls and on private networks; some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. Technically, ARTEX pairs with any large language model, commercial or Chinese-origin, turning high-level goals into machine commands executed by AI agents and automating phases from reconnaissance through data exfiltration. The FBI found Chinese models most prevalent in attacker instances, with DeepSeek Flash models dominating. It was published on GitHub in August by an account called "Autumn-27"; the developer, identified as Li Puhua, later removed the repository, but forks and a commercial version persist.

Why it matters

Read this with the story above and you get a complete causal chain: the tool goes public in July and August, the FBI warns on Oct 13 and puts healthcare on the list, and on Oct 22 an EU institution's medical reimbursements stop. Healthcare carries one weakness on that chain that others do not: it has unusually many external interfaces — payers, labs, imaging centres, pharmacies, telehealth, patient portals — and those are usually monitored far less tightly than the core EHR. What "privileged access in under three hours" means for a hospital is that an incident-response cadence built around a human on shift is already too slow.

Discount this

The alert is dated Oct 13, before this report's window; it is here because it is the direct background to two of this week's stories. Healthexec's write-up notes the FBI gave healthcare no sector-specific guidance, only four general aims: reduce external attack surface, harden identity and access, strengthen application protections, preserve evidence. The "1,600-plus" is Censys's count of exposed instances earlier this month — not a count of attacks, and not a claim that all of those instances are in attackers' hands.

Desktop agents GoogleApplemacOS 27.311/19

Same day: Google's agent moves into your Mac, and Apple raises the bar for everyone else's from four steps to fourteen

What

On Nov 19 Google shipped computer use in Gemini for Mac: multistep tasks across local files, apps and the web — filling in forms, repeating one operation across a batch of files, sorting files by set criteria, building slideshows from files or online dashboards. It is English and US only at launch and needs a Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra subscription; users can pause, stop or approve actions before they happen, and the agent runs in a live preview in the chat so the screen and cursor stay usable. The same day, Bloomberg reported Apple is building an autonomous agent of its own — writing code, browsing, controlling apps, moving across devices — on the evidence of a job listing posted Nov 17 and since pulled. The listing seeks a senior engineering manager and reads "imagine an AI agent that can use a computer the way a capable person does," states that agents running code or using credentials need strong permission models, sandboxing and transparency, puts the hire alongside the Siri, UI Frameworks and Apple Foundation Models teams, and points to a launch no earlier than late 2027. Meanwhile in the macOS 27.3 beta, enabling ChatGPT's third-party computer-use support now takes 14 steps instead of four, with new warning screens asking users to reconsider handing a third-party agent control of their Mac. Apple says this protects users; some AI companies and users read it as blocking competitors.

Why it matters

The agent race is moving from how strong the model is to who controls the operating system's consent flow. Apple's fourteen steps are not a technical limit; they are a dial that can be turned each release, and that dial decides how usable every third-party agent actually is on a Mac. For medicine the implication is this: hospital workstations are nearly all managed devices, so what decides whether a clinician can use an agent is not which model cleared which benchmark but the MDM policy and the OS permission screen. The first gate for a desktop agent entering clinical work is the IT department, not the FDA.

Discount this

The Apple story is reporting and inference from a job listing, not a company announcement: the listing was pulled, the timing is "no earlier than late 2027," the product is early, and Bloomberg's original is paywalled — this report relies on The Next Web's account and Mark Gurman's public posts on X. For Gemini for Mac, Google published no error rate, no task success rate and no safety evaluation, and US-English-only means there is no way to check it in any other setting yet. The fourteen-versus-four comparison comes from Gurman's reading of a beta, not from an Apple statement.

Privacy MetaMuseReuters11/18

It wrote a stranger's bank balance into a chat about the weather: Meta's Muse is crossing accounts

What

Reuters reported on Nov 18, from internal posts by Meta employees, that Muse was mixing up conversations across accounts and surfacing material that belonged to other people or that users never asked for — childcare arrangements, career advice, product orders and bank account balances. One employee said Muse added unrequested bank balance details to a conversation about weather and tides; another said Muse reminded her, on her personal phone, to reply to her sister's message on Signal, an app she said she never gave Muse access to. Muse launched in September 2026 and has passed 10 million downloads. A Meta spokesperson said employee testing is central to product development and that employees run different Muse builds from the public, including unreleased features.

Why it matters

This is the hardest class of agent bug to fix. A classic permissions failure is "something that should have been blocked wasn't." This is different: the model pulled memory that did not belong to this context into the answer it was assembling. The fault is not in the access-control layer; it is in the layer where the agent composes one reply out of memories from many sources. Transpose that failure mode to medicine and you get one patient's medication history appearing in another patient's summary, with no access log anywhere showing that anyone looked at a record they shouldn't have. Medical AI's audit tooling is almost entirely built on "who viewed which record when," and is blind to this.

Discount this

Every case here comes from Meta employees reporting on internal builds, and what Reuters saw were internal posts; Meta states explicitly that employee builds differ from the public one. The reporting does not say how many users were affected, nor whether the public build shows the same behaviour — this is "seen in internal testing," not "confirmed leakage to ordinary users." Reuters' original is subscription-only; this report relies on TechCrunch's account.

Liability CitiPNCJPMorgan11/18

Four big US banks rewrote their terms in one month: if your agent gets hacked, the loss is yours

What

Citi, the third-largest US commercial bank, updated its Online User Agreement this week to say customers who grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activity through those agents. Under the update, if an AI tool leaks credentials or is compromised, the customer is liable for what hackers then do with the account, and the bank will not reverse transactions an automated assistant makes, intentional or not. Citi adds that AI agents it provides itself carry no added liability. The same piece reports that PNC, the eighth-largest, changed its terms on Nov 18, that JPMorgan and Bank of America — the two largest — announced similar policies last month, and that Citizens emailed customers this week warning they would be liable for whatever their AI assistant does. The article also notes Anthropic's personal finance tool, launched in early November, is billed as "read-only" and "can't move, manage, or invest your money."

Why it matters

This is the week's most underrated story, because it is a risk-pricing move with actual legal force. In every other story this week, agent risk is still an argument; here, four institutions holding much of America's retail deposits have written it into contract, and all in the same direction: our own agents as before, third-party agents at your risk. Medicine will likely travel the same road — when hospitals start letting clinicians connect third-party agents to the EHR, the liability clause will decide whether it happens well before the technical spec does. Note the asymmetry too: the same capability carries no added liability when the platform supplies it, and all of it when a third party does.

Discount this

The reporting gives no effective dates, only relative time — "this week," "last month," "today." For Citizens it says only that an email went out, not that the terms changed. It gives no asset figures or customer counts either, only rankings. And how far these clauses actually hold up under state consumer-protection law is untested; there is no case law to point at yet.

Model releases MicrosoftMAIIgnite 202611/18

Microsoft shipped seven MAI models at once, every comparison measured in-house — and one of them runs ten million patient encounters a month

What

Microsoft shipped a full rank of MAI models at Ignite 2026: MAI Cyber 2 Flash (claimed to match GPT-6 Astra's quality on CyberGym-E2E at under 2% of its cost per task), MAI Code 2 (averaged over DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal Bench 2.1, SWE-Atlas and FrontierCode v1.1, claimed at 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol's performance for 40% of the price and 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's for about 5%, with developers preferring it 64% of the time in a blind frontend test), MAI Code 2 Flash, MAI Image 2.7 (claimed first on Artificial Analysis for image editing, fourth for text-to-image), MAI Image 2.7 Flash (about $10 per 1,000 images), MAI Realtime 1 (from $0.01 per audio minute) and the Decision 1.1 router (claimed 50x faster and 79x cheaper than GPT-6.1 Sol, used by 4,000 customers). On hardware, the Maia 200 accelerator is in production at 1.7x more tokens per watt. Medicine's square is Dragon Copilot Transcribe, built on MAI Transcribe 2 and live in Dragon Copilot, which the post says covers about 10 million patient encounters a month and halves multilingual error rates.

Why it matters

What this rank of models sells is not a capability ceiling but unit cost — nearly every comparison reads "near-frontier performance at a fraction of the price." That is the classic signal of a maturing model market: competition shifting from who is strongest to who is cheapest along the same performance line. What health buyers should look at is the Dragon Copilot Transcribe line, because it is the only figure on the stage with a real denominator (about ten million encounters a month) — a denominator big enough to give a decimal point in the error rate clinical meaning. Unfortunately the announcement says nothing about what baseline the multilingual error rate was halved from, in which languages, or on what test set.

Discount this

Not one figure in the post comes from a third party, with the single exception of the cited Artificial Analysis ranking. "About 10 million patient encounters a month" is coverage, not accuracy, and "halves multilingual error rates" comes with no baseline, no language list and no test set. MAI Cyber 2 Flash's availability is described only as existing MDASH customers, MAI Realtime 1 requires an interest form, and MAI Licensed Weights and custom models carry no general-availability detail. The AT&T line — a custom model beating GPT-6.1 Sol at 1/50th the cost — is a single customer anecdote.

Policy AUDIT AI ActSECWarner11/19

On the day of the IPO, four senators moved to put AI special purpose vehicles and compute concentration into SEC filings

What

Senators Mark Warner (D-VA), John Kennedy (R-LA), Angela Alsobrooks (D-MD) and Mike Rounds (R-SD) introduced the bipartisan AUDIT AI Act — Advancing Uniform Disclosure of Investments and Transparency of Artificial Intelligence — on Nov 19. It would require public companies to disclose whether they have an AI governance policy and a dedicated board AI committee, and to explain the absence of either, with any policy made public; non-financial public companies to disclose AI-related joint ventures, special purpose vehicles, variable interest entities and other major debt arrangements in SEC filings; companies with significant AI exposure to give standardised disclosure of material risks including third-party model reliance, compute concentration, costs and data liabilities; the SEC to study how companies account for AI R&D, revenue and capex and recommend changes; and the SEC to report annually on how automated investment strategies affect price discovery, market efficiency and herding. Kennedy: "AI is the future, but investing in it shouldn't be a guessing game." Warner: "We can't afford to have massive financial exposure building up behind the scenes."

Why it matters

The bill targets not model safety but the balance-sheet structure of the AI build-out: who leased how much compute through which entity, financed with what debt, and how deeply dependent on whose model. If "third-party model reliance" and "compute concentration" really do become standardised disclosures, the effect carries all the way to medical AI: the great majority of medical AI products are a thin layer over three or four frontier model providers, and nothing in a procurement document today requires that to be stated. For a hospital, a vendor going under is harder to deal with than a vendor being inaccurate.

Discount this

This is a bill, not a law — no committee review, no schedule, no vote. The release does not mention compute leases or "circular deals," so this report sticks to the SPVs, joint ventures and debt structures it does name. The bill text itself was not obtained; the summary above comes from the senator's own press release.

M&A / architecture NVIDIAInceptionMercury 311/19

NVIDIA reportedly nears a $500m-plus deal for diffusion-LLM start-up Inception, with all three founding professors joining full time

What

Reported by The Information and relayed by the decoder: NVIDIA is nearing a deal to buy Inception for at least $500m, plus performance bonuses for its 30-odd employees. Inception is a two-year-old San Francisco Bay Area start-up building language models that generate text by diffusion — refining a whole response step by step rather than predicting one token at a time — with Mercury 3 as its latest release. Co-founders Stefano Ermon, Aditya Grover and Volodymyr Kuleshov, all professors, are expected to join NVIDIA full time. The piece frames the deal as strengthening NVIDIA's open-source Nemotron models and its physical models for robots, and as a hedge now that both OpenAI and Anthropic are building their own inference chips; Palantir and US government agencies already use Nemotron as an alternative to Chinese models. Talks began before CEO Jensen Huang's resignation, and after Microsoft had expressed interest.

Why it matters

Of everything this week, this is the only deal buying an architecture rather than a distribution channel. Autoregressive generation's latency scales with output length; diffusion refines the whole output at once, so the relationship between latency and length is different in kind. For anything that must return a full structured output inside a few hundred milliseconds — real-time speech, on-device inference, batch processing of long documents — that is a different road. The nearest medical correspondence: on-device inference and long-chart summarisation have both been held back by the per-token cost of autoregression, which is the same problem MedJev-4B was attacking in Thursday's technology edition — only from the single-forward-pass direction instead.

Discount this

This is an unclosed deal reported from three people familiar with the talks, with neither company initially commenting, and the price is a range: "at least $500m." The decoder says explicitly that it has no tokens-per-second figure for Mercury 3, so this report cites no speed number at all (the "1,000+ tokens per second" circulating in secondary coverage refers to an earlier Mercury release, whose primary source this report did not obtain). Whether diffusion LLMs match comparable autoregressive models on quality has no independent evaluation to cite.

02 — Product Analysis

Two agents: one touches everything with no third-party number attached, one does a single thing and has a denominator — medicine should learn the shape of the second

Gemini for Mac (computer use)

General-purpose desktop agent · Google (US)

Function and position. The Gemini desktop app on macOS, running multistep tasks across local files, native apps and the web: filling in forms, repeating one operation over a batch of files, sorting files into folders by set criteria, generating slideshows from files or online dashboards. It is sold to individual productivity users who already hold a Google AI subscription, and is English and US only at launch, requiring AI Plus, Pro or Ultra.

  • Strength : it makes the human-in-the-loop the default rather than a setting — actions can be paused, stopped or approved before they run, the agent works in a live preview inside the chat, and it does not take over the screen or cursor. That is more honest than much of the field: it concedes the agent will get things wrong, so it puts a stop point before every action.
  • Concern : there is no verifiable performance or safety figure of any kind — no task success rate, no error rate, no taxonomy of mis-actions, no third-party evaluation. For a product granted permission to open a user's local files and apps that is a wide blank, and Muse's cross-account mixing in the same week is a demonstration of what can live inside it.
  • Concern : the platform risk is not in Google's hands. macOS 27.3 took third-party computer-use enablement from four steps to fourteen — Gurman's example is ChatGPT, but in principle the same consent flow governs every non-Apple agent. How usable this class of product actually is gets decided by the operating system, not by the feature list.

Dragon Copilot Transcribe (MAI Transcribe 2)

Clinical speech documentation · Microsoft (US)

Function and position. A medical transcription model built on MAI Transcribe 2, live in the Dragon Copilot line and sold into hospital and clinic documentation workflows. Microsoft says it covers about 10 million patient encounters a month and halves multilingual error rates. The sharpest difference from the panel beside it is how narrow its scope is: it does not operate your computer, it turns one conversation into one document.

  • Strength : about 10 million encounters a month is the only real-world denominator in the whole launch. A denominator is what makes statistics mean something: how much an error-rate drop is worth, how often a rare failure shows up, which languages lag — at this scale those questions are answerable, even if Microsoft did not answer them this time.
  • Strength : a narrow scope is itself a safety design. The permission it holds is "listen to this consultation, write this note," not "open my files, click my apps" — and the cross-source memory failure mode Muse showed has a far smaller surface to work with in that architecture.
  • Concern : "halves multilingual error rates" is missing the three things needed to read it: from what to what, in which languages, on what test set. Halving sounds strong, but if the baseline was 20% going to 10%, that is still one word in ten wrong in an outpatient note. The figure also has no third party on it, exactly as in this week's product edition — where the only number on Nov 20 that had passed a regulator came from a single-purpose 510(k).

The sentence that joins the two columns. The temptation medical AI has to resist over the next year is taking the left column's scope of capability with the left column's standard of evidence. A desktop agent can ship with no third-party number because the cost of its mistakes is borne by the individual user. That does not hold in clinical work: the cost is borne by the patient, and the patient is not the one who pressed approve.

Medicine's position is peculiar: it is the one industry where the consent flow already exists and already has a legal name. The name is informed consent.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The two things that land hardest in Taiwan this week: one on the production line, one in hospital security policy

(1) New export rules took effect Nov 19, and TSMC has suspended sub-16nm orders for Chinese customers. Per CNA on Nov 20, relaying Reuters: the US Commerce Department's new advanced-chip export rules took effect on Nov 19 US time, restricting Chinese firms' access to advanced AI compute through overseas data centres and tightening controls on Chinese chip designers using overseas foundries. The decisive change is the direction of proof: TSMC could previously check that a chip's compute and specifications fell below the control thresholds and skip the licence, whereas the new rules presume advanced chips are controlled, so a licence is needed unless a specific exception applies. The reporting says orders at 16nm and more advanced nodes from affected Chinese customers must be suspended unless the customer obtains a US licence, that Chinese chip designers are generally held to 28nm and 40nm provided their chips use no HBM and need no advanced packaging, and that some previously approved advanced-node orders have been paused. TSMC's review guidance covers customers' operating locations, parent companies, major shareholders, packaging providers and shipping routes, including orders placed through overseas subsidiaries or intermediaries in Taiwan, the US, Europe, Japan and Southeast Asia. TSMC issued no official statement; these details come from Reuters' anonymous sources and the TSMC guidance it cites.

(2) In the same week, NVIDIA put its expansion commitments on six Taiwanese suppliers. CTS reported on Nov 18 that NVIDIA, announcing third-quarter results, expanded its commitments with supply-chain partners to raise substrate, advanced-packaging and foundry capacity, naming TSMC, Foxconn, SPIL, Kinsus, King Yuan Electronics and Unimicron: total substrate capacity to 2.5x within three years, expanded advanced packaging, and semiconductor test capacity doubled in 2027. TSMC said it would plan against future technology requirements and have manufacturing capability ready; Foxconn said it would deepen cooperation on next-generation graphics chips and networking platforms. Read together, Taiwan received expansion orders and tightened controls in the same week — and both rest on the same supply chain.

(3) The hospital side: the ban exists, the enforcement does not. To put the FBI naming healthcare among ARTEX's ten target sectors at Taiwanese scale, the thing worth reading is a survey from the same week. The HIPAA Journal reported on Nov 18 on DNSFilter's "Seeing the Risk, Missing the Signal: AI Security in Healthcare": 43% of healthcare security leaders call employees sharing sensitive data with AI the biggest emerging risk of the next twelve months; one in ten has already had an incident caused by an unapproved AI tool; 27% confirmed and 23% suspect that employees connected an unapproved AI tool to Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or SSO; 70% have a formal policy prohibiting unapproved AI tools but only 40% say it is consistently enforced; 47% say an employee can authorise a new AI tool to access company systems without higher-level approval; and 60% rely on their AI and SaaS vendors to tell them an incident happened, against 43% in other sectors. These are US figures, but they describe a structure any Taiwanese hospital would recognise: IT writes the policy, the clinical side connects the tools, and notification depends on the vendor. What can actually be done is neither much nor hard: keep an auditable list of which AI tools hold authorised access to the institution's Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, and before any agent-class tool comes through the door, ask where the liability for its mistakes is written down — four US banks demonstrated this week how bluntly that answer can be put.
