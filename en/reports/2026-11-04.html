Wednesday · Regulation & Policy

Washington let go — and the same day, three documents pushed accountability onto hospitals

Wednesday, November 4, 2026 · Covering Oct 29–Nov 4 · Taipei time

November 3 was the densest day of the year for medical-AI governance, and all three documents point the same way. ONC's HTI-5 final rule removes 14 of the 60 health-IT certification criteria now in effect and revises 7 more — among them the "source attribute" transparency requirement for predictive decision-support interventions. ONC puts the saving at more than $1.5 billion in regulatory costs, with most changes effective December 28 (MedCity News, 2026-11-03). The same day, the AMA published an issue brief on clinical-AI liability stating plainly that warnings and disclaimers from developers are "not sufficient," and that a deployer — a health system, hospital or practice — owes a duty of care covering tool selection, pre-deployment evaluation, training and performance monitoring over time (MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03). Also that day, the tally from the FDA's generative-AI device discussion paper surfaced: of more than 500 submissions, AdvaMed wants the existing framework extended, a cardiology society wants "support" separated from "decide," Oura wants generative features inside the wellness exemption, and a patient group and a Stanford lab want premarket evidence tightened (MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03). Stack the three together and the shape is clear: the federal layer that used to vet tools on a hospital's behalf is being dismantled, and nothing has yet replaced it — while the replacement Sen. Cassidy floated in his Oct 29 PATIENT AI Act discussion draft works by letting federal product-liability law preempt the states, 11 of which have already passed 14 health-AI laws this year (Becker's, 2026-10-30; Transparency Coalition, 2026-07-27).

01 — Top Stories

Seven items, one line: the federal layer drops verification duties, outsources liability allocation to a professional body, leaves disagreement to a docket, and reaches for preemption to unify
Final rule ONC / HHSHTI-511/03

HTI-5 finalized: source-attribute disclosure for predictive AI is gone, and hospitals must build their own evaluation process

What

ONC's fifth Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability final rule removes 14 and revises 7 of the 60 certification criteria now in effect — the proposal would have removed 34 — and ONC estimates more than $1.5 billion in regulatory savings. Most changes take effect 2026-12-28, a few on 2027-01-01. Among the deletions: the developer obligation to supply source attribution for AI recommendations, the source-attribute access and modification requirements, and the intervention risk management requirements. The Federal Register text says ONC "finalized removal of source attribute information transparency" for both evidence-based and predictive decision-support interventions. The rule also eliminates the voluntary certification criteria for security tags that restrict sensitive records such as behavioral health and substance-use data; HIPAA's own requirements are unchanged (MedCity News, 2026-11-03).

Why it matters

The line that carries this story is from Mel Soliz, an attorney working on health-data privacy and interoperability: "Certification no longer guarantees access to information about a predictive tool's intended use." Without that federal layer, hospitals and health systems have to build evaluation processes themselves; she recommends writing disclosure, validation and bias-testing requirements straight into AI procurement contracts. She also calls the loss of security tagging the most disappointing change for providers, warning that without tags "it becomes exceedingly challenging to apply the appropriate consent rules." The deletion is a cost win for EHR developers and a net workload increase for hospitals — in the same week hospitals are being told they carry more of the liability.

Discount this

The "more than $1.5 billion" is ONC's own regulatory impact estimate, not an independent audit — and it counts developer compliance costs, not the evaluation costs shifted onto hospitals. The "roughly 39 retained" figure used here is derived from 60 minus 14 minus 7; the MedCity News piece does not state it directly.

Liability AMAClinical AI11/03

The AMA splits clinical-AI liability three ways — developer, deployer, physician — and a disclaimer does not discharge the duty

What

The AMA issue brief allocates liability in more detail than any previous industry document. Physicians still owe clinical judgment — scrutinizing AI output and rejecting recommendations where needed — but the AMA argues they should not answer for AI errors they cannot control. Developers must ensure recommendations are safe and accurate and must supply information on intended use, performance and limitations so users can spot and respond to errors; "warnings and disclaimers alone are not sufficient." Deployers — health systems, hospitals, physician practices — must choose the right tool for the clinical scenario, evaluate it before physicians touch it, provide training, monitor performance over time, and stand up adverse-event processes. Harmed patients should have pathways to recourse. Jennifer Goldsack, who heads the AMA Center on Digital Health and AI, said physicians may be held accountable for risks they "cannot control" and "in some cases, may not even be able to identify" (MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03; Healthcare Dive, 2026-11-03).

Why it matters

The brief landed the same day as HTI-5, and the two pull in opposite directions: ONC lifted the developer's source-attribution duty out of the certification criteria, while the AMA says developers owe users enough information to identify errors. That duty now has no regulatory vehicle — only contracts and tort litigation — which is exactly why Soliz tells hospitals to write disclosure into procurement. The AMA's own 2026 survey explains the incentive: about one-third of physicians name "clear liability frameworks" as the primary driver of AI adoption, about a quarter have had no AI training at all, and 92% want more.

Discount this

This is a professional body's position paper with no legal force, and the AMA represents physicians — pushing liability toward developers and hospitals serves its members. Neither secondary report gives the sample size or method behind the cited survey figures (one-third, one-quarter, 92%).

Docket FDA / CDRHFDA-2026-N-787411/03

FDA's generative-AI device docket: 500-plus comments, five irreconcilable pictures of how to regulate

What

On 2026-08-18 the FDA issued a discussion paper on generative-AI-enabled devices and opened comments (docket FDA-2026-N-7874, closing 2026-10-19), posing questions in four areas: a possible two-axis risk framework; a premarket "competency assessment" approach combining non-clinical benchmarking with clinical confirmation of performance before patient use; several risk-proportionate postmarket monitoring options; and foundation models and agentic systems. Acting Commissioner Kyle Diamantas said "artificial intelligence is transforming medicine"; CDRH Director Michelle Tarver stressed keeping pace with digital health innovation; Digital Health Center of Excellence Director Rick Abramson called generative devices a reshaping force (FDA press announcement, 2026-08-18). The docket closed with more than 500 submissions, and MedTech Dive's Nov 3 round-up sorts them into five positions. AdvaMed wants the existing framework extended rather than a generative-specific one, says including generative AI should not automatically trigger a competency assessment, wants the predetermined change control plan mechanism broadened from what it calls "too restrictive," and wants risk-based postmarket monitoring left to manufacturers (MDMA and GE HealthCare take the same line). SCAI, the cardiovascular interventional society, wants clarity on when generative AI supports a decision versus makes one; AI task-force co-chair Dr. Afnan Tariq frames it as "are we replacing physicians or are we scaling physicians?" Oura proposes that informational, non-directive outputs not be treated as devices or be placed under enforcement discretion, comparing them to symptom checkers; the company has no FDA-cleared features today. Generation Patient, a nonprofit for young adults with chronic conditions, backs expanded postmarket monitoring but not at the expense of premarket evidence — policy director Luis Gil Abinader says that "would shift the burden of harm onto patients" — and wants AI companion tools out of the wellness exemption, citing a JAMA Pediatrics survey that nearly one-fifth of Americans aged 12 to 21 have used AI chatbots for mental-health advice. Stanford's Brainstorm lab, led by psychiatry professor Dr. Nina Vasan, proposes testing performance against "reasonably foreseeable use" rather than labeled indications alone, and a separate pass/fail standard for high-consequence failures — missed suicide risk, missed sepsis, a wrong insulin adjustment — so they are not buried in an average, because "a model's miss repeats across every patient, site, and device built on it." The lab's testing found safeguards degrading over long conversations: ChatGPT's protections eroded across extended exchanges, and a tester got around Claude's boundaries by opening a new chat (MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03).

Why it matters

The five positions differ in direction, not degree: industry wants the premarket bar left where it is and the monitoring duty kept in-house; clinicians and patients want the premarket bar raised and failures scored separately. No compromise satisfies both, and providers have already staked out a third front — in the same docket, provider groups including the AMA argued that developers, not providers, should monitor these devices, because the alternative would strain providers, especially small and rural organizations. The real fight in this docket has slid from "how do we evaluate the model" to "who pays for watching it forever."

Discount this

The five positions are MedTech Dive's representative selection from 500-plus submissions, not a distribution — nothing here supports a claim about which camp is the majority. The Stanford Brainstorm finding on safeguard decay over long conversations is that lab's own testing, with no peer-reviewed publication seen; it is cited here from secondary coverage, not from the original submission. The JAMA Pediatrics "nearly one-fifth" is likewise second-hand.

Fed vs states FDACMS11/03

FDA and CMS turn toward working with the states — and toward shielding doctors from AI liability

What

The headline on STAT health tech correspondent Mario Aguilar's Nov 3 piece is "FDA, CMS look to collaborate with states on health AI," with the dek: "Federal officials look to work with states on regulating AI, protecting doctors from AI liability, and more." It follows his Oct 30 piece, "FDA, CMS scramble to avoid regulatory logjam as more states look to test AI in clinical settings," whose subhead points at state sandboxes running into federal rules (STAT, 2026-11-03; STAT, 2026-10-30). Both sit behind the STAT+ paywall; this entry is written only from the publicly visible headline and dek, and does not paraphrase content we could not see.

Why it matters

The two headlines alone mark a shift in the federal position. State sandboxes (next item) are now authorizing AI to renew prescriptions and prescribe — activities that sit inside scope of practice, which is state territory — yet once the AI is deemed a device it is back under the FDA. The language of "collaborate" rather than "preempt" runs opposite to the federal product-liability preemption in Cassidy's draft: in the same week, the executive branch moves toward convergence while the legislative branch moves toward unification. Worth tracking, because it decides whether a medical-AI company faces one rulebook or fifty-one over the next two years.

Discount this

There is no body text here at all — no named officials, no policy document, no timeline. This is a lead worth watching, not confirmed policy, and no inference about what the FDA or CMS will actually do should rest on it.

Draft bill Sen. Bill CassidyPATIENT AI Act10/29

The PATIENT AI Act discussion draft: federal preemption, an HCPCS payment path, and a CMMI test of paying for autonomous AI — comments due Dec 1

What

Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a physician who chairs the Senate HELP Committee, released the PATIENT AI Act discussion draft on Oct 29. Jurisdiction: clarify whether the FDA or ONC oversees a given AI tool, and fold AI applications that are not FDA-regulated devices into ONC's certified health IT definition. Review: give the FDA a risk-based review process for software-based devices, including authority to accept premarket software changes and review updates on a rolling basis; let HHS certify third-party bodies to review low- and moderate-risk software. Liability: existing federal preemption of state product-liability law would apply to integrated and software-based devices, and HHS would commission a nonprofit to recommend approaches to AI liability. Payment: HHS would build a coverage evaluation framework for AI-based services and propose payment methodologies, plus a process for using Level II HCPCS billing codes; the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation would test a payment model for AI applications that deliver care autonomously. Privacy: HHS would issue HIPAA compliance guidance for AI use. Cassidy said implementing AI in health care "must be done responsibly and to the benefit of patients, innovators, and consumers." Stakeholder feedback is due Dec 1 to patientai@help.senate.gov (Becker's Hospital Review, 2026-10-30; ExecutiveGov).

Why it matters

The draft answers three of the gaps left by the items above, and each answer tilts toward developers. First, the transparency duty HTI-5 removed is not restored; instead non-device AI is folded into ONC's certification definition — wider scope, no reinstated text. Second, where the AMA pushes liability toward developers, the draft narrows the state-court route via federal product-liability preemption. Third, the payment codes medical AI has always lacked arrive as Level II HCPCS plus a CMMI autonomous-AI model — the first time a federal legislative document has addressed head-on how to pay for AI that sees the patient itself. For Taiwan and other markets that matters because a US autonomous-AI payment model would produce the first comparable rate schedule and indication list.

Discount this

This is a discussion draft, not an introduced bill: no bill number, no cosponsors, no schedule. The content here comes from Becker's secondary summary; we did not obtain the draft text, and the section count reported elsewhere (19 sections, 5 on Medicare) is not verified against the original and is therefore not asserted here as fact.

State law 11 states / 14 lawsUtah OAIP10/05

What preemption would override: 14 health-AI laws in 11 states this year, plus Utah's three clinical sandbox pilots

What

The Transparency Coalition's tracker counts 14 health-AI laws passed by 11 states in 2026 (15 bills listed, with Rhode Island's H 7349 and S 2197 identical), in four categories. Limiting insurer AI in authorizations (7): insurers may not rely solely on AI to deny, and a licensed clinician must review — Alabama SB 63 (effective 2026-10-01), Colorado HB 1139, Georgia SB 444 and Utah SB 319 (all 2027-01-01), Washington SB 5395 (2026-06-11), plus Illinois SB 3114 and Iowa HF 2635. Therapy-chatbot bans (5): Colorado HB 1195 (2026-08-12), Maine LD 2082 (2026-07-29, breach treated as an unfair trade practice), Rhode Island H 7349/S 2197 (2027-01-01), Tennessee SB 1580 (2026-07-01, barring AI from representing itself as a qualified mental health professional), Vermont H 816 (2026-06-17). Patient consent (1): Iowa HB 475 (2026-08-01) requires verbal disclosure before AI transcribes an appointment. Scope of practice (1): Utah SB 150 excludes AI tools that only advise or treat without practitioner interaction from its definition of "innovation." The same tracker counts 84 new AI laws across 27 states in 2026 across all policy areas (Transparency Coalition, 2026-07-27). On the other side, Utah's Office of AI Policy added three clinical AI pilots to its sandbox on 2026-10-05 and made healthcare a core pillar of its "Pro-Human AI Initiative": Expect Fitness (analyzes pelvic-floor questionnaires, assigns ICD-10 codes, drafts home exercise plans, with licensed physical therapists reviewing in the initial phase); August AI (refills of existing non-controlled maintenance medications for stable adults in 30/60/90-day supplies, verifying identity and checking Surescripts data for interactions, gaps and suicidality, with no authority to start new prescriptions or change dosages); and Nolla Health (prescriptions for mild-to-moderate acne without a synchronous visit, scoring severity from five-angle facial images and selecting from a restricted formulary including compounded topicals). The legal mechanism is a regulatory mitigation agreement between OAIP, the Division of Professional Licensing and the participant, authorized by Utah's 2024 SB 149: the state agrees not to enforce specified state healthcare provisions against that participant and its affiliated clinicians, for that service only. An RMA does not provide a liability shield, does not change the standard of care, does not exempt anyone from federal law, and does not cover pharmacies receiving AI-generated orders, which are not parties. Nolla's phase gates: two physicians review every prescription in phase 1; in phase 2 the AI sends prescriptions straight to pharmacists with retrospective physician review of all cases; in the final phase physicians review at least 10%. Advancing requires at least 95% concordance with physician determinations and zero missed hard stops (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13).

Why it matters

The same Holland & Knight analysis notes roughly 40 states have now passed laws governing providers' clinical use of AI. That is what Cassidy's preemption clause would reach, and what the FDA and CMS mean by "collaborate" in the STAT headline. Utah's design demonstrates the alternative route: not loosening the standard, but trading measurable gates for temporary non-enforcement — 95% concordance, zero missed hard stops, monthly reporting of user counts and complaints. Set that against the source-attribute disclosure HTI-5 deleted: the federal layer dropped a static document requirement, while Utah demands dynamic performance evidence. The latter is more use to a patient — but only in one state, only for three companies, only for one service each.

Discount this

The state-law list is from a 2026-07-27 tracker, over three months old, and new laws may have passed since; the Transparency Coalition itself expects more states to act before year-end. That page's headline says 14, its URL says 15, and its body says "more than a dozen in 11 states" — we use 11 states and 14 distinct laws, with the derivation stated. Effective dates for Iowa HF 2635 and Illinois SB 3114 are not given in the source. Utah's phase gates are contract terms, not achieved results: none of the three participants has published an actual concordance figure.

Sandbox DoctronicUtah OAIP10/XX

Autonomy written as numbers: Doctronic's AI prescription refills in Utah's sandbox — 191 drugs, human oversight of the first 250, then 10% sampling

What

Utah is testing AI that renews routine prescriptions without a clinician's direct involvement, under an agreement between Doctronic and the Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy inside the state's Department of Commerce. Covered drugs include statins, blood-pressure medications, certain psychiatric medications and birth control, across a refill formulary of 191 commonly prescribed drugs reviewed by independent pharmacists and the state; the formulary excludes narcotics, stimulants, injectables and short-term antibiotics. Autonomy opens up in phases: a human clinician oversees the first 250 refills, the second phase samples 10% of cases, and the final phase moves to random sampling. Doctronic reports monthly to the state on user counts, impact and technology assessments, user complaints, and accepted and denied refills, and the program includes safety checks, data-privacy requirements and ongoing reporting. Named: Doctronic co-founder Dr. Adam Oskowitz, co-founder and co-CEO Matt Pavelle, and Utah OAIP director Dr. Zach Boyd. Utah's AI regulatory sandbox was created in 2024; Texas's AI sandbox law took effect January 1 (Fierce Healthcare).

Why it matters

This is the most concrete "autonomous AI regulatory contract" on the table, and exactly the kind of service Cassidy's draft would send to CMMI for a payment test. Three design details are worth noting: (1) the gate is counted in cases, not time (the first 250 refills), so autonomy opens at the pace of real usage rather than the calendar; (2) the formulary is doubly bounded, positive list plus exclusions, removing whole high-risk pharmacological classes rather than trusting the model to judge; and (3) reporting includes denied refills, meaning the state wants to see not only false positives but the care gaps created by over-caution. Against that: pharmacies are not parties to the agreement (previous item) — the pharmacist receiving an AI-generated order gets no regulatory relief, so the risk sits with someone who signed nothing.

Discount this

The Fierce Healthcare page shows no publication date, so this is placed only around Utah OAIP's October 2026 expansion and tagged 10/XX rather than a specific day. Holland & Knight's analysis names "August AI" as the refill pilot participant, while Fierce names only Doctronic and does not mention August AI; whether these are the same pilot or two separate refill pilots in Utah could not be confirmed, so they are reported separately rather than merged. Every figure here is a contract term or a vendor/state self-report, with no third-party audited results.

Baseline FDA510(k)1,614

A baseline for all of this: 1,614 FDA-authorized AI devices — 96.2% via 510(k), 76.2% radiology, 54.3% from outside the US

What

Tallied from the FDA's AI-Enabled Medical Device List (list updated 2026-09-04, covering decisions through 2026-06-29; CSV downloaded 2026-09-26): 1,614 AI-enabled devices in total — 1,553 via 510(k) (96.2%), 40 De Novo (2.5%), 21 PMA (1.3%). By lead review panel: radiology 1,230 (76.2%), cardiovascular 154 (9.5%), neurology 73 (4.5%), anesthesiology 30 (1.9%), gastroenterology and urology 27 (1.7%), hematology 22 (1.4%), all other panels 78 (4.8%) — radiology, cardiovascular and neurology together account for 90.3%. Separately, 54.3% (843 devices) of AI 510(k)s come from companies outside the US, with South Korea fifth at 80. 2025 was the biggest year for decisions so far (335), with 181 authorized in 2026 through June 29. AI 510(k)s decided in 2025 took a median of 141 calendar days from FDA receipt to decision; Special 510(k)s a median of 34 days. Only 2 device names on the list mention a PCCP, so the true count using one is unknowable (Complizen analysis, 2026-09-26; page updated 2026-10-27; underlying list at FDA).

Why it matters

These are the denominators for every argument above, and they say two things. First, 96.2% via 510(k) means the overwhelming majority of AI devices reach market on substantial equivalence to an existing device, not on prospective clinical trials — so when AdvaMed says "extend the existing framework," what is being extended is the substantial-equivalence route, and the "reasonably foreseeable use" testing Stanford's Brainstorm lab wants has almost nowhere to attach inside it. Second, only 2 device names mention a PCCP — and the PCCP is precisely the mechanism AdvaMed calls "too restrictive" and wants broadened. Asking for relief on a mechanism almost nobody uses is itself a position worth probing. The 76.2% concentration in radiology also explains why the generative-AI regulatory debate is being held on a blank map: of the 1,614 devices authorized so far, none has conversational output as its primary function.

Discount this

This is a third party's (Complizen) analysis of a public FDA list, not an official FDA statistic, and the list's decisions stop at 2026-06-29, so the full-year 2026 count is not settled. The claim that "none has conversational output as its primary function" is our inference from the 76.2% radiology concentration and the FDA's own position that generative-device regulation is still at the discussion stage — it is not a field one can look up on the list. The "only 2" PCCP figure refers to device-name strings; the original author states the real count is unknowable.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products born in a regulatory gap: one slices autonomy into case counts, the other replaces the visit with five photographs of a face

Doctronic — AI Prescription Refill

Autonomous AI prescription refills · Doctronic (US) · Utah OAIP sandbox

Function and position. Renews routine maintenance medications for existing patients with no clinician directly involved, across a formulary of 191 commonly prescribed drugs (statins, blood-pressure medications, certain psychiatric medications, birth control), excluding narcotics, stimulants, injectables and short-term antibiotics, with the formulary reviewed by independent pharmacists and the state. The buyer is really the state on one side and the self-pay patient on the other: what is sold is not a tool for a hospital but a care path that bypasses the synchronous visit (Fierce Healthcare).

  • Strength : autonomy is gated on case counts, not dates — the first 250 refills under human oversight, then 10% sampling, then random sampling — turning "loosening up" into an auditable milestone rather than a promise (Fierce Healthcare).
  • Strength : monthly state reporting covers both accepted and denied refills alongside user counts and complaints, so errors are tracked in both directions, not just false positives (Fierce Healthcare).
  • Concern : an RMA gives no liability shield, no change to the standard of care, no exemption from federal law — and pharmacies are not parties, so the pharmacist filling an AI-generated order gets no regulatory relief at all (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13).
  • Concern : the missing figure is performance itself. No concordance rate, miss rate or adverse-event count has been published — everything verifiable today is a contract term. Psychiatric medications and birth control are on the formulary, and those are precisely the classes where discontinuation and interaction risk depends most on conversational context.

Nolla Health — Acne Prescribing

Image-scored prescribing without a visit · Nolla Health (US) · Utah OAIP sandbox

Function and position. Prescribing for mild-to-moderate acne without a synchronous visit: severity is scored from five-angle facial images, then treatment is selected from a restricted formulary including compounded topicals. It is the most autonomous of Utah's three pilots — not a refill of an existing drug but an initial treatment choice made by the AI (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13).

  • Strength : the phase gate is the most copyable piece of regulatory design in this issue — at least 95% concordance with physician determinations and zero missed hard stops, splitting "performs well on average" from "never makes the fatal error" into two independent conditions. That is exactly the two-track standard Stanford's Brainstorm lab asked the FDA for (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13; MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03).
  • Strength : the middle state is carefully built — in phase 2 the AI sends prescriptions straight to pharmacists but physicians retrospectively review every case, rather than jumping to sampling; only the final phase drops to at least 10% (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13).
  • Concern : "concordance with physicians" is a moving baseline. It measures fidelity to human judgment, not clinical outcome — and if clinicians already over-treat acne, 95% concordance locks that bias in. There is no endpoint measure anywhere in the gate (clearance rate, adverse-effect rate, referral rate).
  • Concern : the real risk in five-angle photo scoring is not mis-grading acne but treating something that is not acne as acne — and nothing in the public record discloses how differential-diagnosis risk (rosacea, folliculitis, cutaneous malignancy) is handled, nor what is on the hard-stop list. Compounded topicals sit in the US 503A/503B compounding regime outside FDA premarket review; that quality risk has nothing to do with the AI but stacks onto the same pathway.

Today's competitive structure is a triangle whose three corners veto each other. Industry (AdvaMed, GE HealthCare, MDMA) defends 510(k) substantial equivalence and wants a looser PCCP. The consumer corner (Oura) would rather stay outside the device definition altogether. And the fourth player — the states — is not arguing in the docket at all: it is trading measurable gates (95% concordance, zero missed hard stops, oversight of the first 250 cases) for an operating licence via RMA. The asymmetry worth noting is that in the same week the federal layer dropped a static disclosure duty, the only body demanding dynamic performance evidence was an office with jurisdiction over one state and three signed companies.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan has the principles in statute; what it lacks is exactly the three things this week in Washington put on display — risk tiering, liability allocation, a payment path

(1) The gap before the secondary rules arrive is the gap hospitals carry themselves. Taiwan's Artificial Intelligence Basic Act passed its third reading in the Legislative Yuan on 23 December 2025 (Ministry of Digital Affairs press release) and was promulgated and took effect on 14 January 2026 (Lee and Li). The Act already states that government shall ensure management rules for high-risk AI are in place, that high-risk AI products and systems shall carry notices or warnings, and that government shall regulate the allocation of liability and the conditions for attributing it for high-risk AI, and establish remedy, compensation or insurance mechanisms. But the classification framework for "high-risk" is to be set separately by the competent authority: the National Science and Technology Council is the central competent authority, the Ministry of Digital Affairs is to drive the risk-classification framework and help each sector regulator write its rules, and the Executive Yuan is to establish a National AI Strategy Special Committee. So the layer that medical AI actually lands on — the Ministry of Health and Welfare's risk tiering and liability-attribution rules — is still pending. What Washington demonstrated this week is the concrete consequence of such a gap: once ONC lifted source-attribute disclosure out of the certification criteria, the advice lawyers gave hospitals was not to wait for regulation but to write disclosure, validation and bias testing into procurement contracts (MedCity News, 2026-11-03). Taiwan's gap comes from secondary rules not yet written rather than from deregulation, but the position hospitals are in is identical: until the rules land, the procurement contract is the only instrument you control yourself.

(2) The AMA's three-way framework converts directly into a Taiwanese hospital's procurement checklist. Translate the Nov 3 brief's deployer duties into tender and acceptance conditions and they bind without any regulatory authorization: choose the right tool for the clinical scenario (= the specification names indications and excluded populations); evaluate before physicians use it (= an in-house validation report with performance figures on this hospital's own data); provide training (= vendor training hours and audience written into the tender); monitor performance over time (= a periodic performance-reporting obligation and a drift threshold for the contract term); stand up adverse-event processes (= AI-related adverse events folded into the existing patient-safety reporting system). Against the AMA's own survey — about a quarter of physicians with no AI training and 92% wanting more — the training clause is the cheapest and most overlooked of the five (MedTech Dive, 2026-11-03). Nolla Health's RMA gates offer a further template: write "concordance with our own physicians' determinations" and "zero missed hard stops" as two independent acceptance conditions, rather than looking at a single aggregate accuracy figure (Holland & Knight, 2026-10-13).

(3) Payment codes: Taiwan's NHI has no slot for this, and the US is drawing one. Cassidy's draft directs HHS to build a coverage evaluation framework for AI-based services, propose payment methodologies and create a process for using Level II HCPCS billing codes, with CMMI then testing a payment model for AI that delivers care autonomously (Becker's, 2026-10-30). For Taiwan the significance is the lag: if the US settles rates, indications and review conditions for autonomous AI across 2027–2028, Taiwanese medical-AI vendors gain the first comparable dataset to take to the National Health Insurance Administration. Conversely, with no AI payment path before then, in-hospital adoption has to justify itself on labour-cost savings alone — and last week's Jefferson Health numbers showed how thin that argument is (5,234 accounts provisioned, only 1,802 in use, 3.8 minutes saved per physician per day; see this report, 2026-11-01).

(4) Export timing: the European clock for devices has already been pushed back. The EU AI Act Digital Omnibus political agreement (reached 2026-05-06, confirmed by member-state representatives 2026-05-13) defers the high-risk obligations: Annex III stand-alone systems move from 2026-08-02 to 2027-12-02, and Annex I — AI embedded in regulated products, which is where medical devices sit — moves from 2027-08-02 to 2028-08-02. The agreement also lets the Commission limit AI Act requirements that duplicate sectoral legislation such as the medical-device rules, and narrows the "safety component" definition so that AI used only for convenience, optimization or quality control generally falls outside the high-risk category unless its failure would endanger health and safety. Article 50 transparency obligations still apply from 2026-08-02, and the deadline for member states to establish AI regulatory sandboxes is postponed a year to 2027-08-02 (Gibson Dunn, 2026-05-27). For Taiwanese device and medical-AI companies that is an extra year of preparation — but MDR obligations are not deferred with it: the extension moves the AI-specific requirements, not the conditions for putting a device on the market.
