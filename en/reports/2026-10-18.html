Sunday · Weekly Review & Further Reading

Three sets of numbers from this week land on the same line: 952,008 clinicians on the adoption side, 58.1% on the oversight side, and FDA's generative-AI device comment window shutting tomorrow

Sunday, October 18, 2026 · Covering Oct 12–18 · Taipei time

There was no shortage of news this week; what was missing was the angle that puts it side by side. The JAMA study published on 15 Oct was the first peer-reviewed account of how much medical AI is actually being used: 271 million queries from 952,008 NPI-verified US clinicians between January 2024 and June 2026. The systematic review published the next day, 16 Oct, measured the other end of the same system: in a national sample only 58.1% of hospitals monitor anything at all once an AI tool goes live. And the document meant to close the gap between those two figures is still a discussion paper, issued by FDA on 18 August, with comments due 19 October — the window shuts tomorrow. This week's capital, meanwhile, went entirely to the adoption side: Suki's $90M Series E and NVIDIA's stake in Doctolib both landed on 13 Oct.

Story of the week OpenEvidenceJAMAHarvard Medical School10/15

The first peer-reviewed count of how much medical AI is really used: 271 million queries, 952,008 US clinicians — and the heaviest users are the most junior doctors

What

The cross-sectional study published in JAMA on 15 Oct analysed de-identified OpenEvidence query logs from 1 January 2024 to 30 June 2026: 271 million queries from 952,008 NPI-verified US clinicians (556,200 physicians, 171,034 nurse practitioners, 93,241 physician assistants, 35,819 pharmacists). Monthly active users rose from 355 in January 2024 to 703,265 in June 2026; monthly queries from roughly 6,000 to 27.8 million; queries per active user from 18.0 to 39.5 a month; month-over-month retention from about 70% to about 90% (92.5% of June 2026 actives were returning users). The sharpest figure is the seniority split: physicians within ten years of graduation are 24.3% of the physician workforce but filed 61.8% of physician queries. Out-of-specialty querying ran from 35% in haematology-oncology to 88% in emergency medicine; surgeons aimed 70% of their queries at non-surgical topics. JAMA, 2026-10-15 (DOI 10.1001/jama.2026.21868); Becker's, same day.

Why it matters

Before this paper, the answer to how many clinicians use AI came only from vendors. Now there is a lower bound, and its shape matters more than its size: intensity runs inversely to seniority (44.5 queries per active user-month for NPs and PAs, 37.1 for physicians, a use ratio of just 0.31 for those twenty-plus years out), and 51.9% of physician queries sit in clinical decision-making. That is not reference lookup; it is judgement support. Which makes the week's second and third items two faces of one question: judgement support at this volume, with nobody measuring its performance after go-live and the regulatory framework still unwritten.

Discount this

The data came from OpenEvidence; three co-authors (Finlayson, Ray, Zack) are or were company employees or officers, Zack as chief medical officer, and the authors state the company supplied the data and contributed to the analysis. Query content was classified by an LLM (GPT-5.6 Terra) on a stratified random 14.4% sample, validated against only 200 queries labelled by six blinded clinicians. Single platform, main question interface only — the authors call every figure a lower bound — and there is no way to tell whether a query happened during patient care.

Evidence gap Frontiers in Digital HealthUniversity of New MexicoFDA10/16

The other end of the same week: FDA has authorised more than 1,451 AI/ML devices, while only 58.1% of hospitals monitor after go-live and 51.4% have no bias protocol

What

The systematic review and regulatory gap analysis by Conteh et al. in Frontiers in Digital Health (vol. 8, article 1955958, DOI 10.3389/fdgth.2026.1955958) followed PRISMA 2020 to screen 1,065 records down to 22 studies (inter-rater Cohen's kappa 0.84), searching January 2017 to January 2026. It sets FDA's cumulative 1,451-plus authorised AI/ML devices through end-2025 against four structural gaps. Post-market monitoring: in a 2024 national sample only 58.1% of hospitals monitored after implementation, 47.4% never evaluated post-deployment accuracy, 51.4% had no bias assessment protocol and 61% assessed accuracy but not bias. Equity: one readmission model's training data was under 2% Hispanic and Asian and over 75% Caucasian with no subgroup evaluation, and the KATE triage system scored 78.5% in adults against 67.1% in children, an 11.4-point gap. Accountability: 79% of hospitals run predictive models built by EHR vendors, yet no framework assigns responsibility among hospitals, developers, vendors and clinicians. Governance capacity: only 65% of hospitals used predictive models and only 44% assessed bias. Bioengineer.org reposting Scienmag, 2026-10-16/17.

Why it matters

This review supplies the week's most useful contrast, because it holds both the regulatory-grade successes and the post-deployment collapses in one place. The successes are hard numbers: an FDA-authorised pulmonary embolism tool reached 99.9% negative predictive value across 11,736 tests, cut worklist detection time from 7,714 minutes to 87 and missed cases from 44.8% to 2.6%; a sepsis prediction algorithm got antibiotics delivered within 2.76 hours and cut in-hospital mortality from 21.3% to 9.0%, a 58% relative reduction. So are the failures: a COVID-19 chest radiograph tool scoring above 0.95 AUC on open datasets fell to roughly 0.70 in prospective deployment (0.66–0.74 across quartiles over 19 weeks). The difference is not the model; it is whether anyone measures it after go-live. And barely half of US hospitals have the machinery to do that.

Discount this

Twenty-two studies is a small base, and the authors note most were retrospective and single-centre, with only four databases searched, English-language publications only and retrospective protocol registration. The CMS 2022–2025 finding across 70 quality measures, where AI-using hospitals trended better on some indicators, is an observational association the authors explicitly decline to call causal. Studies from Canada, Saudi Arabia and the Netherlands were used for context, not as direct evidence on US regulatory performance. This report cites the Scienmag/Bioengineer press coverage; the journal full text was not retrieved.

Closes tomorrow FDACDRHGenAI devices10/19

The document meant to close that gap shuts its comment window tomorrow — and it says of itself that it is neither a draft nor a final guidance

What

FDA issued its discussion paper on a regulatory approach for generative-AI-enabled medical devices on 18 August 2026 and opened a public docket, with comments due 19 October 2026 — the day after this report goes out. Its premarket approach rests on a competency-assessment metaphor explicitly modelled on physician training and credentialing, split into non-clinical device benchmarking and clinical confirmation, alongside a two-axis risk framework and discussion covering foundation models and agentic AI systems. On the postmarket side it only explores several possible risk-proportionate monitoring approaches. MD+DI, 2026-08-19; Maynard Nexsen analysis; Covington client alert (PDF).

Why it matters

Place this between the two items above and the week's structure is clear: usage is already in the millions, post-market monitoring covers barely half of hospitals, and the document joining the two does not even carry guidance force. Note too that the only LLM-enabled device so far through 510(k), UpDoc (K253281, cleared 23 December 2025), came through under the product code "Calculator, Drug Dose" (21 CFR 868.1890, Class II) with Hygieia's d-Nav System (K181916) as predicate — meaning the generative components did not trigger De Novo and the dosing logic stayed deterministic and clinician-configured. The system has not yet opened a path for generative AI; products are already walking through the existing one.

Discount this

This item is drawn from secondary legal analysis and trade press; the FDA document itself was not read line by line, and MD+DI gives neither the docket number nor the total count of questions. UpDoc's "first" label comes from the company's own press release of 25 June 2026, which Innolitics states it did not verify, noting it cannot tell whether that release refers to K253281 or to a newer submission not yet in the public database, and that the public record does not identify the underlying foundation model.

Governance AMAAgentic AI10/13

Where the federal answer is still missing, the AMA wrote its own: clinical authority must be earned through evidence and maintained through demonstrated performance

What

On 13 October the American Medical Association published a governance framework for agentic AI, whose core claim is that an AI system's clinical authority must be earned through evidence and maintained through ongoing performance, with authority and oversight rising or falling as evidence, performance, risk or the underlying technology change. It runs on four questions. Function: which clinical task, on a span from documentation and monitoring to triage, diagnosis, treatment and prescribing. Evidence and risk: what demonstrates the system can do it and what failure costs — higher stakes and greater autonomy demand stronger evidence. Authority and oversight: which actions it may take and under what conditions, from offering input to making and executing decisions. Accountability: who bears the risk and the outcome, with responsibility tracking authority, control, knowledge of risk and ability to prevent harm — developers, deployers, health systems, physicians or payers. The AMA explicitly defines licensure here as authorising an AI system to operate in a defined clinical role, not a licence to practise medicine. Becker's, 2026-10-13; MedTech Dive; Fierce Healthcare.

Why it matters

The AMA framework and FDA's discussion paper landed in the same week on the same metaphor — credentialing, competency assessment — with one difference: the AMA builds revocability into the design, while FDA has not yet answered whether its postmarket levers even sit inside existing statutory authority. The substance of governance is migrating from the federal level to professional bodies and health systems themselves. For vendors, the gate ahead may not be a 510(k) but each health system's own authority tiers and reassessment cycle.

Discount this

The AMA framework carries no legal force and defines no formal tiers or numeric thresholds — Becker's notes explicitly that the document describes a continuum of authority and a continuous cycle rather than codified levels. There are no statistics in it; it is a principles document.

On 13 Oct, OpenEvidence and Johns Hopkins Medicine announced a collaboration spanning two areas: medical education — designing a training model that reinforces core scientific and clinical reasoning and building adaptive AI systems to support continuing learning — and clinical care, developing tools that assemble the best available evidence, account for an individual patient's history and draw on JHM's established practices. Proofpoint's 2026 healthcare cyber report, released this week, surveyed 705 IT and IT-security practitioners: 97% of organisations faced at least one cyberattack in the past twelve months, and among those attacked 74% reported subsequent disruption to patient care. 02 — Product Analysis

Two products this week stand at opposite ends of the gap: one had its scale measured by peer review, the other is betting money on what comes after scale

OpenEvidence

Clinical question-answering and evidence platform · OpenEvidence (US) · free to verified US clinicians

Function and position. Clinicians ask in natural language and get a synthesised, cited answer. This week's JAMA study gives the shape of its use: 51.9% of physician queries serve clinical decision-making, 33.1% concern treatment and management, 14.1% medication information, 13.9% diagnosis — and out-of-specialty queries run 35% to 88%. Its real battleground is not looking things up inside one's own specialty but handling patients outside one's range. On the business model, both the release and the study state it is free to verified US clinicians, JAMA, 2026-10-15.

  • Strength : retention. Month-over-month retention rose from about 70% to about 90%, 92.5% of June 2026 actives were returning users, and only 9.6% of clinicians queried on a single day and never came back — a curve rarely seen in clinical tools, JAMA study.
  • Strength : institutional legitimacy. The 13 Oct collaboration with Johns Hopkins Medicine plugs straight into medical education, JHM release.
  • Concern : the study that measured the scale drew both its data and several authors from the company itself (Zack is its chief medical officer), and it measures no outcomes at all — no accuracy, no clinical endpoints, no error rate. Usage and effectiveness are different things, and this week only established the first.
  • Concern : the seniority distribution cuts both ways. Physicians within ten years of graduation are 24.3% of the workforce and file 61.8% of queries — the strongest growth engine and also the crux of the debate at the Endpoints panel on 15 Oct (Pandita of UCI Health and Rodman of Beth Israel Deaconess): whether clinical reasoning gets trained or outsourced. No longitudinal data answers it yet.

Suki

Ambient clinical documentation and agent platform · Suki (US) · embedded-channel first

Function and position. The core is ambient dictation and clinical documentation, but this week's $90M Series E names its use of funds plainly: AI agents for referrals and prior authorisation. Its biggest strategic difference from Abridge, Ambience and Nabla is channel — more than 30 technology partners embed Suki inside their own platforms (Optum, Zoom, athenahealth) rather than signing health systems one by one, while the company also claims over 450 healthcare organisations on its clinician-facing app, Business Wire, 2026-10-13.

  • Strength : channel diffusion speed. Partner-business revenue up more than tenfold in two years and encounters supported up more than 190% year over year in 2026 are both direct consequences of the embedded route, company release.
  • Strength : a citable third-party economics figure. KLAS-validated financial impact of more than $2,600 per provider per month is one of the few numbers in this lane that is not purely self-reported, Healthcare Dive.
  • Concern : rented customer relationships are shallow. For a Suki embedded inside Optum or athenahealth, the switching decision belongs to the partner, not to the physician using it; the CEO's stated plan to go deeper with existing partners is, in part, an acknowledgement of that.
  • Concern : referrals and prior authorisation are exactly where an agent makes real decisions, and that falls precisely in the cell that both the AMA framework and FDA's discussion paper left unsettled this week. The company's release says nothing at all about agent oversight, error rates or accountability — and where a figure is missing it should be said: here all of them are.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The gap measured in the US this week is one Taiwan built institutions for back in 2024; what needs checking now is whether those institutions are working

(1) The evidence-generation and reimbursement links the US is missing are ones Taiwan named two years ago. On 7 October 2024 the Ministry of Health and Welfare launched three AI centres: a Responsible AI Implementation Centre (management rules, information security and privacy, publication of model, data and performance information, explainability analysis, model lifecycle management); a Clinical AI Evidence Validation Centre, run with the Ministry's Department of Information Technology and the TFDA, linking hospital networks to validate models and speed review; and an AI Impact Research Centre, run with the Department of Information Technology and the National Health Insurance Administration, running cross-system clinical trials to assess clinical benefit and set a scientific pricing basis for reimbursement. The subsidy programme was announced on 23 July 2024 and closed on 1 August; 30 hospitals submitted 48 proposals, and after three rounds of review by domestic and international committee members 16 hospitals and 19 proposals were selected, including National Taiwan University Hospital, Taichung Veterans General, Taipei Veterans General, National Cheng Kung University Hospital, China Medical University Hospital, Tri-Service General Hospital and Linkou Chang Gung, MOHW release, 2024-10-07. Lay that architecture over the four gaps in this week's Frontiers review and the mapping is almost one to one: post-market monitoring to the Responsible AI centre, equity and subgroup performance to the Validation centre, reimbursement and clinical benefit to the Impact Research centre. What Taiwan lacks is not institutional design but published execution data — two years in, how many models have completed validation, how many have entered reimbursement, and what share of deployments are monitored after go-live are all unavailable in public sources. The US review carries weight precisely because it can produce ugly numbers like 58.1%, 47.4% and 51.4%.

(2) The draft medical implementation rules under the AI Basic Act were due in exactly these weeks. On 6 August 2026 the Ministry said a draft of AI implementation rules for the medical field under the AI Basic Act was expected within three months — which lands around November, meaning now. The same report put FHIR Box's progress as: three medical centres piloting in 2025, launch at end of 2026 with records interoperable across Taiwan's medical centres, and expansion to roughly 80% of medical institutions nationwide within three to five years, set against an EU approach the report describes as taking about ten years and a note that rebuilding one hospital's record system alone could cost over NT$10 billion, United Daily News, 2026-08-06. Converting this week's international news to Taiwanese scale: the AMA's 13 Oct framework addresses authority tiers and reassessment; FDA's paper closing on 19 Oct addresses premarket competency assessment and postmarket monitoring. If Taiwan's forthcoming draft states principles only — without saying who measures after go-live, what they measure and how often — it will reproduce the very gap the US had measured this week: complete institutions, empty execution. The practical implication for industry is that the window for comment is now, not after the draft appears.

(3) One concrete domestic comparison. What this means in this week's context: Taiwan can build products to the point of exporting intellectual property, but the report contains neither any clearance or certification nor any clinical outcome data, and testing in the Ministry's data centre is not evidence generation. That is precisely what the validation link among the three AI centres exists to produce — if it is working, the next news item about products like this should be validation results, not a target number of hospitals.
