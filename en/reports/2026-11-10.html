The capital market repriced itself this week, and the money in medical AI went to distribution

Tuesday, November 10, 2026 · Covering Nov 2–10 · Taipei time

The cleanest thread this week was not in a hospital but on a price sheet. On Nov 8–9, OpenAI closed a first $11B tranche at a $1.25T valuation, below its earlier target of roughly $1.4T; at the same time investors cited by the FT put Anthropic's IPO valuation at $1.25–1.75T, against the roughly $2T discussed in October. On the medical side, not one new model-scale deal all week — the money went to distribution and the back office instead: on Nov 9 AWS announced a five-year, $100M Health Impact Initiative that pays in cloud credits capped at $10,000 to start; on Nov 5 HCA pushed a Google-co-built nurse handoff tool to about 100 hospitals in two months; and both of the week's acquisitions bought EHR access, neither at a disclosed price. The public market filed the invoice in the same days: Doximity grew revenue 4% while its adjusted EBITDA margin slid from 59.8% to 50.9%.

Big Tech AWS / AmazonHealth Impact Initiative11/09

AWS's "$100M for health AI" is five years of cloud credits, with a $10,000 cap to get started

What

On Nov 9, AWS announced its Health Impact Initiative (HII): a five-year, $100M commitment of AI, agentic services and cloud infrastructure. It pays in promotional credits — sized to each organization's estimated need via the AWS Pricing Calculator, or up to $10,000 to get started, valid 12 months and extendable to 24, governed by AWS Promotional Credit terms. First awardees include Eve's Ark (women's health data preservation, over a petabyte), Admera Health (genomic sequencing), Care 2 Communities (primary care in Haiti; 350,000 people served, nearly 120,000 visits a year), India's Akshaya Patra Foundation (over 2.35 million children fed daily) and U.S. Hunger. Its predecessor, the 2021 Health Equity Initiative, put in $60M across 400-plus awardees, supporting over 600 health organizations in 80-plus countries.

Why it matters

This is not philanthropy, it is a distribution subsidy: the $100M is denominated in AWS's own list prices, and the money spent flows back to the cloud that issued the credits. The previous round averaged about $150,000 per awardee ($60M across 400-plus over five years); this one opens at $10,000, which makes the unit a proof of concept, not a deployment. Set against Rock Health's $31.0M average deal size in 2026, HII is not competing with venture capital for capital — it is competing for which cloud the next generation of health applications is built on.

Discount this

The "$100M" is a five-year credit ceiling, not cash, and AWS does not say how it is divided across awards or how much is actually drawn down. Beyond the $10,000 opening cap, no per-award figure is published.

Distribution HCA HealthcareGoogle11/05

HCA is pushing a nurse handoff AI to about 100 of its 189 hospitals in two months — its fastest rollout ever

What

On Nov 5, Becker's reported that HCA Healthcare, which runs 189 hospitals, is rolling a Google-co-developed nurse handoff AI to about 100 hospitals over the next two months, expanding from two divisions to eight, with tens of thousands of nurses getting access — the company's fastest technology rollout to date. In earlier single-site testing nurses rated the tool 86% accurate and 90% helpful; chief clinical officer Michael Schlosser said handoff-related safety events and near misses have "basically gone to zero in our pilot sites." Becker's separately reported the same week that HCA expects roughly 10,000 physicians on ambient documentation by year-end.

Why it matters

The model is Google's; the asset is the 189 hospitals. Every dollar that moved this week repeats the same judgment: what is scarce in medical AI is not model capability but the organization that can change how tens of thousands of nurses hand off patients within two months. How many years would an OpenEvidence-shaped startup need to reach 100,000 nurses? HCA did it in two months.

Discount this

The 86% and 90% are nurse self-ratings at a single hospital, not independently audited accuracy. "Basically gone to zero" is a spoken remark by an executive, with no denominator, no time window and no peer-reviewed safety-event count. The whole item comes from Becker's interview at a CEO+CFO roundtable; HCA published no corresponding release or dataset.

Capital markets OpenAIAnthropic11/08–09

OpenAI's first $11B closed at $1.25T against a roughly $1.4T target; Anthropic's IPO valuation fell from about $2T to $1.25–1.75T

What

OpenAI closed the first $11B of a new round at a $1.25T valuation, with Coatue and T. Rowe Price participating and a reported possible expansion to roughly $20B — a price below its earlier target of about $1.4T. At the same time, investors cited by the FT put Anthropic's IPO at $1.25–1.75T raising $80–100B, below the roughly $2T discussed in October, while Bloomberg reports buyers talking $1.1–1.3T. The backdrop is the early-November selloff — and in the same days Anthropic bought commitments for 1.7GW of gas-fired generation for $3.4B and Google bought Crusoe's unfinished Wyoming site outright for a planned $17B campus, so the capex denominator keeps growing.

Why it matters

Medical AI's 2026 valuations were all marked against frontier-lab prices: OpenEvidence at $15B in September, Abridge at $5.3B, Neko Health at roughly $7B were each set on the assumption that the frontier would keep getting dearer. That assumption was cut 10–40% this week. Worth noting alongside it: digital health has had no new listing since HeartFlow in August 2025, and Oura has reportedly postponed its IPO — the only exit left was M&A, and neither of this week's two deals disclosed a price.

Discount this

Both come from unnamed investors relayed by the press: OpenAI's full round is not closed and Anthropic's IPO is not priced, so $1.25–1.75T is a range, not a transaction. Bloomberg's and the FT's figures do not agree, and this report sets them side by side rather than picking one.

M&A Ellipsis HealthKouper Health11/06

Ellipsis Health buys Kouper Health: a voice AI company buying write access into Epic, Oracle and athenahealth — price undisclosed

What

On Nov 6, Ellipsis Health announced its acquisition of Kouper Health with terms undisclosed. Ellipsis's voice care manager, Sage, is built on a vocal-biomarker and conversational engine the company says was trained on more than 3 million clinically labeled patient interactions, automating care-management enrollment, PHQ-9/GAD-7 assessments, medication adherence checks and post-discharge outreach. Kouper Health, founded in 2022 and backed by CVS Health Ventures, General Catalyst and 25madison, does native EHR integration and longitudinal tracking — stitching ER, inpatient, primary, specialty and home-health touchpoints into continuous journeys and closing the loop on scheduling inside host systems such as Epic, Oracle Health and athenahealth. The same report cites a figure that 75% of U.S. health systems have deployed at least one AI solution, up from 59% in 2025.

Why it matters

A company with a model bought a company with an integration. This is the most stable shape in medical AI M&A this year: model capability can be rented, and foundation-model vendors keep cutting its price, but the right to book a follow-up appointment inside Epic has no substitute. Once Sage finishes a PHQ-9 by phone, it has to write the result back to the chart and get the visit on the calendar — otherwise it is just a phone call.

Discount this

No price, no customer counts, no revenue on either side, so there is no way to judge what EHR access is worth on the market. "More than 3 million clinically labeled interactions" is a training-set size, not a performance figure, and nothing in the deal materials supports vocal biomarkers for clinical distress with a prospective trial. The 75%/59% pair is the article's own figure, with no survey or sample identified.

M&A LeanTaaSLookDeep Health11/03

The week's second deal: LeanTaaS buys LookDeep Health to wire in-room vision AI into hospital capacity operations

What

On Nov 3, LeanTaaS announced its acquisition of LookDeep Health, adding bedside computer vision that tracks in-room conditions to its existing hospital capacity and scheduling tools. Terms were not disclosed. LeanTaaS's business is optimizing OR, clinic and inpatient-bed capacity; LookDeep's is in-room visual monitoring. It is the week's second deal in which an operations-and-workflow incumbent bought a perception layer.

Why it matters

Both deals point the same way, and it is the counter-intuitive one: the buyers are companies already inside hospital workflow, and the targets are perception and models. In this market, technology that can see the patient is cheaper than a position that can be scheduled into the process. The implication for startups is blunt: if your moat is only a model or a sensor, you are the one being bought, and the price will not be published.

Discount this

Again no price, no deployment counts, no revenue on either side. This item rests on HIT Consultant's listing; no primary release from LeanTaaS or LookDeep was found, and neither the integration timeline nor the product roadmap is public.

Earnings Doximity (DOCS)Ask / DoseCheck11/05

Doximity put its AI into 190-plus health systems and got 4% revenue growth and nine points off its EBITDA margin

What

On Nov 5, Doximity reported fiscal Q2 2027 (quarter ended Sept 30, 2026): revenue $174.5M, up 4% from $168.5M; GAAP net income $34.7M against $62.1M, a 19.9% margin versus 36.8%; adjusted EBITDA $88.9M, down 12%, with the margin falling from 59.8% to 50.9%; non-GAAP diluted EPS $0.36 against $0.45; free cash flow $91.3M, essentially flat; and $54.2M of buybacks in the quarter versus $21.9M a year earlier. The company says more than 190 health systems have purchased its AI suite (Ask, formerly DoxGPT; DoseCheck; and the UpToDate partnership), on a network covering over 85% of U.S. physicians. Fiscal 2027 guidance was raised to $678–683M revenue and $317–325M adjusted EBITDA. Revenue beat estimates and the stock still dropped.

Why it matters

This is the cleanest income statement yet for "give physicians AI free, charge somewhere else." Four percent revenue growth against nine points of lost EBITDA margin says the AI features add cost to an existing high-margin advertising business rather than price. Note what the company disclosed: 190-plus health systems have purchased — a count, not a sum. If the AI suite were contributing revenue, this was the quarter to put a number on it. Against the pharma-advertising model this report covered last week at OpenEvidence, Doximity is what that same cash flow looks like once it is publicly audited.

Discount this

Outlets label the quarter inconsistently: Doximity's fiscal Q2 2027 is calendar Q3 2026, and some coverage writes "Q3 CY2026" — same figures, different label. The 190-plus health systems is a company statement with no dollar amount attached, and adjusted EBITDA excludes stock compensation, which accounts for most of the gap between $88.9M and $34.7M of GAAP net income.

Earnings Tempus AI (TEM)Data & Applications11/02

Tempus grew 23%, and its best gross margin was not diagnostics (62.4%) but the data it sells to pharma (71.5%)

What

On Nov 2, Tempus reported Q3 2026: total revenue $411.5M, up 23% from $334.2M. Diagnostics was $303.5M, up 20% (oncology volume up 32%, hereditary revenue up just 3%, MRD at roughly 12,000 tests, up 300%); Data and Applications was $108.1M, up 33%. Gross profit was $266.7M, up 27%, for a 64.8% GAAP gross margin overall — split into 71.5% for Data and Applications and 62.4% for Diagnostics. Net loss narrowed to $14.9M from $80.0M a year earlier, and adjusted EBITDA was $25.6M against $1.5M. Full-year 2026 revenue guidance was raised to $1.60–1.605B, about 25% growth, with adjusted EBITDA guidance unchanged at roughly $65M. Data-licensing bookings passed $550M year to date, including a BMS oncology data license of up to $137.5M over five years and a BMS/Pathos dementia-related license of $55M over five years. Analysts raised price targets afterwards.

Why it matters

Tempus is the largest public sample in medical AI, and its income statement says the same thing every quarter: the side that makes money is not at the bedside, it is in pharma's procurement department. Data and Applications is 26% of revenue but carries the highest gross margin and grows faster (33% versus 20%). That explains why every dollar this week went to distribution and integration — distribution produces data, and data produces the 71.5%. Hereditary revenue up 3% against MRD volume up 300% is the other lesson: what AI does in the clinic and what the company grows on are two different questions.

Discount this

The $25.6M of adjusted EBITDA excludes stock compensation and other items, and the company is still at a GAAP net loss. The $550M-plus of data-licensing is bookings, not recognized revenue, and both the $137.5M and $55M BMS figures are five-year ceilings. MRD's 300% growth comes off a very small base of roughly 12,000 tests and should not be extrapolated.

Business-model risk CMS WISeRVirtix Health11/03

The one medical AI model paid on the money it denies: a 53% first-quarter denial rate in Washington, and four of six vendors flagged

What

CMS's WISeR (Wasteful and Inappropriate Services Reduction) demonstration launched in six states in early 2026, outsourcing traditional Medicare prior-authorization review to private technology vendors — and paying those vendors a share of the healthcare spending they avert. CMS documents released by Rep. Suzan DelBene on Sept 16 show that Virtix Health, the Washington contractor, issued over 6,000 prior-authorization decisions through the end of March with a 53% denial rate, while the Arizona contractor denied under 20% of 14,300 decisions in the same period. CMS actuarial staff wrote in a 2025 assessment that "model participants will have an incentive to deny as many claims as possible," projecting claims reduced by 10% to 22.5%. Follow-up reporting on Nov 3 says four of the six vendors were flagged in the program's first weeks. Virtix says its affirmation rate has since improved to closer to 70%.

Why it matters

Put this next to the other seven items and the difference is immediate: AWS sells compute, HCA buys time, Tempus sells data, Doximity sells attention — WISeR is the only model where vendor revenue comes directly from saying no. The state-to-state contrast (53% versus under 20%) raises something harder still: under one set of rules, two vendors' readings differ by a factor of two and a half, which leaves the central selling point — that AI review is more consistent — unproven on its own terms. EFF obtained these records through litigation and disclosure requests, not a CMS publication.

Discount this

The 53% is a first-quarter figure through end of March; the vendor says it has improved to an affirmation rate closer to 70%, but no complete post-improvement data is public, and neither is the share of denials overturned on appeal. "Four of six flagged" comes from a Nov 3 secondary reading of the records, not a CMS announcement, and the Arizona contractor is unnamed in the released documents.

Putting the week back on a quarterly scale: $11.1B across 358 U.S. deals this year, $31.0M average, and no new IPO since August 2025

What

Rock Health's Q3 2026 market update, published Oct 9: U.S. digital health startups raised $3.7B across 114 deals in the third quarter, and $11.1B across 358 deals year to date, at a 2026 average deal size of $31.0M against $29.5M in 2025 and $20.6M in 2024. M&A ran 45 deals in Q3 and 164 year to date, versus 172 through Q3 2025. On exits, there has been no new public listing since HeartFlow in August 2025, and Oura has reportedly postponed its IPO. Deal counts at a decade low with deal sizes at a high means the money has not shrunk — the number of companies receiving it has. That is exactly the shape of this week: no startup megaround, but two acquisitions, one cloud subsidy and two earnings reports. In the fifteenth month without an IPO, M&A is the only exit, and that exit comes without a price — which leaves early-stage valuations anchored to frontier-lab prices, and frontier prices were cut this week. The loop closes.

Discount this

Rock Health counts U.S. startups only and CB Insights counts globally, so the two series are not directly comparable and are set side by side here. Rock Health's "could reach $14.8B for the year" is an extrapolation from the Q3 average, not a result. Oura's postponed IPO is reported secondhand.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products, two kinds of free: one free to physicians and billed to pharma and health systems, one free to patients and billed to care-management budgets

Doximity AI Suite（Ask / DoseCheck / UpToDate）

Clinical Q&A and dose checking embedded in a physician network · Doximity (US, NYSE: DOCS)

Function and position. Ask (formerly DoxGPT) is clinical Q&A for physicians, DoseCheck handles dose checking, and a UpToDate partnership brings in evidence content. The products are free to physicians, with revenue from pharma marketing and health-system subscriptions; the company says more than 190 health systems have purchased the AI suite on a network covering over 85% of U.S. physicians.

  • Strength : the distribution is already paid for. With 85% of U.S. physicians on the network, the marginal acquisition cost of one more AI feature is close to zero — which is what OpenEvidence's $15B valuation is buying and Doximity already owns.
  • Strength : the cash is still there. Free cash flow was $91.3M, essentially flat, with $54.2M of buybacks in the quarter — the AI spend comes out of a business that still generates cash, not out of a funding round.
  • Concern : no AI revenue figure. The company gave a count (190-plus) and no dollars, while in the same quarter adjusted EBITDA margin fell nine points and the stock dropped on a revenue beat. The missing number is precisely the one that would settle whether the model works.
  • Concern : pharma-funded clinical Q&A carries a structural bias risk. This report's Nov 6 edition cited the Nature Health study of 883,403 answers, in which branded drugs were 20.5 times likelier than generics to be recommended — Doximity's revenue source points the same way as that bias, and the quarter's filing contains no corresponding audit disclosure.

Ellipsis Health「Sage」+ Kouper Health

Voice care manager plus native EHR navigation · Ellipsis Health (US, post-acquisition)

Function and position. Sage phones patients using a vocal-biomarker and conversational engine, handling care-management enrollment, PHQ-9/GAD-7 assessments, medication adherence checks and post-discharge outreach; folding in Kouper adds closed-loop scheduling and longitudinal journey tracking inside Epic, Oracle Health and athenahealth. The buyers are health systems' and payers' care-management budgets.

  • Strength : after the deal the workflow goes from "make a call" to "make the call, write the result to the chart and book the follow-up," with the loop closing inside the host EHR — the minimum condition for care management to be billable.
  • Strength : Kouper's investor list includes CVS Health Ventures, which is distribution on the payer side, not only the provider side.
  • Concern : vocal biomarkers for clinical distress come with no prospective trial and no third-party validation in the deal materials. "More than 3 million clinically labeled interactions" is a training-set size, not a sensitivity, specificity or outcome — what is missing is missing.
  • Concern : no price was disclosed, and neither were revenues or customer counts on either side, so there is no way to value this voice-plus-integration combination on the market — or to tell whether Ellipsis did the deal from a position of strength or weakness.

04 — Taiwan Angle

America spent this week on distribution and integration; Taiwan's counterparts are a national deployment budget and a reimbursement rule not yet settled

(1) AWS's $100M is cloud credits; Taiwan's counterpart buys deployment inside hospitals. Set HII's five-year $100M — in credits, capped at $10,000 to start — beside the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Healthy Taiwan Deep Cultivation Program, at US$1.5 billion, spent on actual deployment at medical centers including Chang Gung, Kaohsiung Medical University Chung-Ho, Mackay, National Taiwan University Hospital, Taichung Veterans General and Taipei Veterans General. That program reports 85 medical AI solutions cleared by the FDA or Taiwan's TFDA, covering over 14 million patient visits a year, with Foxconn's Nurabot claimed to save frontline nurses roughly two to three hours a day. The difference is not the sum but the unit of account: cloud list prices on one side, nursing hours on the other. The message for Taiwanese vendors is that a subsidy like HII will not produce revenue, but it will decide which cloud the next generation of systems grows on.

(2) WISeR's incentive structure is the one Taiwan must avoid before AI enters claims review. WISeR outsourced review and paid vendors a share of averted spending; the result was two states' denial rates differing by a factor of two and a half under one rule set, with CMS's own actuaries writing in 2025 that "participants will have an incentive to deny as many claims as possible." Taiwan's institutional position is different — a single payer, with review inside the NHIA — but the shape is identical: as long as the performance metric hangs on money saved, the optimal move for an AI is to deny more. The AI-Driven Healthy Taiwan forum at NCKU on Nov 20, where NHIA Director-General Chen Liang-yu speaks on NHI sustainability and Minister of Health and Welfare Shih Chung-liang opens, is exactly the venue for the question: when AI enters reimbursement review, which side does the metric hang on?

(3) Both acquisitions tell Taiwanese vendors the moat is HIS/PACS integration, not the model. Ellipsis bought Kouper for write access into Epic, Oracle and athenahealth, and LeanTaaS bought LookDeep to wire perception into existing scheduling — in both cases incumbent workflow consumed new perception. Taiwan's health IT market is more fragmented than America's, with a high share of medical centers running HIS systems they built themselves, and that is simultaneously bad news and good news for local vendors: a model license can be replaced by anyone, but wiring a workflow into a given medical center's HIS and passing its security and process review is something a foreign buyer cannot take away — and it is the only asset local vendors can compound.
