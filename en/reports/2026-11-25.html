Wednesday · Regulation & Policy

The government wrote down how it will pay, and left the question of who answers blank — in one week, the same federal government issued a roadmap for paying for SI-led care and an audit finding that not one chatbot has ever been authorised

Wednesday, November 25, 2026 · Covering Nov 13–24 · Taipei time

Today's material is not a press release; it is the government's own paperwork. Published on 13 November on a CMS domain, A Clinical Super Intelligence Roadmap sets the federal position on clinical AI out as four pillars and one test — "better than today" — and then says how the money will flow: the CMS Innovation Center is to design participation pathways for SI-led care, the ACCESS model is to extend into primary care, and parts of the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit could be delivered through SI tools. The efficacy evidence the same document leans on is, as Becker's seven notes make plain, almost entirely vendor- and single-site-reported. Seven days later GAO published GAO-27-109184 on 20 November, on the kind of medical AI Americans actually use most: mental health chatbots, used by nearly one in five 12-to-21-year-olds in 2025, for which FDA, as of November 2026, "has not granted marketing authorization for any chatbots for mental health care." Rules for paying on one side, no authorisations at all on the other — that is the shape of today.

01 — Top Stories

Eight items on one axis: how fast the state is opening pathways, against how fast it is assigning accountability.
Federal policy CMSHHSFDA11/13

A Clinical Super Intelligence Roadmap: four pillars, a "better than today" test, and the first written commitment to pay for SI-led care

What

Published on 13 November, A Clinical Super Intelligence Roadmap: Better Answers, Faster Care, Lower Costs names the White House, HHS, CMS, FDA and ARPA-H (through the ADVOCATE programme), and rests on four pillars: Earn Public Trust, Build the Data Foundation, Establish Clear Rules of the Road, and Pay for What Works. Its central test is that clinical SI must meet a "better than today" standard for safety, effectiveness, access and outcomes rather than perfection; the document also states that it "does not mandate SI adoption" and creates no new agency. The payment section is the most concrete: extend the ACCESS model announced in December 2025 into primary care, deliver parts of the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit through SI tools, design SI-led care structures inside CMS Innovation Center models (with required escalation to human clinicians), and open SI-led insurance options in Medicare Advantage and the marketplaces. The AMA's national advocacy update of 20 November confirms both the 13 November release date and those four priorities.

Why it matters

Until now, US policy papers on clinical AI have been about how to review it. This is the first to give a quarter of its length to how to pay for it, and it widens the payable object from "a physician using AI" to "SI-led, human on standby." For vendors, that means a revenue model that for the first time need not run through CPT codes and physician work, but can attach directly to outcomes. For delivery systems, it means that from 2027 the competitor may not be another hospital but a model licensed to sell insurance inside Medicare Advantage. Nextgov's 16 November report flags four things the roadmap touches but has not built: partnership with state sandboxes, risk-based rules, post-market surveillance, and clarifying liability when AI harms a patient.

Discount this

Almost none of the clinical efficacy evidence this policy document cites has been independently audited: OpenAI o3 ranking the final diagnosis first in 60% of complex cases against 15.9% for internal medicine physicians; Microsoft MAI-DxO getting 85.5% of challenging cases right against roughly 20% for practising physicians; a Cedars-Sinai system giving optimal recommendations in 77.1% of real cases against 67.1% for physicians. Every one of those is developer- or single-site-reported. The only independently gathered figure in the document is a December 2022 Pew finding that 60% of US adults are uncomfortable with providers relying on it for diagnosis or treatment — four years old, and pointing the other way. Two versions of the roadmap sit on the same page, with wording drifting between AI and "SI (formerly AI)"; the document carries no issuance date of its own, and 13 November comes from the AMA and Becker's accounts.

Audit & accountability GAOFDAGAO-27-10918411/20

GAO: nearly one in five Americans aged 12 to 21 use chatbots for mental health, and FDA has authorised none of them

What

Published on 20 November, GAO-27-109184, Science & Tech Spotlight: AI Chatbots and Mental Health, records three things. First, "Nearly one-fifth of Americans aged 12 to 21 reported using an AI chatbot for mental health advice in 2025." Second, as of November 2026 FDA "has not granted marketing authorization for any chatbots for mental health care," while two companies have advertised an FDA designation for accelerated review — which GAO notes does not guarantee future authorisation. Third, the central technical risk is sycophancy, the tendency to agree with and flatter the user, and "There is no universal off switch": reducing it requires users to change their own settings or prompts and companies to change system prompts, reward signals and context framing. CASRAI's 21 November summary adds the institutional detail: these platforms fall outside HIPAA, so the chat record has almost no statutory protection.

Why it matters

GAO makes no policy recommendation; it hands legislators four questions, the last of which is "Who is responsible for negative outcomes, and how should they be held accountable?" Read beside the roadmap above, those four questions are the real content of the week: in the same seven days, the federal government designed payment pathways for SI-led care and conceded that in the highest-volume category of all, even the question of who answers for harm is open. The implication travels: "they use it in the United States" is not evidence that a patient-facing conversational medical AI has been through any regulator at all.

Discount this

GAO's Spotlight series is a short technology explainer, not an audit: no recommendations, no agency comment. GAO's own highlights page gives neither an exact percentage nor a sample size behind "nearly one-fifth," and the secondary summaries' "about 20%" is a rounding of it. The text and takeaways cited here come from GAO's product page and highlights; the full report PDF was not read page by page.

Federal vs states FDAColoradoTexasxAI

Buried in the roadmap: express preemption of state requirements — just as the states' health-AI laws take effect on 1 January 2027

What

Under the pillar "Establish Clear Rules of the Road," the roadmap says FDA may adopt a competency-based premarket approach for certain SI devices and that some of those actions "may include express preemption of state requirements" — note four of Becker's seven, 16 November. The sentence lands on a battlefield a year old. Per a preemption tracker current to 13 November, Executive Order 14365, signed 11 December 2025 (90 FR 58499), ordered a DOJ AI Litigation Task Force, which the Attorney General created by memorandum on 9 January 2026; xAI sued over Colorado's AI Act (SB 24-205) in April 2026 (xAI v. Weiser, No. 1:26-cv-01515, D. Colo.), DOJ moved to intervene on 24 April, the court suspended enforcement on 27 April, and the merits remain undecided. Colorado replaced the statute with SB 26-189 in May 2026, effective 1 January 2027, and Manatt's health AI policy tracker notes the replacement broadens exemptions for HIPAA-covered entities and FDA-regulated devices. Texas HB 149 (TRAIGA) has applied since 1 January 2026 and carries an AI disclosure duty in healthcare settings; New York's RAISE Act takes effect alongside Colorado's on 1 January 2027.

Why it matters

Because the federal government has no authorisation route, as GAO says, the states built their own: Pennsylvania sued Character Technologies in May 2026 under its Medical Practice Act, arguing that a chatbot posing as a licensed psychiatrist amounted to unauthorised practice of medicine, also per Manatt's tracker. If FDA does write express preemption into a competency-based pathway, it would close the state route while the federal route has yet to authorise a single conversational mental health product. That is the highest-stakes sentence in today's issue — and it sits inside a document that promises no new agency and no mandate.

Discount this

"May include" is a modal: the roadmap names no device class, no state statute and no timetable. The preemption chronology and state effective dates here come from two law-firm and tracker compilations (current to 13 November and 30 July respectively), not from the opinions, the state statutes or the Federal Register; the tracker itself notes that firms disagree on the RAISE Act's effective date and that the enacted text should be checked.

Terminology FDAEO 1443411/16

From 16 November FDA writes SI instead of AI in public documents — and says on the same page that this adds no obligation for anyone who files

What

FDA's own page (content current as of 11/16/2026) records that the President issued Executive Order 14434, Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence, on 29 September 2026, directing departments and agencies, "to the maximum extent permitted by law," to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" in several classes of document, with section 3(a) defining the new terms as the technologies covered by artificial intelligence as defined at 15 U.S.C. 9401(3). FDA began using the new terms in newly issued public-facing documents and communications on 16 November 2026. On existing material the page is explicit: "Existing guidance documents, regulations, Federal Register notices" stay in force and "remain in effect as written," with conforming terminology added at the next substantive revision and web content and forms updated on normal maintenance cycles; statutory and regulatory quotations keep their original wording, because "FDA will continue to quote and cite those terms as written." The last line is the one that matters: the order "does not create any new requirement for sponsors, applicants, manufacturers, or others."

Why it matters

For anyone filing, the compliance content changes by nothing and the retrieval cost rises by a lot: from 16 November the same regulatory topic will be covered by old guidances written in AI and new documents written in SI, both in force. Anyone doing regulatory intelligence, drafting submissions or maintaining an internal compliance crosswalk now has to search FDA's guidance library and the Federal Register on two sets of keywords or miss things. It is a policy that alters no obligation and alters everyone's workflow.

Discount this

This rename appeared as a single line in our 22 November weekly review; what is added today is the legal boundary stated on FDA's own page — existing documents keep their wording, and no obligation is created. This is terminology policy, not deregulation, and should not be read as any loosening of FDA's substantive requirements for AI-enabled devices.

Device regulation FDACDRH510(k)11/20

The final guidance on transferring a 510(k): a device has one holder at a time — a sentence written for medical AI companies being acquired

What

On 20 November 2026 FDA issued the final guidance Transfer of a Premarket Notification (510(k)) Clearance – Questions and Answers, jointly from CDRH and CBER, written as twelve questions and answers, under docket FDA-2024-D-1528 and Federal Register document 2026-23668; the draft was issued 5 June 2025 (90 FR 23937). The substance: where a device is not significantly changed in design, components, manufacturing method or intended use, the new holder generally does not need a fresh 510(k), and generally does where it is; FDA treats a device as having a single 510(k) holder at any time; a new holder required to list must register within 30 days of beginning a regulated operation and list under 21 CFR 807.25, using the original submission number for an unchanged device; labelling may need updating, and the GUDID must be updated when information such as the labeler name changes; failing to register, list, update GUDID or file a required marketing application may render the device adulterated or misbranded. This summary is taken from an item-by-item account of the guidance.

Why it matters

The guidance does not contain the word AI, and it lands squarely on the defining transaction type of medical AI in 2026: acquisition. The value of an AI device often sits not in hardware but in the 510(k) plus its Predetermined Change Control Plan, and the one-holder principle means a deal has to state plainly who takes the clearance, who registers and lists, and who updates GUDID. Handle it after closing and the device's legal standing during the gap is questionable. For teams that license rather than market in their own name — a description that fits a good number of Taiwanese AI device companies — a change of US holder obliges them to move on labelling and GUDID in step, and that is the step most often missed.

Discount this

This is nonbinding guidance, not a regulation. The Federal Register notice page was refused by the site's robots rules during this research, so the detail of the twelve questions and answers, and the docket and document numbers, come from a secondary item-by-item account; the guidance PDF itself was not read page by page. That account states explicitly that the guidance does not mention AI, software devices or PCCPs, so the application to AI acquisitions is this report's inference, not FDA's statement.

Leadership FDASenate HELP11/18

The agency that is supposed to write the SI review rules lost its nominee the night before the vote

What

Per RAPS's This Week at FDA of 20 November, the White House withdrew the nomination of Heidi Overton, a White House healthcare policy advisor, to lead FDA on 18 November, the night before a Senate HELP committee vote; Reuters, citing anonymous sources, reported that Senator Roger Marshall (R-KS) is under consideration as a replacement. The same roundup records two items on international regulatory pathways: FDA launched the Global Reliance Disclosure Program (GRDP), which will share minimally redacted drug and biologic approval packages with sponsors to carry to other regulators, which FDA says could cut duplicate evaluations; and on generics, a new batch of product-specific guidances brought the 2026 total to 268, with a virtual session on FY2027 generic drug research needs set for 10 December.

Why it matters

The roadmap assigns its most substantive items to FDA: a fast path for low-risk wellness and clinical decision support tools, a competency-based premarket approach for certain SI devices, and real-world post-market monitoring built jointly with CMS. Each needs a commissioner with political authority to drive it. Since 18 November the post has not even had a nominee. GRDP's direction is worth noting from Taiwan: it institutionalises the export of US review outcomes for other regulators to lean on, and over time will shift how much weight other countries give a US authorisation.

Discount this

The successor is a Reuters report from anonymous sources and is unconfirmed. The RAPS roundup itself notes it contains no AI or SI items, so the claim that this will slow the SI rules is this report's inference from setting the roadmap's task assignments beside the vacancy, with no official statement behind the causation.

Global governance UNBridgetown11/23

In Barbados, Guterres asks for a multilateral framework with independent oversight "within the next year"

What

Per Al Jazeera's report of 24 November, UN Secretary-General Guterres told the Bridgetown Special Summit on AI Safety in Barbados on 23 November that member states should build a multilateral framework for managing AI risk "within the next year," backed by "independent and credible oversight." He named no body to carry it; the report suggests a workable starting point is agreement on shared safety measures, a testing regime and basic requirements for independent oversight. He also argued that global governance institutions still reflect the world of 1945 and need reform, and said he would advise his successor that controlling AI is a precondition for humanity keeping control of its own world. His term ends in December and a successor has not been chosen.

Why it matters

Placed in today's context, the value of this item is not whether it happens but that it asks for precisely the thing the United States did not supply this week: independent oversight. In the roadmap, post-market monitoring is to be developed by FDA and CMS themselves, the efficacy evidence is supplied by vendors, and the accountability question is left blank by GAO. The only public demand for "independent and credible oversight" in the same seven days came from an outgoing international official with no jurisdiction. That contrast is the conclusion.

Discount this

This is a political appeal with no draft treaty, no institution and no timetable, and the report itself notes that US–China competition for research breakthroughs makes international rules look distant. The article contains no health or medical content at all; connecting it to medical AI is this report's framing, not the scope of Guterres's remarks.

EU & Asia EU 2026/1744NMPAPMDAMFDS

The EU pushed high-risk obligations for AI embedded in medical devices to 2 August 2028, while Beijing, Tokyo and Seoul drafted review points for generative-AI devices

What

The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI amends the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and, per one regulatory tracker, was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July: the application date for standalone high-risk systems under Annex III moves from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products, medical devices among them, from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028. A second account adds two deadlines: the Commission must publish guidelines on overlaps with sectoral legislation by 1 August 2027, which bears directly on device manufacturers, and the sandbox deadline moves to 2 August 2027. In Asia, a global device-regulation monthly issued 3 November 2026, covering 41 updates across 17 jurisdictions between 10 September and 22 October, records: China's NMPA consulting on three items — key points for inspecting AI medical devices, an independent software inspection guideline under the device GMP, and a registration review guideline for clinical evaluation of AI multi-disease decision-support devices; Japan's PMDA releasing a draft on review points for generative-AI medical devices for public comment; and South Korea's MFDS establishing a review guideline for software in professional-use digital medical devices and giving advance notice of amendments to the Enforcement Rules of the Medical Device Act that include a ban on AI-related advertising.

Why it matters

The three regions moved in different directions this quarter: the EU bought time, the US opened payment pathways, and East Asia wrote technical review points. For a team selling AI devices into both the US and Europe, the practical conclusion is that the European deadline really falls in the second half of 2027 — and the Commission's guidance on sectoral overlap will not exist until 1 August 2027 — while the US variable is not premarket review but payment and state preemption. Korea's advance notice of a ban on AI-related advertising is easy to overlook and directly constrains marketing language, which affects the day-to-day operation of products already on the market.

Discount this

The EU dates come from an editorial regulatory tracker that itself flags a conflict between its metadata date (18 June 2026) and the Official Journal date in its body (24 July 2026), and advises checking the Official Journal text; this report uses the body date without verifying against the OJ. The Asian paragraph comes from an excerpt of a secondary monthly in which most items carry no exact date and no full official document title, and can only be placed inside the 10 September to 22 October window — treat each regulator's own notice as authoritative. This item as a whole falls outside the 18–24 November window and is included to provide the contemporaneous international comparison.

02 — Product Analysis

Same month, same agency, two entirely different routes: one through a classic 510(k), the other trading real-world data for payment.

UPTUNITY | Aneurysm（CINA-Aneurysm / CINA-Aneurysm+）

CADe/CADx for brain aneurysm detection and follow-up · Bracco Diagnostics (Italy) with Avicenna.AI (France)

Function and position. Per the announcement of 13 November 2026, two products received 510(k) clearance: K262062 (CINA-Aneurysm, marketed as UPTUNITY | Aneurysm Detection) and K262147 (CINA-Aneurysm+, marketed as UPTUNITY | Aneurysm Management). The first is positioned as a "concurrent reading aid" for appropriately trained specialists, identifying suspected saccular brain aneurysms greater than 3 mm on head CTA images and reporting presence, location and size; the second provides quantitative measurement and exam-to-exam comparison for known aneurysms. The release states the software is to be used alongside review of the CTA images, does not replace a full patient evaluation or a qualified physician's reading, and "should not be solely relied upon to make or confirm a diagnosis." It is the first product in Bracco's new UPTUNITY line, co-developed with Avicenna.AI, which leads US promotion and distribution; the partnership was announced in March 2026 and the product will be shown at RSNA 2026 from 29 November to 3 December.

  • Strength : the regulatory position is cleanly drawn. Holding clearances for both detection (CADe/CADx) and follow-up (MIMPS) puts discovery and long-term monitoring in one workflow, and the latter is where the real clinical management burden of brain aneurysms sits; the release also supplies the epidemiological denominator, roughly one in 50 US adults with an unruptured brain aneurysm — see the original release.
  • Concern : the release contains not one performance figure. No sensitivity, no specificity, no false positives per case, no reader study, not even a sample size — for a detection product with a 3 mm threshold, those are not optional. The only number offered is disease prevalence. Withholding them until RSNA means buyers cannot assess the device before the show floor or the 510(k) summary.
  • Concern : the "concurrent reading aid" label is itself an allocation of liability. It leaves interpretive responsibility with the radiologist, and the executive quote says so outright — "The clinical decision stays where it belongs: with the radiologist." Elegantly put, and it is also the device-side answer to GAO's four questions: nothing in the regulation requires the vendor to carry the consequences of a false negative.

Dexcom Glucose Health Program（FDA TEMPO 試辦首位參與者）

Continuous glucose monitoring extended into prediabetes screening · Dexcom (US)

Function and position. Per FDA's announcement of 22 July 2026, Dexcom is the first participant selected for the TEMPO (Technology-Enabled Meaningful Patient Outcomes for Digital Health Devices) pilot. The pilot runs in connection with the CMS Innovation Center's ACCESS model and aims to expand patient access to new technologies in specific clinical use areas while safeguarding safety; participating manufacturers must "collect, monitor, and report real-world data (RWD)," and FDA's inclusion criteria include patient risk and "a reasonable expectation that the device could provide patient benefit." Dexcom's programme is positioned as an aid in screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, and may help improve glycemic control and lower HbA1c in people with prediabetes. FDA plans to select up to about ten participants in each of ACCESS's four clinical use areas, with no deadline for statements of interest; ACCESS's first cohort began in July 2026. CMS Innovation Center head Abe Sutton's framing is that ACCESS "provides a clear payment pathway that rewards measurable improvements in patient health rather than individual services."

  • Strength : this is the only live instance of the roadmap's fourth pillar, Pay for What Works, and it puts review and payment at the same table. Manatt's tracker records ACCESS as a ten-year voluntary demonstration paying $180 to $360 per patient per year, with the first cohort dated 5 July 2026 — a rare piece of published unit economics for a digital health product.
  • Concern : FDA's announcement says nothing at all about whether TEMPO participation changes premarket requirements — no mention of submission, clearance or authorisation. Secondary coverage has described it as skipping premarket review, but that claim is not in the official text, which says only that the mechanism matches the "rapid and iterative" nature of digital health device development. Until the agency states it plainly, this is the most consequential unknown in the whole TEMPO structure.
  • Concern : attaching a screening tool to outcome-based payment needs watching. ACCESS rewards measurable improvements in health, while the immediate output of a screening product is finding more patients — and in prediabetes, where the definition is itself contested, widening screening and improving outcomes are not the same thing. The announcement does not explain how the payment design separates them.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The US wrote payment first and evidence later; Taiwan is building evidence first and payment later — one problem, two orderings

(1) On who answers for it, Taiwan has already written it down, and more plainly than the roadmap does. The Ministry of Health and Welfare issued its Guideline on Medical Institutions' Use of Generative AI on 30 May 2026, and United Daily News's report of 31 May records the core requirements: anything touching clinical judgement, patient safety or the medical record must be confirmed and finalised by a licensed healthcare professional, AI may not replace professional medical judgement, and final responsibility rests with the professional; where a consultation involves audio or video recording, patients and family must be told in advance and consent, a refusal cannot be overridden, and recorded data may be used only for the purpose disclosed; where a member of the public is talking to an AI rather than a person, the institution must disclose it and state that the content is for reference only; and before adoption an institution must complete risk, cybersecurity and personal data assessments, establish a dedicated management mechanism and monitor performance regularly. Hung Tzu-jen, chair of the Taiwan Healthcare Management Association, took part in drafting it. Set that beside GAO's four questions: Taiwan's answer on accountability is explicit, and as of 20 November the American one is blank.

(2) Taiwan is doing "Pay for What Works" too, but starting from the evidence end, and expecting it to take three years. Since 2024 the Ministry of Health and Welfare has run the Taiwan AI Impact Research Center, led by its Department of Information Management and explained publicly by its director Li Chien-chang, with five medical centres as demonstration sites (Taipei Veterans General, Taichung Veterans General, Tri-Service General, National Cheng Kung University Hospital and National Taiwan University Hospital). The aim is local randomised controlled trials and health technology assessment, with data going to the National Health Insurance Administration's HTA centre and then to the NHI joint drafting committee, which decides on coverage. Per United Daily News, trials already running include a randomised trial of AI for low-dose CT lung cancer screening at Taipei Veterans General, AI validation in ophthalmology extended to general outpatient AI-assisted diagnosis at NTU Hospital, an interventional pay-for-performance study of AI-ECG in STEMI at Tri-Service General, VeriOsteo OP bone-screening AI used as a multi-centre CIRB case study at Taichung Veterans General, and work linking gynaecologic cancer imaging body composition to chemotherapy dosing at NCKU Hospital. Li expects to put the material before the committee in about three years, and notes that once something enters NHI coverage it almost never leaves, so the early design has to be careful. Against the American sequence of writing the payment pathway before the evidence, Taiwan's ordering is slow — and that sentence about coverage never leaving is exactly why.

(3) The item Taiwanese teams should actually read today is the 510(k) transfer guidance that never mentions AI. A good number of Taiwanese AI devices reach the market by licensing, or jointly under a US partner's name, and the final guidance of 20 November sets out in question-and-answer form that a device has only one 510(k) holder at a time, that a new holder must register and list within 30 days, that GUDID must be updated when information such as the labeler name changes, and that failing to do so may render the device adulterated or misbranded. Put plainly: when a US partner is acquired or a contract changes hands, the Taiwanese side is not a spectator, and responsibility for keeping labelling and GUDID in step bears directly on whether the product may lawfully continue to be sold. Domestically, the November global device-regulation monthly cited above records TFDA piloting online review with original documents filed for the record, and designating a clinical trial platform for digital medical devices — consistent in direction with international practice, though neither item carries an exact date or full official title in that monthly and both should be checked against TFDA's own notices.
