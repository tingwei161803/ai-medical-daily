Friday · Product & Company Deep Dive

The ambient scribe vendors spent three years climbing toward one position. On 10/21 Epic said it intends to stand there itself — Connectors, with MCP as the first standard — eight days after Abridge opened Assistant to every clinician it reaches

Friday, October 23, 2026 · Covering Oct 13–22 · Taipei time

One question today: when a clinician opens the computer, whose AI speaks first. Epic announced Connectors on 10/21, letting third-party developers wire their services into Epic so Epic's AI can pull data out of other apps and act inside them; Fierce Healthcare adds that the first standard is the Model Context Protocol and that Showroom listings wait until March 2027. And on 10/13 Abridge opened Assistant to every clinician at every Abridge health system, whether or not they use it for notes; on 10/7 it signed the University of Texas System's eight institutions, and on 9/14 stepped into the mid-revenue cycle with pre-bill review. The two routes have collided on one patch of ground — and this week's money (Suki's $90M Series E, Confido's $12.5M extension) is riding on the same assumption.

01 — Top Stories

Five stories, one line through them: ownership of the interface layer is being renegotiated, with the EHR, the ambient scribe vendors and the coding-automation vendors all at the table
Platform EpicConnectors10/21

Epic opens Connectors on MCP, turning Art, Emmie and Penny into a cross-app action layer — while stating flatly that it creates "no new autonomous actions"

What

On 10/21, ahead of its Open@Epic conference, Epic announced Connectors: developer-built links letting Epic pull information from other apps or complete tasks inside them, which Epic frames as sparing people "swivel-chair work." The capability goes to developers now, but organizations will not find them on Epic Showroom until March 2027. Fierce Healthcare adds three points: the first standard is the Model Context Protocol, testing with some health systems starts by end-2026, and developers pay to list at undisclosed prices. It works with Emmie for patients, Art for clinicians, Penny for revenue cycle, and agents built in Agent Factory, which debuted in August with over 100 pre-built features. Availity, Gozio Health and VPL are quoted but not confirmed as launch partners.

Why it matters

For three years the ambient scribe growth story has been one sentence: enter the workflow through the note, then grow upward into the clinician's assistant. Epic has drawn the end of that road at its own front door — if Art can issue commands inside other vendors' apps, the cross-system orchestration layer need not be third-party. The permission design matters more: VP of health informatics Johnston Thayer said "permissions are all handled at the health system level" and "there will not be new autonomous actions taken as a result of these capabilities," leaving risk with the hospital and orchestration with Epic.

Discount this

This is an announcement, not a shipment. Showroom listings wait until March 2027, and Epic published no user counts, revenue or named launch partners; all that is quotable is Agent Factory's 100-plus pre-built features and the "thousands of test cases" Epic runs on Emmie itself — no third-party audited data.

Enterprise deal AbridgeUT System10/7

Abridge signs the University of Texas System's eight institutions: nearly 11.7M outpatient visits and over 2M hospital days a year, after a 12-month pilot grew recording clinicians 73% and monthly notes 80%

What

Announced 10/7 and reported by Fierce Healthcare on 10/9: Abridge and UT REAL Health AI signed an enterprise agreement giving all eight institutions — UT Southwestern, UT Medical Branch, MD Anderson, Dell Medical School, UT Health Tyler, UTHealth Houston, UT Health San Antonio and UT Health Rio Grande Valley — access to its platform, with implementation decisions left to each campus. UT Health-owned and affiliated sites delivered nearly 11.7 million outpatient visits and more than 2 million hospital days in the last year; the pilot launched in 2025 spanned five locations and more than 50 specialties, and over 12 months grew recording clinicians 73% and monthly notes 80%. Abridge says it serves 300 health systems and will support more than 100 million conversations this year. The Daily Texan followed up on 10/21.

Why it matters

The shape matters more than the price: this is not a purchase but a governance framework, with system-level vetting through UT REAL Health AI, established in 2025, and local decision-making retained. Becker's roundup of 15 enterprise AI deals in 2026 shows the same UT System handed its generative AI platform to Qualified Health in January. Signing a governance platform and a point solution separately means large academic systems now buy in layers — governance, interface and workflow tendered apart — and what Abridge won is the interface layer.

Discount this

No contract value, no term, no license count — Fierce states that no financial terms were disclosed. The 300 systems and 100 million conversations are self-reported; 73%/80% are relative growth with no absolute base. And "all eight can access it" is not the same as all eight having deployed it.

Product AbridgeAssistant / pre-bill10/13

Abridge Assistant goes to every clinician — including those who don't use it for notes — while pre-bill review steps down into the mid-cycle before the claim

What

In its 10/13 post, Abridge describes Assistant as a chat-and-voice clinical assistant usable inside the EHR or on a phone; as of the September release, every clinician at every Abridge health system can use it, whether or not they use Abridge for documentation. Capabilities include chart queries with each source cited, guideline-based answers, calculators pre-filled with chart values, referral and handoff drafts, and visible reasoning steps on multi-step requests; grounding is the chart plus the conversation plus trusted evidence plus each system's own clinical pathways. Fierce's 8/17 report supplies numbers: adopted by 300-plus systems since April, with monthly actives past half of eligible clinicians and queries per clinician tripling in two months, drawing on UpToDate, NEJM, the JAMA Network and JCO. The other thread is pre-bill review, launched 9/14: before an inpatient claim goes out it compares coded diagnoses and DRG against what the documentation supports and shows the evidence — but changes no documentation, no codes and no claim status.

Why it matters

Opening it to clinicians who don't use it for notes turns ambient documentation from a product into a distribution channel: a second product rides security, governance and EHR integrations already negotiated, discounting three years of procurement friction at once. UPMC's Robert Bart says "more than 60% of our Abridge users have adopted it largely through self-discovery" and CHRISTUS's Tim Barker says "it's not interruptive, it's simply there when you need it" — neither describes a feature; both describe a default. CEO Shiv Rao states the other thread's business model outright: health systems "are reimbursed for the care they document that they delivered." One recording first saves time, then protects revenue.

Discount this

All self-reported, and the missing category is the important one: no published accuracy, hallucination or safety benchmark, and "clinician-built rubrics with human and LLM judges" is an internal process. Sutter Health's 78% measures work satisfaction, not correctness — and sits inside a revenue cycle launch. Pre-bill review carries no DRG accuracy or denial-rate figure at all.

Autonomy AKASAAutonomous mid-cycle10/2

AKASA claims fully autonomous coding of complex inpatient cases within 90 seconds of discharge — customers at over $180B in net patient revenue and roughly 10% of U.S. discharges — and publishes not one accuracy percentage

What

On 10/2 AKASA launched an autonomous mid-cycle AI platform, extending AI prebill review into autonomy for inpatient coding and CDI and claiming to fully code highly complex cases across all specialties with no human intervention. The figures: inpatient volume processed up nearly 6x in a year, customers at more than $180 billion in aggregate net patient revenue and roughly 10% of U.S. inpatient discharges, with coding complete within 90 seconds of discharge against 30–60 minutes for a human coder. Third-party blinded evaluations covered encounters making up "65%–85% of inpatient volume," and AKASA says its AI matched or exceeded human coders on MS-DRG assignment, principal diagnosis, clinical quality capture and present-on-admission accuracy. For context it cites a 2025 npj Health Systems study putting coding error rates as high as 20% and a July 2026 GAO review naming verifiable accuracy as a central challenge. Cleveland Clinic, already using its prebill products, says it "intends to explore" autonomy.

Why it matters

This attacks the same ground from the other end: Abridge walks down from the conversation to the claim, AKASA walks up from the claim to the documentation, and they meet at CDI. The difference is their stance on autonomy — Abridge says it changes no codes, AKASA says no human intervenes. CEO Malinka Walaliyadde calls an autonomous mid-cycle "a holy grail in our industry"; a16z's Julie Yoo says "healthcare cannot meet the scale of demand ahead through human labor alone." The same week Savista and Minset claimed up to 93% fully touchless coding, auditing 100% of charts initially before moving to 10% sampling — same direction, far more conservative framing.

Discount this

A vendor release whose most important number is precisely the one missing: "matched or exceeded human coders" comes with no percentage points, and the evaluations' sample size, evaluator and methodology are unpublished — "65%–85% of inpatient volume" is a scope, not a result. "Roughly 10% of U.S. discharges" is customer size, not the share handled autonomously: the release itself says deployments scale autonomous volume to each system's needs.

Funding Suki / ConfidoAsk / CareOS10/13–10/14

Two cheques the same week on one assumption: Suki's $90M Series E (past $250M raised) and Confido's $12.5M extension taking its Series A to $22.5M

What

Announced 10/13 and reported 10/15: Suki closed a $90M Series E, taking total funding past $250M at an undisclosed valuation, with new investors Healthier Capital, Hercules Capital and Crosslink Capital. It reports more than 250 healthcare organizations, over 30 technology partners embedding its AI, and encounters supported up more than 190% year over year in 2026. Suki Ask launched 10/8 as the first integration of Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate Expert AI API across Epic, Oracle Health, athenahealth and MEDITECH, with clinicians preferring answers carrying patient context 83% of the time (p<0.001) in early evaluations. The same week, Confido Health announced a $12.5M Series A extension on 10/14 led by Z47, taking its Series A to $22.5M; CareOS integrates with more than 50 systems across 1,500-plus sites of care, about 3,200 providers and 27 states.

Why it matters

Three companies did the same thing in one month: Abridge's Assistant, Suki's Ask and Heidi's agentic turn all add a decision-support layer on top of the note, and all wire to UpToDate — the evidence layer has been standardized into an API by Wolters Kluwer, so differentiation moves from content to context, which is what Suki's 83% is selling. But the two bets run opposite: Suki embeds its AI in other companies' platforms (30-plus partners), choosing to be a component rather than an interface, which carries less risk than a front door of its own if Epic Connectors becomes the standard; Confido skips health system governance review entirely, selling to private equity firms that then push it into their portfolios — the report cites AMA 2025 data putting PE ownership at about 6.5% of U.S. physician practices, up to 50% in some local markets, with support staff compensation at roughly 25–30% of practice revenue.

Discount this

Suki's valuation is undisclosed and "revenue doubled" comes without a base; the 83% measures preference, not accuracy or patient outcomes, as the report itself notes. Confido's 95% handle rate, 3–5 FTE equivalents and $10M–$15M annual savings are company-provided and flagged as not independently verified, with the savings explicitly projected — and an extension that closed in February was announced in October.

02 — Product Analysis

Two bets on one market: Abridge uses distribution to make its assistant the default; Ambience stakes its own fees on outcomes the hospital can measure

Ambience Chorus / The Ambience Standard

An outcome-based contract that puts the license fee at risk · Ambience Healthcare (US)

Function and position. The Ambience Standard, launched 8/19, is not a feature but a commercial model: no per-seat, per-token or per-feature pricing, but software fees tied to verified clinical, operational and financial outcomes. Its three parts are Value-at-Risk Performance Contracts staking vendor fees on mutually agreed metrics, Forward-Deployed Transformation Specialists placing practicing clinicians and systems engineers inside health systems, and Ambience Chorus, an AI agent harness coordinating context and clinical guardrails across more than 200 medical and surgical subspecialties.

  • Strength : it brings the only data today carrying the words peer-reviewed — a peer-reviewed three-month evaluation at St. Luke's showing physician burnout falling from 45% to 31% and turnover intent from 31% to 18%; Cleveland Clinic deployed 4,000 clinicians in four months at 70% sustained utilization and NPS 60; MultiCare reports 92% adoption; Ardent claims 3x verified ROI.
  • Concern : the loudest number is the thinnest. "Surpasses clinician performance by 27% in medical coding" comes from a company post dated May 27, 2025: a relative improvement over 18 board-certified physicians on an unnamed benchmark, with no case count, no confidence intervals and no absolute accuracy for either side — a reinforcement-fine-tuned model, not a peer-reviewed study.
  • Concern : neither the contract terms nor the capital cadence is transparent. "At risk against mutually agreed metrics" comes with no published thresholds, stake percentage or term; and the last disclosed raise is the $243M Series C at a valuation over $1B on July 29, 2025 — a markedly quieter year than Abridge's.

Today's competitive structure is a squeeze. The ambient scribe vendors are extending from the conversation in both directions — upward into clinical assistants and decision support (Abridge Assistant, Suki Ask), downward into coding and claims (Abridge pre-bill, Ambience Chorus) — and someone is already waiting at both ends: upward, Epic's Art and Connectors; downward, AKASA and Savista-Minset, selling autonomy rates. Only three shapes of differentiation survive that: holding raw data Epic cannot get (the full context of the recorded conversation), holding risk the hospital would rather not carry (Ambience staking its fees, AKASA claiming the autonomy), or declining the interface fight to become a component (Suki, embedded in 30 platforms). And the contrast worth remembering today: of the vendors claiming autonomy or superhuman performance, not one published a percentage point — while the company that took its accuracy all the way to confidence intervals the same week was SimBioSys, with an FDA 510(k).

04 — Taiwan Angle

Epic's Connectors is a connection layer one company builds inside its own ecosystem; in Taiwan the same layer is being built by the health ministry

(1) The U.S. fight over the interface layer presumes one dominant EHR. Taiwan has none, so the state is filling that layer itself. Epic Connectors changed every vendor's arithmetic on announcement because "connect to Epic" is nearly equivalent to "connect to the market" — a contrast United Daily News drew on 10/19, noting that about 80% of U.S. hospitals run the same commercial system while Taiwan's hospitals use dozens of different EMRs with no common standard, which officials call one reason new technology lands slowly. Taiwan's answer is a publicly built connection layer: the health ministry's next-generation EMR platform is built on FHIR, with all 27 medical centers connected by the end of 2026 (Chang Gung, Mackay and Chung Shan Medical University Hospital have completed trial connections) and roughly 200–300 regional and district hospitals finishing through 2027; original records stay at each hospital rather than in one central store, and a dual consent mechanism for cross-hospital access and AI research is planned by March 2027. The consequence is direct: in the U.S. an agent that wants to act across apps goes through Epic's Connectors and pays to list; in Taiwan that path is public infrastructure anyone can join — which also means no private vendor collects rent for being that layer.

(2) Taiwan's regulatory premise lands on the root of this business model: recording needs prior consent, the patient may refuse, and responsibility for accuracy sits with the physician. The health ministry's Guidelines for Medical Institutions Using Generative AI, issued 2026-05-30, hold that AI is a support tool only, that final responsibility for clinical judgment, patient safety and the record rests with licensed medical staff, and that AI must not replace professional medical judgment; clinicians using AI to draft records must verify the content's accuracy themselves; where audio or video is recorded, patients must be told in advance and consent, and a hospital cannot require the service if a patient refuses; and if a patient is talking to AI, the institution must say so. Stack that on today's main line: Abridge's "every clinician can use it" rollout must first pass encounter-by-encounter consent in Taiwan, and the duty to verify converts the fact that neither Abridge nor Ambience publishes an accuracy figure into the personal liability of the Taiwanese physician. The other side is payment — Li Chien-chang, director of the ministry's Department of Technology, explained in a talk that NHI reimbursement requires a randomized controlled trial showing the technology saves the NHI money before payment under value-based pricing, a process officials put at two to three years. Which is precisely why Ambience's value-at-risk contracts speak the same grammar as Taiwan's value-based NHI payment, and per-seat licensing does not.
