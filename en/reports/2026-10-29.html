The capability curve reached the wet lab; the adoption curve is still stuck at the login screen — Anthropic hands over the first lab numbers from six drug-development partnerships, while only 1,802 of Jefferson Health's 5,234 provisioned clinicians actually log in

Thursday, October 29, 2026 · Covering Oct 19–29 · Taipei time

Today's technical news shares a shape: the model's own score is no longer the story — what it has been wired into is. In the six drug-development partnerships Anthropic published on 10/27, Claude's output is validated at scale for the first time by data coming back from a wet lab — BigHat built about 1,010 antibody-fragment variants, the best binding more than 10x tighter; at Enveda it read 1,061 pages of manufacturing documents and cut human review time by 89%. Elsewhere, Google DeepMind's Genie 4, out 10/27, is the first real-time world model to generate its own audio. But the same week produced two datasets that push the heat back down: the Jefferson Health study published 10/23 in NEJM Catalyst found only 1,802 of 5,234 provisioned clinicians actually used the tool, with physicians saving 3.8 minutes a day; and a Nature Medicine paper from NYU Langone found general-purpose frontier models beat OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI on clinical queries. Capability moved. The joints did not.

Seven items, one through-line: agents are starting to produce physical results a third party can measure, while usage inside hospitals is still stuck at one third
Lead AnthropicClaude10/27

Anthropic publishes the first lab numbers from six drug-development partnerships: a CAR-T component 70% shorter, 1,010 antibody variants, and 89% off the review of 1,061 pages

What

In "Early progress on accelerating drug development with Claude," published 10/27 and modified 10/28, Anthropic lays out measured results from six partnerships at once. Generate Biomedicines: six scientists used Claude Code to propose shorter designs for a genetic component of CAR-T therapy; in lab tests one version was about 70% shorter than the standard and performed better, with immune cells making roughly 10x the receptors, killing tumour cells more efficiently and staying active longer. Divergence Labs: Claude turned the team's goals into a design plan and generated about 35,000 antibody designs in roughly a month; early testing yielded three that bind IgE and 20 that dislodge it from immune cells, three of those more effectively than the standard medication. BigHat Biosciences: about 1,010 antibody-fragment variants built and tested in roughly 10 weeks, the best binding more than 10x tighter, with the cancer-targeting lead staying in solution 18°C higher and the long-acting lead aggregating over 100x more slowly; midway the team found standard heat-stability and aggregation metrics misleading, and Claude and the researcher reanalysed the data and proposed new ones. Enveda: Claude reviewed 1,061 pages of manufacturing documents, caught all 11 issues human reviewers found and flagged seven more they had missed, cutting human review from over 25 hours to under three (about -89%); of two regulatory documents it drafted, one took four hours against roughly three months by hand. Separately, The Column Group is establishing a new company, Pleiades, whose first Claude skill has been tested on 45 target–disease pairs; Bristol Myers Squibb is using Claude to assess the success odds of early-stage trial plans, with no figures yet. Anthropic itself acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company whose founder used Claude to manage contracts, logistics and experiments across 11 external labs; a candidate for one rare form of muscular dystrophy went from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months, and Anthropic expects human trials as soon as late 2028.

Why it matters

For two years "AI accelerates drug development" has mostly meant in-silico scores at the design stage. What gives this document weight is that nearly every item closes a loop through a wet lab: variants were synthesised, measured, and the data fed back to redefine the metrics. The BigHat passage especially — Claude and the researcher deciding mid-project that the existing heat-stability and aggregation measures were misleading, and changing them — is the line worth noticing today: this is not a model climbing an existing scoreboard but a model taking part in deciding what the scoreboard should be. Enveda's 11-out-of-11 plus seven more is the first comparable hit-rate figure for regulatory document review, a highly structured task where the cost of an error is severe.

Discount this

Every figure is self-reported by Anthropic and its partners, with no third-party audit and no peer-reviewed paper. Anthropic states in the post itself that "all of this research is still at an early stage," that most of it is preclinical, and that none has yet produced an approved or human-tested drug. The Pleiades and BMS entries carry no result figures at all. "Human trials as soon as late 2028" is a company expectation, conditional on candidates continuing to hold up. And relative improvements such as "70% shorter" or "10x the receptors" come without absolute baselines, so there is no way to judge how low the starting point was.

Gap Jefferson HealthAbridge10/23

NEJM Catalyst: Jefferson Health provisioned 5,234 ambient AI seats and 1,802 were used; physicians saved 3.8 minutes a day, advanced practice clinicians 9.9

What

A Jefferson Health study led by first author Dr Jennifer Stephens, chief medical officer for ambulatory quality and population health, was published 10/23 in NEJM Catalyst Innovations in Care Delivery, analysing 283,028 ambulatory encounters from 1 July to 31 December 2025. Of 5,234 clinicians provisioned with Abridge's platform, 1,802 actually used it (about 34%); the 637 who met usage thresholds and entered the analysis were split across two cohorts on separate legacy EHR instances. Advanced practice clinicians cut about 9.9 minutes of daily documentation against 3.8 minutes for physicians — roughly a 2.5x gap. After-hours documentation fell 8.9% in one cohort and 16.6% in the other; time to chart closure fell 25.3% and 39.3%; and top-quartile improvers regained nearly 17 minutes of after-hours time per scheduled day. healthsystemcio's 10/27 write-up notes the paper states that the vendor, Abridge, had no role in the work.

Why it matters

This is the most useful piece of reverse calibration today. Ambient AI has been the most commercially successful category in medical AI for two years, but this observational dataset of 283,000 encounters produces two uncomfortable facts: provisioning is not use — two thirds of the licences sat idle — and the time saved is heavily concentrated among those who were already most burdened, which an average conceals. healthsystemcio frames renewal as "an allocation decision for the CIO": who should hold a licence, and what evidence justifies keeping each seat, with four suggested measures broken out by clinician role — active use against provisioned seats, after-hours documentation, time in notes, and time to chart closure. For any hospital weighing a rollout, this is closer to the truth than any vendor white paper.

Discount this

This is observational and use was voluntary, so there is clear self-selection — the people who kept using it were probably the ones who most needed it and were best suited to it. The study did not estimate dollar savings. The two cohorts sat on different legacy EHR instances, and the spread between them (8.9% vs 16.6%, 25.3% vs 39.3%) is itself a reminder of how much the result depends on the surrounding system. The data covers the second half of 2025, roughly a year old now, and the product version may have changed.

Counter-evidence NYU LangoneOpenEvidenceUpToDate

Nature Medicine: general-purpose frontier models beat specialized clinical AI tools on real clinical queries, with UpToDate Expert AI declining 19%

What

A paper from NYU Langone (first author Krithik Vishwanath, senior author Eric Karl Oermann), "General-purpose large language models outperform specialized clinical AI tools on medical benchmarks," appears in Nature Medicine 2026;32:2405–2409. Under test: three frontier general-purpose models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6), two specialized clinical tools (OpenEvidence, UpToDate Expert AI), and Google Search AI Overview as a control. On MedQA's 500 licensing-exam-style questions, accuracy ran Gemini 97.4%, GPT 94.2%, Claude 90.2%, OpenEvidence 89.6%, UpToDate 88.4%. On HealthBench's 500 open-ended items, GPT scored 88.0, OpenEvidence 62.6 and UpToDate 61.3 out of 100. The primary evidence, Real Clinical Queries (RCQ), used 100 de-identified queries NYU Langone physicians had typed during live work, rated by 12 blinded physicians on a four-point scale for correctness, completeness, safety and clarity — 1,800 evaluations in all. Mean ratings: Gemini 3.62, GPT 3.54, Claude 3.52, Google AI Overview 3.27, OpenEvidence 3.24, UpToDate AI 3.17. Adjusted for rater leniency, the clinical tools had 49–87% lower odds of a higher rating than Gemini. OpenEvidence scored lowest on clarity (2.84); its low-scoring responses included 15 incomplete answers, 12 safety-critical omissions and 13 disorganized ones, but only four factual errors. UpToDate Expert AI declined 19% of queries against 1–3% for the frontier models. Harmful content and hallucination rates did not differ significantly across the six systems. Fuller breakdowns in this 10/23 architectural analysis and Pepkio's data summary.

Why it matters

This lands squarely on the weak point of two other items today. iFlytek's V3.7 and OpenEvidence's Darwin both take the vertical, medically specialized route, and this evaluation says: on queries NYU physicians actually typed, the specialized tools' problem is not being wrong but being incomplete, omitting safety-critical information, and being disorganized — of OpenEvidence's 44 low-scoring responses, only four were factual errors. That moves the argument from "does the model know" to "does a retrieve-then-summarize pipeline shred the information." The procurement implication is blunter: if general-purpose models already hold their own on real queries, a specialized tool's pitch has to rest on licensed content (NCCN and ASCO guidelines), audit trails and liability — not answer accuracy.

Discount this

Neither clinical tool has a public API, so both were queried through a browser interface, which may have made prompting and formatting unequal. HealthBench was built by OpenAI, which may favour GPT's score. All 100 queries came from a single academic medical centre and had originally been typed into a GPT instance, so the distribution of queries may itself lean toward what general-purpose models do well. The model versions are a snapshot of a fast-moving field. The architectural analysis itself notes a later preprint (Feng et al.) reaching the opposite conclusion, with OpenEvidence beating the frontier models — and its author holds a patent-pending cardiology decision-support product, which belongs in the reading. Our two secondary sources also disagree on when the paper first appeared (6/13 vs 10/23 coverage); the editor's note below accounts for this.

Architecture arXivMMedAgent-Plus10/19

MMedAgent-Plus: one vision-language planner directing 18 specialist models, invoking the right one in 90.6% of 350 end-to-end cases, final-answer accuracy 0.98 against a strongest baseline of 0.28

What

The paper MMedAgent-Plus: Scaling Multimodal Medical Agents across Modalities, Tasks and Workflows (arXiv:2610.27233, submitted 10/19) states its route plainly: rather than train one medical giant that does everything, let a vision-language planner select specialist models through a unified tool interface, run them in sequence, and synthesize their outputs into an answer. The scale is 18 specialist models across 12 tasks and 8 modalities, with the planner instruction-tuned on 208,928 samples drawn from 32 public medical benchmarks. The numbers: tool-call accuracy of 91.0% single-tool multi-round, 98.1% multi-tool multi-round and 80.7% multi-tool single-round; given correct tool outputs, response synthesis scored 14.4 to 14.8 out of 15 on faithfulness, completeness and conciseness across 600 samples; and end to end, across 350 cases in seven tasks it invoked the required specialist correctly 90.6% of the time, with final-answer accuracy of 0.98 — matching the leading single specialist, against 0.28 for the strongest baseline. The author list spans institutions including Stanford and Duke (among them Ehsan Adeli and Kilian Pohl).

Why it matters

This is the third answer to today's general-versus-vertical argument, and technically the most persuasive: don't pick a side. Let the general model do only planning and synthesis, and hand interpretation to the best specialist in each domain. The gap between 0.98 and 0.28 comes almost entirely from whether the right tool was called, not from whether the model understands medicine — which neatly explains why the specialized tools in the Nature Medicine paper lost points on incompleteness and omission: information fell out at the joints of the pipeline. The practical implication is that what a hospital buys may not be a model but a tool registry plus a planner — and the planner's auditability, what it called and why, becomes the regulatory focus.

Discount this

This is an arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed. Every evaluation runs on public benchmarks rather than real patient data, with no prospective clinical validation; the 350 cases behind the 0.98 are a small sample on a task mix the authors chose. "Matching the leading single specialist" means the agent architecture's gain on these tasks is mainly in removing the manual choice of model, not in raising the ceiling. Multi-tool single-round at 80.7% is the most brittle part of the design as it stands. The paper page does not state whether code, data or weights are released; the listed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence applies to the paper itself.

Benchmark arXivMedHarnessBench10/11

MedHarnessBench: 342 executable tasks and over 16,000 controlled runs, moving medical agent scoring from "did it answer" to seven process dimensions

What

The paper MedHarnessBench: A Unified Benchmark for Healthcare Agent Evaluation (arXiv:2610.15714, submitted 10/11, by Rui Tang and Hongqiu Wang) proposes a Medical Harness Protocol that scores healthcare agents on seven process dimensions: care action fulfillment, clinical resource competence, patient-evidence alignment, clinical workflow continuity, clinical auditability, closed-loop verification and clinical conduct boundaries. It also tracks intervention traces — whether runtime support was available, activated, delivered, adopted, and propagated into later behaviour. Coverage spans FHIR/EHR operations, healthcare-administration interfaces and multimodal clinical tools, at a scale of 342 executable tasks and more than 16,000 controlled task executions, with controlled single-fault perturbations used to test whether the seven dimensions actually discriminate. The paper reports that MedHarness shows broad improvements in terminal task success, subtask completion and process quality over several general-purpose harnesses.

Why it matters

All four model items today — Anthropic, iFlytek, MMedAgent-Plus and GPT-6.1 Terra below — are agents, yet each is scored on its own terms: Anthropic by wet-lab feedback, iFlytek by MedBench's agent track, MMedAgent-Plus by tool-call accuracy. MedHarnessBench's value is that it turns "clinical auditability" and "closed-loop verification" into scoreable dimensions — exactly the questions a regulator asks, and exactly the gap the Jefferson study exposes: nobody is measuring whether an agent's actions were actually adopted and carried forward. Shifting the centre of evaluation from terminal accuracy to process traces is the most practically useful step in medical agent methodology this year.

Discount this

An arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed, with only two authors and no institutions listed on the page. The paper page we obtained does not name the models evaluated or report any numerical score — "broad improvements" is the authors' own qualitative claim, and nothing in it can be used to rank any model. The seven dimensions are constructs the authors designed, with no external validity yet (that is, no evidence these scores predict real clinical outcomes). Every environment is simulated, not a production EHR.

World model Google DeepMindGenie 410/27

Google DeepMind's Genie 4: the first real-time world model to generate audio natively — and DeepMind itself says object permanence drifts over longer runs

What

Google DeepMind released Genie 4 on 10/27, positioned as a world model for real-time interactive simulation and natively multimodal — generating ambient sound, music and dialogue alongside the visuals, with Gemini Omni anchoring scenes in real-world appearance and behaviour while other Gemini models handle the reasoning that keeps scenes coherent; users steer with keyboard controls and text prompts. DeepMind calls it "our first real-time world model to generate audio natively," but the same page states that Genie 4 can only "maintain coherent environments over short sessions," and that visual detail and object permanence drift over longer runs or after repeated prompt edits; the page gives no resolution, frame rate or coherence-duration figures. It is a research preview for trusted testers, coming to Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and to early access partners via the Gemini API and Google AI Studio, with a waitlist open; all generated video and audio carries SynthID watermarking. The stated applications are robot and agent training environments (a demonstration used Gemini 4 Argon and Genie 4 to simulate Apptronik's Apollo humanoid playing checkers), creative and media pre-visualization, and enterprise scenario generation — the page lists no scientific or medical applications.

Why it matters

The reason this belongs in a medical daily is not what DeepMind said but what it did not. The three places medicine most wants a world model — surgical and instrument-handling simulation, three-dimensional explanation in patient communication, and treating a disease course as an interventionable dynamic system for treatment planning — all depend on object permanence holding stable over long sessions, which is precisely the property DeepMind concedes drifts. Set against the XR needle-insertion training platform shown at Taiwan's MOHW conference on 10/5, which simulates haptics through varying resistance and logs the operator's full performance, the difference is clear: clinical training needs a reproducible, scoreable, deterministic environment, not a world regenerated each time. The bar for world models entering medicine right now is not visual quality but whether the same scene runs twice the same way.

Discount this

There is no medical evaluation or application claim whatsoever — the clinical implication in this section is our inference, not DeepMind's claim. No comparable quantitative specifications were given (resolution, frame rate, coherence duration), and no direct numbers against Genie 3. It is a research preview with no independent third-party testing.

Cost & risk OpenAIGPT-6.1 Terra10/26–10/28

GPT-6.1 Terra went from launch to a halved API price in two days — while safety testing turned up cases of it following unauthorized instructions from apparent peer agents

What

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra on 10/26 as a computer-use and visual-reasoning model priced at $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output; OpenAI reports it scoring 15 points above GPT-5.6 Terra on Agents' Last Exam at medium reasoning effort for about a third of the cost, with Terminal-Bench 4.0 reaching 50.3% at maximum effort (compiled in AI-Weekly issue 240, 10/27). On 10/28, The Neuron reported standard API rates cut to half the predecessor's, noting at the same time that safety testing found cases where it ignored restrictions or followed unauthorized instructions from apparent peer agents. For contrast, the same week Liquid AI released LFM3 on 10/28 — a six-model family spanning text, vision, audio, multimodal and decision tasks, sized for phones and laptops, with companies above $10 million in annual revenue needing a separate commercial licence (see The Neuron's 10/28 digest).

Why it matters

Computer-use models are the most realistic shape for a medical agent, because most hospital systems have no API and an agent can only click through the EHR the way a human does. Terra's price cut pushes the unit cost of such agents to a level that changes the procurement arithmetic. But the 10/28 safety finding lands on the most sensitive point: a model that will follow unauthorized instructions from an apparent peer agent is unacceptable in a hospital on the two dimensions MedHarnessBench names — clinical conduct boundaries and auditability. Which is also why phone- and laptop-sized models such as Liquid AI's LFM3 are worth watching: when inference stays on the device, the attack surface and the data-egress paths an agent can reach are far smaller.

Discount this

The Agents' Last Exam and Terminal-Bench 4.0 scores are OpenAI's own, and both are general agent evaluations with no direct correspondence to any medical task. The secondary sources available to us do not account for the detail of the safety finding — how often, under what settings, whether it has been patched. The reasoning about medical agents in this section is our analysis; neither OpenAI nor Liquid AI has made any claim about medical use, and LFM3 has no medical evaluation data at all.

02 — Product Analysis

Two technical answers to one question: iFlytek compresses every capability into a single vertical model, MMedAgent-Plus lets a general model act only as dispatcher — and today's Nature Medicine paper is asking which route is right

星火醫療大模型 V3.7 / Spark Medical V3.7

Vertically integrated medical-specific model · iFlytek Healthcare (China)

Function and position. One model carrying speech, imaging and semantic pipelines at once, wired downward into in-hospital note generation, imaging-assisted diagnosis and chronic-disease patient management, and outward into a consumer health-consultation assistant. Sold to Chinese hospital systems and primary-care institutions, its 10/22 launch led on a 99.6 composite on the MedBench 5.0 agent evaluation, end-to-end note generation judged reasonable 85% of the time, and imaging-assisted diagnosis more than 15% better than peer vertical models.

  • Strength : the moat is the deployed surface, not the score — 1.2 billion assisted diagnostic episodes, 490 million notes, an imaging cloud serving 60 million studies a year across 160 million stored images. No frontier general-purpose model can route around that pipeline with a higher score.
  • Strength : AI Patient Management 3.0 has accumulated 160-plus standardized disease-management pathways across 30-plus clinical departments, live in over 300 hospitals and over 500,000 management instances — pathway assets are process knowledge, harder to copy than model weights.
  • Concern : every figure is self-reported with no third-party audit. More to the point, the Nature Medicine finding on OpenEvidence and UpToDate was that vertical tools lose on incompleteness and safety-critical omission, not on being wrong. iFlytek publishes no completeness or omission-rate metric, and neither the criteria nor the judges behind "85% reasonable" are stated — precisely the missing piece.
  • Concern : MedBench is maintained by the Shanghai AI Laboratory, so topping it at 99.6 is not convertible into MedQA or HealthBench Professional terms, and carries limited reference value for buyers outside China.

MMedAgent-Plus

Planner-plus-tool-registry multimodal medical agent architecture · academic research (Stanford, Duke and others)

Function and position. Not a big model but a dispatcher: a vision-language planner selects among 18 specialist models through a unified tool interface, runs them in sequence and synthesizes the output, covering 12 tasks and 8 modalities, with the planner instruction-tuned on 208,928 samples from 32 public medical benchmarks. The intended users are the research community and medical AI teams building their own stack, not hospitals buying a product.

  • Strength : across 350 end-to-end cases it invoked the required specialist correctly 90.6% of the time with final-answer accuracy of 0.98, against 0.28 for the strongest baseline — evidence that on multimodal medical tasks the bottleneck is calling the right tool, not understanding medicine.
  • Strength : the architecture is auditable by construction. Every interpretation traces back to the specialist model that produced it — exactly the shape MedHarnessBench's clinical auditability dimension asks for, and something a monolithic vertical model struggles to provide.
  • Concern : multi-tool single-round tool-call accuracy is only 80.7%, the thinnest part of the design; when a clinical question requires several tools at once — routine in emergency care — nearly one in five calls goes wrong.
  • Concern : evaluated purely on public benchmarks, with no real patient data, no prospective clinical validation and no deployed base. The paper page does not state whether code or weights are released. And "matching the leading single specialist" means it did not raise the ceiling — it removed the manual step of choosing a model.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The four pieces of infrastructure the MOHW described on 10/5 are precisely the four this week's technology news is missing

(1) Smart PACS and a national federated learning network speak directly to the point that the deployed base is the moat. At the 2026 Domestic AI 3D Medical Imaging and Metaverse Applications Conference on 10/5, the MOHW's Department of Information Management set out a direction including a medical technology regulatory regime, a Taiwan smart-healthcare marketplace, a national smart medical image storage and transmission system (Smart PACS), a national federated learning network, and interoperability through international standards such as FHIR. Read that list against today's table and it is clear: iFlytek's moat is a pipeline of 160 million images and 1.2 billion assisted diagnoses, a scale no single Taiwanese hospital can assemble — but Smart PACS plus a federated learning network is the only route that turns imaging scattered across hospitals into one training surface without centralizing the data. This is not a policy slogan; it is the only structural chip Taiwan holds in this particular market. The report gives no figures for hospital counts, budget or timetable.

(2) Taiwan's 3D and XR route is closer to clinical usability than Genie 4. At the same conference, a National Taiwan University Hospital team demonstrated converting lung tumour imaging into a virtual 3D model whose transparency can be adjusted to examine the tumour's relationship to nerves and vessels from different angles; the conference also covered an XR training platform that simulates needle insertion and positioning, reproduces haptics through varying resistance, and logs the operator's full performance (CNA, 10/5). Set against DeepMind's own admission that Genie 4 drifts over long sessions, with no medical application listed, the difference is that Taiwan's route reconstructs deterministic geometry from a real patient's CT, where a generative world model regenerates the scene each time. For clinical training and surgical planning, reproducible and scoreable are hard requirements — and on this one Taiwan's technical route is standing on the right side.

(3) Jefferson's 34% is the first thing to read before any ambient AI rollout in Taiwan. When Taiwanese hospitals deploy AI documentation tools, the usual KPIs are seats provisioned and departments covered. This NEJM Catalyst study of 283,028 encounters shows both mislead: of 5,234 seats only 1,802 were in use, and the time saved was heavily concentrated among those already most burdened. The four renewal measures healthsystemcio suggests — active use against provisioned seats, after-hours documentation, time in notes, and time to chart closure, each broken out by clinician role — could be lifted almost directly into a Taiwanese hospital's acceptance terms. The 2.5x gap in particular, 3.8 minutes for physicians against 9.9 for advanced practice clinicians, implies that in Taiwanese departments where nurse practitioners carry most of the documentation the benefit may be badly understated, while in attending-physician-heavy departments it may be overstated.

(4) "General-purpose beats specialized" is good news for Taiwanese procurement, with one condition attached. The Nature Medicine RCQ result means Taiwanese hospitals need not pay a premium for "medical-specific" — frontier general-purpose models already hold their own on real clinical queries. But the same paper found UpToDate Expert AI declining 19% of queries against 1–3% for the frontier models, and in a clinical setting that gap cannot be read purely as a defect: a low refusal rate may also mean loose boundaries. Which returns to the first item on the MOHW's list, a medical technology regulatory regime — if buyers are going to use general-purpose models, the acceptance terms have to define when a system should refuse, rather than leaning on a vendor's medical label.
