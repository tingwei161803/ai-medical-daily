Friday · Product & Company Deep Dive

After the EHR vendors took scribing, everyone crowded into the moment a clinician asks a question — and that position just got its first public price: $100 million

Friday, November 27, 2026 · Covering Nov 11–26 · Taipei time

Today takes apart two strategies inside one product category. Menlo Ventures' 2026 annual report states the structure plainly: healthcare AI spend reached $3.7B, up 2.5x, but the growth is not in scribing — ambient scribes at $950M grew only 1.6x while clinical copilots at $550M grew 5.5x; EHR-native scribes went from 33% share to 46%, took 68% of new scribe dollars and grew revenue 120%, while AI-native scribes grew revenue 28% and lost 13% of share. So across two weeks in November, three companies went after the moment a clinician asks a question — one with cash, one with a licence, one with free: Wolters Kluwer agreed on 11/13 to buy AgileMD for up to $100M, Wiley licensed nearly 10,000 Cochrane systematic reviews to Abridge on 11/16, and OpenEvidence shipped Access Intelligence as a free feature on 11/11. What actually pressed down on the category's gross-margin ceiling was OpenAI pricing back-office tasks at 8.5 cents each on 11/16.

01 — Top Stories

Five stories, one through-line: decision support stopped being a feature and became a product category that can be priced, acquired, and undercut to free.
M&A Wolters KluwerAgileMDUpToDate11/13

The decision-support category gets its first public price: up to $100M for a 23-person, $7M-revenue company

What

On 11/13 Wolters Kluwer Health agreed to acquire AgileMD for up to $100M: $40M upfront in cash, the remainder deferred and contingent on performance targets. AgileMD was founded in San Francisco in 2011 by CEO Borna Safabakhsh and CMO Dana Edelson, M.D.; all 23 employees join Wolters Kluwer Health, and the company expects $7M in annual recurring revenue by the end of 2026. Its two products are Pathways, which operationalises local guidelines inside EHR workflows, and eCART, an FDA-cleared real-time clinical surveillance engine. Closing is expected in Q1 2027 or sooner, and the buyer set its own bar: return on invested capital at or above its 8% after-tax weighted average cost of capital within three to five years, with an immaterial impact on adjusted earnings.

Why it matters

It is the first verifiable price this year on turning a guideline into the next step inside the exam room — roughly 14x ARR, with 60% of it contingent on hitting targets. The asset being defended is named in the same release: UpToDate Expert AI launched in September 2025 and has been adopted by more than 2,700 US hospitals and health systems, over 95% of its UpToDate Enterprise Edition customers. Wolters Kluwer already has the distribution; what it lacked was the wiring from an answer into each hospital's own guidelines and EHR workflow — and it chose to buy that rather than build it. For scale, this is a publisher with €6.1B of 2025 revenue, about 21,100 employees and customers in over 180 countries (same announcement).

Discount this

The release discloses no customer count for AgileMD, no clearance number or clinical outcome data for eCART, and the $7M ARR is expected rather than booked. The $100M is a ceiling; actual consideration depends on performance conditions that were not published. The 2,700-hospital and 95% figures for UpToDate are the vendor's own.

Content licensing WileyCochraneAbridge Assistant11/16

Wiley puts nearly 10,000 Cochrane systematic reviews inside the Abridge Assistant — the scribe company is now selling answers

What

On 11/16 Wiley (NYSE: WLY) and Abridge announced a multi-year partnership licensing nearly 10,000 systematic reviews from the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and about 4,000 Cochrane Clinical Answers into clinical decision support inside the Abridge Assistant. Responses carry concise, cited summaries, with full text reserved for Wiley subscribers. The agreement covers US care-delivery organisations. The same release carries Abridge's own figures: more than 300 US health systems, more than 120 million patient encounters supported this year, 28-plus languages, and Best in KLAS for Ambient AI in both 2025 and 2026.

Why it matters

Cochrane is among the most-cited evidence bases behind clinical guidelines worldwide, and wiring it into a product that is already listening to the encounter binds "what does the evidence say" to "what is wrong with this patient" in one interface — the one square a pure literature-search tool cannot occupy. Cochrane CEO Karla Soares-Weiser framed it as AI changing how people reach evidence; Abridge CEO Shiv Rao, a practising cardiologist, framed it as trusted evidence only becoming useful once it connects to the context of care. The licence also explains why Wolters Kluwer bought AgileMD three days earlier: the evidence layer and the workflow layer are now acquiring each other.

Discount this

This is a paid press release distributed via Business Wire; the 120 million encounters, 300 health systems and 28 languages are all company-reported with no independent audit. The licence fee, revenue split and actual Assistant adoption rate were not disclosed. Best in KLAS is a user-satisfaction survey, not a clinical-performance validation.

Product launch OpenEvidenceAccess Intelligence11/11

OpenEvidence turns "the patient cannot get it or afford it" into a free feature: 2,500+ support programmes and drafted prior authorisations

What

On 11/11 OpenEvidence launched Access Intelligence, bolting cost and coverage onto the clinical decision: availability and eligibility for more than 2,500 patient support programmes (copay savings, patient assistance, insurance support services), which the company says are updated daily; savings pushed to patients through a separate product, Patient TakeHome; pre-filled forms or direct online enrolment for programmes that require both clinician and patient; and drafted prior authorisation requests, appeal letters and letters of medical necessity, grounded in clinical guidelines, prior-authorisation checklists and relevant ICD-10 codes, editable by the clinician and, on the advanced Sackett and Snow models, refinable through follow-up questions. The company says more than 500,000 prior-authorisation documents have already been written on the platform. Everything is free to verified US clinicians.

Why it matters

This moves OpenEvidence from looking things up to actually getting the prescription into the patient's hands — and does it for free. Menlo's report puts about 64% of clinical copilot revenue with OpenEvidence alone, more than 6x the next vendor (Menlo Ventures), and its model is free to physicians, paid for by advertising (compiled public record on OpenEvidence). When a free product already holding more than 60% of a category starts covering prior authorisation, vendors selling prior-authorisation tools get squeezed on price and bypassed on distribution at the same time. VP of Clinical AI Ania Bilski, M.D., put the positioning in one line: "Choosing the right treatment should be the hard part. Getting the patient access to it shouldn't be."

Market data Menlo VenturesMorning Consult11/19

Scribes up 1.6x, copilots up 5.5x: AI-native scribes grew revenue 28% and still lost 13% of share

What

Menlo Ventures' "2026: The State of AI in Healthcare" was fielded by Morning Consult in September 2026 among 550 director-level-and-above executives who had taken part in buying AI (150 provider technology decision-makers, 100 health plan and benefits executives, 300 pharma and biotech executives). The findings: healthcare AI spend hit $3.7B, up 2.5x, making it the largest vertical AI market, more than double legal at $1.8B (up 2.8x); providers account for $2.9B (77%) and payers about $150M (roughly 4%). By category: ambient scribes $950M (1.6x), coding and billing $600M (1.3x), clinical copilots $550M (5.5x), patient engagement and front office $450M (4.5x), and drug discovery from $30M to $700M. The sharpest structural shift is in scribing: startups held 67% of a $600M market in 2025; in 2026 EHR-native add-ons hold 46% of a $950M market, took 68% of new dollars and grew 120%. Adoption of domain-specific AI rose from 22% to 42% (health systems 52%, outpatient providers 34%, life sciences 31%, payers 23%). The other side is slower buying: procurement stretched from 7 to 10 months for health systems and from 5 to 11 months for outpatient providers.

Why it matters

This report is the backdrop to every acquisition and licence above: scribing proved to be a feature that distribution can swallow, and whoever owns the record owns the distribution. The report states outright that standalone note-taking has largely commoditised, and adds a harder observation — product advantages are becoming less durable as AI coding improves. The other signal for buyers is the payer counter-reaction: 43% of payers (57% of large payers) say provider-side AI coding raised their medical cost trend, up from 23% a year ago; 52% rank it among their top three cost drivers; and prior authorisation is now payers' top AI priority, up from 59% to 82%. Providers use AI to bill more, payers use AI to deny more, and for now both sides buy from the same vendors.

Pricing OpenAIGPT-6 Luna / AstraHealthAdminBench11/16

8.5 cents per back-office task: OpenAI prices prior authorisation 98% below its own top model

What

OpenAI unveiled healthcare computer-use agents on 11/16; The Inference's 11/17 analysis assembles the figures: GPT-6 Luna scored 91.8% of available rubric points on HealthAdminBench — Stanford's benchmark of prior authorisations, claim appeals and equipment orders — at 8.5 cents per task, 98% less than GPT-6 Astra at 94.3%. Astra separately scored 99% on clinical safety across 325 tasks and 93% on physician helpfulness across 646 reviews. Deployment partners named include CVS Health, Cigna and Abridge; Ambience Healthcare demoed a prior-authorisation agent; and Assort Health and Infinitus Relay joined ChatGPT for Healthcare as plugins, alongside Availity, Stedi, TriZetto and UpToDate.

Why it matters

UpToDate appears on both ends of two different stories: it is the asset Wolters Kluwer is paying up to $100M to defend, and it is also a plugin inside ChatGPT for Healthcare. That is the category's current bind — content owners plug into the general platform for distribution while trying to stop that platform from reducing them to an entry in a dropdown. The real significance of 8.5 cents is not its accuracy but that it sets a public price floor for administrative work. Once buyers know the same task can be done to 91.8% for 8.5 cents, per-seat and per-task specialist tools have to explain what the remaining few percentage points are worth.

02 — Product Analysis

One position, two opposite strategies: Abridge works up from listening to the conversation, OpenEvidence works down from the literature toward the patient. The difference is who pays.

Abridge Assistant

One answer bound to the chart, the conversation and the evidence · Abridge (US, Pittsburgh)

Function and position. Assistant is a decision-support layer grown on top of a scribe: it is already listening to the encounter and already connected to the chart, so its answer can carry both what is wrong with this patient and what the literature says. From 11/16, nearly 10,000 Cochrane systematic reviews and about 4,000 Cochrane Clinical Answers enter that layer on a multi-year licence (Wiley/Abridge release). Who buys it is unambiguous: the 300-plus US health systems that already bought the scribe, including Northwestern Medicine, Emory Healthcare and Johns Hopkins (Fortune, 2026-06-11).

  • Strength : it owns wiring nobody else has — the chart, the conversation and each system's own guidelines joined up. A pure literature tool cannot do this, and it is exactly the gap Wolters Kluwer paid up to $100M for AgileMD's Pathways to fill (Wolters Kluwer, 11/13).
  • Strength : it has public-sector volume that evidences use rather than installs. Assistant answered more than 40,000 questions from Department of Veterans Affairs clinicians last month, and Abridge shares a $776M VA contract with Knowtex (The Inference; Menlo Ventures).
  • Concern : the original business is being eaten by distribution. Ambient scribing was a $950M market in 2026 but grew only 1.6x; EHR-native share went from 33% to 46% and took 68% of new dollars, while AI-native scribes grew revenue 28% and lost 13% of share (Menlo Ventures). If Assistant does not sell, the scribe side is not coming back.
  • Concern : it has to charge against something free. OpenEvidence is free to verified US physicians, funded by advertising, and already holds about 64% of clinical copilot revenue (Menlo Ventures). Abridge has published no pricing model for Assistant, no paid adoption rate and no clinical outcome data; the Cochrane licence fee is undisclosed, and full text still requires a Wiley subscription.

OpenEvidence + Access Intelligence

Free to physicians, paid for by advertising, now handling affordability too · OpenEvidence (US, Miami)

Function and position. At its core is a clinical question-answering engine built on peer-reviewed literature, free to US physicians verified by National Provider Identifier and funded by advertising; on the content side it licenses NEJM and the JAMA family of journals, and on the organisational side it has the AMA, NCCN, AAFP and ACEP (compiled public record). Access Intelligence, launched 11/11, pushes the product boundary from what to prescribe to whether the patient can get it: eligibility lookups across more than 2,500 patient support programmes, pre-filled enrolment forms, and prior authorisations, appeals and letters of medical necessity drafted against guidelines and ICD-10 codes — more than 500,000 already written on the platform (OpenEvidence, 11/11).

  • Strength : its category share is already monopoly-grade. About 64% of clinical copilot revenue sits with it, more than 6x the next vendor (Menlo Ventures). The public record shows more than 430,000 registered US physicians (about 40% of the US total) and over 8.5M consultations a month as of July 2025, rising to about 760,000 physicians and roughly 18M consultations a month by December 2025 (compiled public record).
  • Strength : free is a distribution weapon, not just a price point. With procurement stretching from 7 to 10 months for health systems and 5 to 11 months for outpatient providers (Menlo Ventures), a product that needs no procurement bypasses the whole pipeline — the physician just signs up.
  • Concern : there is a structural conflict between an advertising-funded model and what Access Intelligence does. The product now decides both whether to surface a treatment and which copay programme it links to, while advertisers pay the bills. The release says nothing about commercial arrangements with programme sponsors, and nothing about the separation between advertising and clinical answers.
  • Concern : what it lacks is precisely the wiring Abridge has. Apart from the Cedars-Sinai chart integration in May 2026, it largely sits outside the EHR workflow and does not connect to local guidelines — the very segment Wolters Kluwer paid to acquire and Abridge pitches as its moat. It has also published no clinical outcome studies, and its self-reported USMLE scores (90% in 2023, 100% in 2025) are not clinical-performance evidence.

The real difference is not the model, it is who pays. Abridge charges health systems, so it has to prove the hours saved and the revenue gained are worth the price — which is why it must own the EHR wiring. OpenEvidence charges advertisers, so it only needs physicians to open it daily — which is why it can ignore procurement, local guidelines and who signed the contract. Both paths still work in 2026, but they cannot both win: once OpenAI prices back-office tasks at 8.5 cents each (The Inference, 11/17), the side charging per seat has to justify its price first.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan shipped a product the same week — but it stands on a different layer of that sandwich

(1) Taiwan's launch this week was in the physical layer, not the copilot layer. On 11/26, Foxconn and Taipei Veterans General Hospital unveiled their AI chemotherapy automation site and opened TVGH's Beihai AI Center. The partnership dates to a letter of intent signed in October 2025, with NT$300 million over three years across smart hospital automation, AI multimodal medical models and home care. The chemo automation has three segments: compounding equipment from Japan's Yuyama paired with Kawasaki Heavy Industries' dual-robotic-arm technology to cut staff contact with high-risk drugs; FARobot autonomous mobile robots that take the lifts to deliver the drugs; and Foxconn's own Nurabot, connected to the hospital information system, verifying patient identity, orders and nurse identity biometrically, running on NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin edge modules, with a digital twin built in NVIDIA Omniverse for process validation. TVGH superintendent Chen Wei-ming drew the line plainly: smart healthcare is not robots replacing pharmacists and nurses. Health Minister Shih Chung-liang said it could become Taiwan's "second sacred mountain of national protection." To be honest about it: the system is in clinical site validation, and the coverage gives no deployment scale, patient counts or cost savings.

(2) The copilot layer's business model is close to unreproducible in Taiwan, and that is not a technical problem. OpenEvidence can be free because it charges advertisers, and the advertisers who can pay are US pharma and device makers; it can bypass procurement because a US physician can self-register with an NPI. Neither holds in Taiwan: the market cannot support equivalent advertising revenue, and under single-payer national health insurance tool adoption is concentrated in hospital-level decisions. So the square for Taiwan is not the copilot one but the part of the middle layer not yet crushed: checking someone else's output, and centrally managing someone else's models. That layer sells governance and orchestration, and Taiwan happens to hold a condition others do not: a single payer plus infrastructure that has already piloted cross-hospital record exchange. Anue's 8/21 report notes that Chang Gung, Mackay and Chung Shan Medical University Hospital have completed real-time cross-hospital record exchange, with the Ministry of Health and Welfare advancing three AI centres.

(3) On reimbursement and governance, Taiwan's window is shorter than America's. DeepHealth's Mammo Risk got FDA clearance and, with it, only a Category III CPT code — meaning the US does not reimburse this either, and uses a tracking number to accumulate usage evidence first (DeepHealth). Taiwan has no equivalent transitional code: national health insurance either pays or it does not, so the route of deploying first, gathering evidence, then arguing for payment is not available, and a hospital's remaining incentive to self-fund is labour saved. On the governance side, one figure deserves a look from every unit now standing up an AI centre: CHIME's survey found full adoption of generative AI rose from 31% to 55% in twelve months while only 16% of AI users have governance and oversight policies (HIStalk, 11/18). Adoption is running more than three times ahead of oversight — a risk in the US, and in a single-payer system a reimbursement dispute waiting to happen.
