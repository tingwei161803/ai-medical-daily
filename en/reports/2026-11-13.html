Friday · Product & Company Deep Dive

Wearables, pre-claim, point of care — the week's three deals all bought position, not models

Friday, November 13, 2026 · Covering Nov 6–13 · Taipei time

Not one dollar this week was paid for a model. On Nov 10 Oura's release announced it would pay $625 million in cash, subject to purchase price adjustments, for Counsel Health — a company with 70 employees and roughly $36 million raised by PitchBook's count. That is $8.93 million per head, and 17 times the capital ever put into the business. The buyer had shelved its own IPO indefinitely on Sept 29 — 55 million shares at $40–44, up to $2.2 billion, implying a valuation of exactly $15 billion. The same day Charta Health closed a $55 million Series B led by Insight Partners on the premise that the chart should be reconciled before the claim is filed; the day before, the American College of Physicians licensed its guidance and Annals of Internal Medicine to OpenEvidence. Three deals, three positions: the continuous physiological signal on the consumer's body, the truth of the chart before the claim exists, and the guideline a physician sees in the second they decide. Not one of the performance figures announced with them has been independently audited.

Lead story · M&A OuraCounsel Health11/10

Oura pays $625M cash for Counsel Health: the first time a smart-ring maker has bought the whole clinical pipe — AI intake plus a real physician — into its own app

What

On Nov 10 Oura announced a definitive agreement to acquire Counsel Health for $625 million in cash, the release stating it is "subject to purchase price adjustments" and expected to close before the end of the calendar year. Counsel founder and CEO Muthu Alagappan, MD, becomes Oura's chief clinical AI officer and brings all 70 employees with him; the company closed a $25 million Series A led by a16z and GV only about a year ago, with PitchBook putting total funding at $36 million. The product had been embedded in the Oura app since June 2026 — Oura was a customer for five months before it decided to buy the supplier.

Why it matters

Consumer wearables have been stuck at the same wall for a decade: they can measure a signal but cannot write a prescription. This deal crosses three lines at once — diagnosis, prescription, referral. The release says a physician can be added to the chat at any point and may provide diagnoses, prescriptions and referrals "as appropriate," across three tracks: preventive, acute, and specialty and chronic care (the last including reproductive health, aging and cardiometabolic health). Rivals have been renting the clinical layer instead: Whoop only announced on-demand clinician access for US users in May 2026. Oura chose to own it outright — $625 million for a channel no competitor can share.

Discount this

The same release carries the line "Oura Ring is not a medical device and is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, monitor, or prevent medical conditions or illnesses." Buying a company that writes prescriptions does not make the ring a regulated device; the clinical liability still sits with the human physicians. Every satisfaction figure is an internal survey: more than 90% of surveyed members said Oura data helped them get better care, and those who saw a Counsel physician rated the experience 4.6 out of 5, with no sample size, no instrument design and no third-party verification. The release also never says which states Counsel is licensed in — the one fact that decides who can actually buy this product.

Financials OuraIPO11/10

The same release slipped in unaudited quarterly numbers: $533M revenue up 153%, $87M adjusted EBITDA — from a company that walked away from its listing on Sept 29

What

Tucked into the M&A release is a set of figures Oura itself labels "preliminary and unaudited": revenue of $533 million for the quarter ended Sept 30, 2026, up 153% year over year, and adjusted EBITDA of $87 million at a 16% margin; fiscal 2026 revenue of roughly $1.75 billion, up 92%, with over six million members and a ring tracking 50-plus metrics continuously. Set that against what it disclosed when it shelved the IPO on Sept 29: prior-year revenue of $907.9 million, 5.7 million paying members (up from 5 million at the end of June), subscriptions at an 89% gross margin but only about a fifth of sales, and about $372 million of cash at the end of June. Its last private mark was roughly $11 billion, from a $900 million round led by Fidelity in October 2025.

Why it matters

$372 million of cash at the end of June, and a $625 million cash cheque now: put those two numbers together and it is clear why the deal waited for a good quarter. The structure matters more. Hardware is still most of revenue, while the 89%-margin subscription is only a fifth of it. Buying Counsel Health buys a reason to push that subscription from "look at your data" to "see a doctor" — and for a company that pulled its listing and must prove it is not a hardware business, that fifth is the whole valuation. CEO Tom Hale said on Sept 29 that Oura had "the luxury of choosing our moment"; the Nov 10 move shows the moment he chose was the one where the story becomes a service business.

Discount this

These figures are not merely unaudited; Oura says they remain "subject to completion of financial closing procedures" and may change. A 153% growth rate appears in an M&A release issued voluntarily by a private company that just pulled its listing, not in a 10-Q. Closing timing, synergies and expected benefits are all forward-looking statements, and the release says plainly that Oura does not plan to update them except as required by law. Note also a discrepancy: TechCrunch wrote that the deal is expected to close before the end of 2027, while the official release and HIT Consultant both say before the end of this calendar year. The release governs.

Funding · Launch Charta HealthInsight Partners11/10

Charta Health raises a $55M Series B and launches a payer product: moving the argument from after adjudication to before the claim

What

On Nov 10 Charta Health announced a $55 million Series B led by Insight Partners, with Madrona, Bain Capital Ventures and South Park Commons returning and QuantumLight joining. The product is autonomous AI chart review: once a note is finalized it parses progress notes, operative reports, lab values and discharge summaries, checks them against ICD-10-CM, CPT, payer policy and local protocols, flags codes the record supports but the claim omits, tests Evaluation and Management levels for undercoding, strips codes the documentation cannot carry, and routes the suggested fixes to human coders. The same day it launched Charta for Payers, built with "one of the largest US health plans," covering retrospective HCC coding, a second-pass claim validation engine, a source-linked coding workspace for audit teams, prospective risk-adjustment prompts and HEDIS care-gap identification. Named customers: American Family Care, Grow Therapy and the Pediatric Associates Family of Companies.

Why it matters

This is the week's only deal sold to both sides of the transaction, and that two-sided position is itself the moat. Founder Justin Liu and CTO Scott Morris were engineering leaders at Rockset, later acquired by OpenAI; the two spent a year earning Certified Professional Coder credentials and auditing with more than 100 health system and payer executives, and the company now requires every engineer and every go-to-market hire to obtain a medical coding certification — a deliberate bet against off-the-shelf generative models. Liu puts it plainly: "Payers and providers should agree on the chart before a claim goes out, not fight about it after." In an industry that earns money from retrospective denial and clawback, moving the point of conflict earlier removes legal costs from both sides at once.

Discount this

Not one performance figure. No accuracy rate, no chart volume, no revenue recovered, no false-positive rate — and for a system that proactively suggests adding codes, the false-positive rate is precisely the number that should be published, because systematically over-eager suggestions amount to teaching providers to upcode. The co-development payer is unnamed, so there is no way to judge how deep the payer half of this two-sided story actually goes. Neither total funding nor valuation was disclosed.

Content licensing OpenEvidenceACP11/9

The ACP licenses its guidance and Annals of Internal Medicine to OpenEvidence: internal medicine's largest corpus goes to a company funded by pharma advertising

What

On Nov 9 the American College of Physicians and OpenEvidence announced a licensing collaboration putting ACP's clinical guidelines, guidance statements, practice points and best practice advice, plus Annals of Internal Medicine's original research, reviews, guidelines and commentary, into OpenEvidence's clinical decision support platform. ACP president Jan K. Carney, MD, MPH, and OpenEvidence chief medical officer Travis Zack, MD, PhD, are quoted by name, the latter saying "internal medicine is the largest specialty on OpenEvidence." Note an internal contradiction in the same release: the opening paragraph cites 163,000 members while the About section cites 152,000, and the release does not reconcile them. Whether money changed hands, and when the content goes live, are both undisclosed.

Why it matters

OpenEvidence's moat is not a model; it is a stack of licensing contracts. By Sacra's accounting it has already signed NEJM and JAMA (first half of 2025), Wiley, Cochrane, the American College of Cardiology, the American Diabetes Association, AAO-HNSF, NCCN's oncology algorithms and the American Urological Association; with the ACP, internal medicine is now covered. The point of the strategy is that a rival can copy a model but cannot copy a corpus already licensed to someone else. The societies are making a choice of their own: handing over their guidance decides which interface the next generation of physicians reads guidelines in.

Discount this

Every usage claim in the release is the company's own and carries no figures: "the most popular AI for doctors in the United States," "per independent studies, more American physicians use it than all other AI platforms combined," "trusted by millions of verified US healthcare professionals," "used daily, on average, by the majority of US physicians" — not one names a study or a verifiable number. Sacra's "more than 65% of US physicians monthly and more than 50% daily" is likewise flagged as a company claim. The incentive structure deserves watching: the platform is free to verified US clinicians and funded by pharmaceutical and device advertising at CPMs above $1,000. Placing society guidance inside an interface paid for by drug makers raises a conflict-of-interest question the release does not address in a single word.

Funding · In-facility AkaraLocke Health11/11

Akara raises $5.5M: replacing cameras with thermal sensing moves the privacy problem in ward-monitoring AI from the contract to the hardware

What

On Nov 11 Akara, a Trinity College Dublin spin-out, announced $5.5 million in new funding, bringing the total to $12.3 million, led by Locke Health with VTC Ventures, EIC Fund, CommonKind and NovaVector Holdings participating and existing backers ACT, Atlantic Bridge and Enterprise Ireland following. The product is wall-mounted sensors plus AI agents and robotics that detect events and notify staff, with natural-language querying and report generation on top. The technical choice is the story: thermal sensing with edge AI and no cameras at all, with processing on each device. Co-founder and CEO is Conor McGinn. The company says it is already deployed in US ambulatory surgery centers and hospitals.

Why it matters

For ward-monitoring products, the obstacle of the last two years has never been model accuracy; it has been the sentence "we would like to put a camera in the patient's room." Swapping the sensing modality for thermal and moving inference onto the device deletes an entire chapter of contract negotiation and security review — the same logic as the week's other deals in another form: advantage comes from position and format, not from the model. In the same week LeanTaaS acquired computer-vision company LookDeep Health and launched iQueue for Virtual Care, taking exactly the camera route. Both paths were funded in the same seven days.

Discount this

The release reports no measured results: no accuracy, no deployment count, no efficiency gain, and not a single named customer. The two figures used as selling points are external context rather than product performance — inclusion in TIME's Best Inventions of 2025, and the claim that more than 40% of US hospitals run at a loss. Nor does the release state outright that the system is on-premise; it says only that processing runs on each device to "limit" transmission of sensitive data. Limiting is not the same as not transmitting.

VisiQuate buys Finvi's Artiva HCx: in this round of revenue-cycle M&A, what changes hands is workflow position, not algorithms

What

Per Health IT Answers' Nov 12 financial roundup, VisiQuate acquired Finvi's Artiva HCx revenue-cycle business — platform, employees and customers — combining VisiQuate's AI and automation with Artiva's denial-management and collections workflows. The same roundup lists Ellipsis Health acquiring AI care-navigation provider Kouper Health (pairing Ellipsis's conversational care-manager AI, Sage, with Kouper's EHR integrations and longitudinal patient data), Sagility acquiring clinical documentation and coding services provider Chirok Health, and a Cadara–Waystar integration aimed at post-acute billing. Set these four deals beside Charta Health's round and the shape of revenue-cycle AI competition becomes clear: nobody is comparing models, everyone is buying the stretch of workflow they lack. VisiQuate had analytics and needed the collections action; Ellipsis had conversational AI and needed EHR integration; Charta had chart inference and needed a payer-side channel, so it built one. The implication is that AI capability in revenue cycle is commoditizing fast, and what is actually scarce is a pipe already plumbed into someone else's system — which can only be bought, never trained.

This item rests entirely on a secondary roundup rather than the parties' own releases; this report did not obtain official announcements for any of these deals, so prices, effective dates and the scope of transferred customers cannot be verified. 02 — Product Analysis

Two products, two kinds of position: Counsel Health claims the moment the patient speaks, Charta for Payers the last second before the claim is filed

Counsel Health / Counsel Studio

AI-native primary care plus a white-label embed layer · Counsel Health (US, being acquired by Oura)

Function and position. A member starts by chatting with Counsel AI, which draws on medical records, prior interactions, clinical guidelines and continuous biometric data; the release calls the architecture a "clinical AI harness" built from specialized agents for history-taking, guideline retrieval, records review and emergency detection. Behind it sits a proprietary Clinician Cockpit where board-certified physicians sit on watch and join the conversation within minutes once an interaction becomes a clinical concern. What makes it worth $625 million is Counsel Studio, launched in July 2026: it drops the whole stack into payers', health systems' and consumer health apps' own interfaces via iFrames, WebViews or APIs, with partners customizing UI/UX (native dark mode included), wiring in their own clinical protocols, escalation pathways and enterprise data sources, and launching "within weeks."

  • Strength : the distribution existed before the acquisition. At Counsel Studio's launch the company said its infrastructure serves more than 8 million people nationwide, with a 96% resolution rate without escalation, a 2-minute average physician response time and average annual savings of $381 per engaged member — if those hold, this is a triage system already running, not a demo.
  • Strength : Oura hands it an input nobody else has. More than six million members, continuous PPG measurement, 50-plus metrics across sleep, activity, stress, readiness, women's health, metabolic health and heart health — the thing telemedicine has always lacked: an objective time series that exists before the consultation begins.
  • Concern : the 96%, the two minutes and the $381 all come from the company's own announcement, with no independent or peer-reviewed evaluation cited and no definition of "resolved," nor any mention of whether repeat care-seeking within 72 hours is tracked. In triage, a high resolution rate and a high miss rate can be two readings of the same number.
  • Concern : the underlying model has never been disclosed. Coverage states explicitly that the article does not name the model behind its AI, and the Counsel Studio announcement gives no models, no agent-architecture detail and no latency figures. For a system doing emergency detection, withholding both the model and the evaluation method leaves clinicians unable to judge how it behaves in the rare, lethal cases.

Charta for Payers

Pre-claim chart reconciliation and risk adjustment · Charta Health (US)

Function and position. Charta's original product is autonomous chart review on the provider side: after the note is finalized and before the claim goes out, it parses progress notes, operative reports, lab values and discharge summaries, checks them against ICD-10-CM, CPT, payer policy and local protocols, then does three things — add codes the record supports but the claim omits, test Evaluation and Management levels for undercoding, and strip codes the documentation cannot carry — routing every suggestion to a human coder. Charta for Payers, launched Nov 10, moves the same inference to the payer side, aimed at Medicare Advantage and ACA Marketplace organizations, with retrospective HCC coding, a second-pass claim validation engine, a source-linked smart coding workspace for audit teams, prospective risk-adjustment prompts for care teams, HCC validation workflows and HEDIS care-gap identification.

  • Strength : the technical route deliberately avoids off-the-shelf generative models. The two Rockset-alumni founders spent a year earning CPC credentials and auditing alongside more than 100 health system and payer executives, and the company requires every engineer and seller to hold a coding certification. In a field where one wrong code triggers an audit, domain knowledge written into hiring criteria is harder to copy than domain knowledge written into a prompt.
  • Strength : the two-sided deployment is itself the product. Sitting on both the provider and payer side means the same chart can be agreed before it is submitted — as CEO Justin Liu puts it, "payers and providers should agree on the chart before a claim goes out, not fight about it after". The named customers — American Family Care, Grow Therapy and Pediatric Associates — sit in three entirely different coding contexts: urgent care, behavioral health and pediatrics.
  • Concern : zero performance figures — no accuracy, no volume, no recovered revenue, and crucially no false-positive rate. A system that proactively says "you missed this code" creates systematic upcoding risk if its suggestions skew toward higher codes; and the users are organizations paid by coding, so the incentive to self-correct is not there.
  • Concern : the payer-side stakes are not on the table. The co-developing "one of the largest US health plans" is unnamed, so adoption depth cannot be judged; and how neutrality is maintained — or whether data is walled off between the two sides — when one platform both argues for codes on the provider side and validates them on the payer side is not addressed at all in what has been published.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The week's three positions each hit a different wall in Taiwan: the telemedicine rules, the NHI clawback regime, and a body of local guidance nobody has licensed

(1) The Oura–Counsel model — chat with the AI, pull in a physician when needed — cannot legally be sold in Taiwan as a consultation. It can only be sold as pre-triage advice. Under the Regulations on Telemedicine Practice issued by the Ministry of Health and Welfare on Jan 22, 2024 and effective July 1 that year, the special circumstances permitting telemedicine expanded from five categories to ten (adding enrolled chronic-disease care patients, end-of-life care, care for inmates in correctional facilities, care for those with limited mobility, and care during disasters, epidemics or other major incidents), covering an estimated 2.47 million people, with services spanning medical advice, consultation, psychiatric psychotherapy and the ordering of tests, plus conditionally permitted prescribing. The threshold is what matters: a physician may see a patient online only where the local health bureau has approved it, or where the case falls under an NHI bundled-payment program; practising video consultation outside the rules carries a fine of NT$20,000 to NT$100,000 under the Physicians Act. So Counsel Health's shape — a physician joining the chat within minutes and prescribing or referring on the spot — counts as lawful care in Taiwan only when it lands inside one of those ten categories with health bureau approval. Outside them, it is health advice and nothing more. This is why local wearables and health apps have spent years stopping at the sentence "please see a doctor": not because the technology could not go further, but because there is no legal channel on the other side of that sentence. The boundary is moving: on June 26, 2026 the MOHW under minister Shih Chung-liang said video consultations for inmates would be opened to all specialties and that remote care resources would be offered to young Taiwanese abroad under the Youth Overseas Dream Fund program, with overseas remote care currently covering only Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia and Indonesia — but it is moving by named population, not by general opening.

(2) Charta Health's line — agree on the chart before the claim goes out, do not fight about it after — describes precisely the sorest structure in Taiwan's NHI, and Taiwan's version comes with a multiplier. The fight here is not denial but extrapolated clawback after sampled review: under the NHI Administration's March 2018 reform, the caps on random-sampling extrapolation are 10x for hospital outpatient and 3x for inpatient; 20x for primary-care Western medicine outpatient and 5.8x for inpatient; 20x for traditional Chinese medicine outpatient; and no cap at all for dental outpatient. One disallowed charge in a clinic can therefore be clawed back twentyfold — and that reform was itself the product of a 2016 hunger strike by an orthopaedic physician in Yilan over magnified clawbacks. The same document records that dual professional review with named reviewers, introduced in October 2016, cut disputed-review caseloads by about 30%, and that from 2017 the ratio of random to targeted sampling was set at 3 to 7. Place that regime beside Charta's product logic and both the opportunity and the risk magnify. The opportunity: reconciling before submission is worth far more under a 20x extrapolation regime than it is in the US. The risk is the false-positive problem flagged in the product analysis above, multiplied by the same factor in the opposite direction — an AI biased toward "you missed a code" would, under sampled review, be accumulating exposure that gets clawed back twentyfold. If such a product comes to Taiwan, the first number it should publish is not accuracy but the false-positive rate.

(3) OpenEvidence's licensing stack hits Taiwanese clinicians directly, yet nothing on Taiwan's side is being licensed out — and nobody is buying. Physicians here already read English-language guidance, so when the ACP moves its guidance and Annals of Internal Medicine into an interface funded by pharmaceutical advertising, the effect travels unchanged to a Taipei clinic room: the interface changes, who pays sets the ordering, and Taiwanese users are not in that ad market's pricing logic at all. The sharper gap is on the other side. Taiwan's genuinely unique clinical assets are its NHI reimbursement rules, its specialty-level local consensus statements, and the filing rules bound up with those extrapolation multipliers — and that entire body of knowledge has never been in any medical AI's corpus. A model that has read NEJM, JAMA, Cochrane, NCCN and now the ACP still does not know which code this procedure should be filed under in Taiwan, or whether it will survive review. What Taiwan should copy from this week is not a model but the move itself: the ACP recognized its guidance as a licensable asset and went and negotiated terms. Taiwan's specialty societies and the NHI Administration hold equally unique corpora, with neither a licensing framework nor a price.
