Three grades of evidence for cardiac AI in one week: a trial's win ratio of 2.00, a registry's "34% fewer", and a cohort's 0.004

Monday, November 9, 2026 · Covering Nov 2–9 · Taipei time

Almost all of this week's clinical AI news was crowded into Chicago. AHA Scientific Sessions 2026 ran November 6–9, and three things landed inside the same window. On Nov 7, Cleerly's PARAMOUNT became the first randomised trial of care guided by AI quantitative coronary CT (AI-QCT), with a win ratio of 2.00 on its 180-day hierarchical composite (95% CI 1.37–2.56, p<0.0001). On Nov 2, Heartflow's DECIDE registry reported 34% fewer major cardiovascular events at one year across more than 6,000 matched patients, without attaching a single confidence interval. And a 27,382-patient retrospective in The Lancet Digital Health measured the gap between AI and clinicians to three decimal places: an adjusted C-index of 0.781 against 0.777. Read together, today's subject is not whether AI works, but which grade of evidence you are willing to trade for which grade of conclusion.

Randomised trial CleerlyAI-QCT11/07

PARAMOUNT: the first randomised trial of AI plaque-based care, win ratio 2.00 — and the data says of itself that it is not peer-reviewed

What

On November 7, in an AHA 2026 late-breaking session, Todd C. Villines of the University of Virginia presented PARAMOUNT (NCT06713239): a prospective, multicentre, open-label randomised trial that enrolled 530 patients at 21 sites between January 2025 and February 2026, with 523 in the preliminary analysis. Participants were symptomatic outpatients with suspected coronary artery disease, LDL-C of at least 100 mg/dL and no prior CAD. Median age was 57, 57% were women (300), and median baseline LDL-C was 127–128 mg/dL. AI-QCT-guided plaque-based care was compared with usual care built on functional stress testing. The primary endpoint, a prespecified hierarchical composite at 180 days, gave a win ratio of 2.00 (95% CI 1.37–2.56, p<0.0001). In unmatched comparisons the plaque-based patient had the better outcome 46.7% of the time against 23.4% for usual care (HCPLive, 2026-11-07; Business Wire release, 2026-11-07).

Why it matters

For a decade, AI imaging products have stood almost entirely on reading accuracy and observational registries — of 1,357 FDA-cleared AI devices, only three had tested patient outcomes. PARAMOUNT is the first to put such a tool inside a randomised frame, and what it demonstrates is not that AI reads better but that acting on the plaque AI sees changes prescriptions and decisions for the better: lipid-lowering therapy rose from 31% to 75% against 32% to 47% in usual care, and among patients taken to invasive angiography, 38% left without revascularisation against 60% — that is, fewer unnecessary interventions. The practical consequence is that payers finally have something to negotiate over.

Discount this

Three deductions are mandatory. First, the trial was open-label: patients and physicians knew their arm, and the layer of the composite that contributed most — diagnostic certainty, a win difference of 16.6 percentage points — is a subjective judgement. Second, Cleerly sponsored the trial, and Villines discloses consulting for Cleerly, HeartFlow and Milestone. Third, the release states plainly that the data are preliminary and not yet peer-reviewed, with full results due in early 2027; HCPLive also renders the diagnostic-certainty contribution as "a win ratio of 16.6%", and since a win ratio is not a percentage, that figure should not be quoted before the paper. And at 530 patients over 180 days, the trial is not powered to say anything about death or myocardial infarction.

Retrospective cohort MycardiumAILancet Digital Health11/06

27,382 patients, 5.6 years of follow-up: AI left-ventricular segmentation beats clinicians by 0.004 — and is 200 times faster

What

Joy G and colleagues published "Performance of artificial intelligence versus clinician left-ventricular segmentation of cardiac MRI for mortality prediction" in The Lancet Digital Health (DOI 10.1016/j.landig.2026.101042), from UCL and City St George's in London, written up by News-Medical on November 6. It is a retrospective, multicentre prognostic study of 27,382 patients who had cardiac MRI at two centres, one British and one American, with a median follow-up of 5.6 years and 13% mortality (another report counts 3,458 deaths). The mortality C-index from ejection fraction was 0.669 for AI against 0.637 for clinicians; adjusted for age and sex, 0.781 against 0.777. Median ejection fraction was 57.8% by AI and 62.1% by clinicians. AI took about 3–4 seconds per patient, against a median of 13 minutes for expert segmentation measured by the same group earlier; in a random sample of 600 patients, 6 (1%) had segmentation errors (News-Medical, 2026-11-06; Resultsense, 2026-11-03).

Why it matters

This is the most honest dataset of the day, because the gap it measures is too small to sell. An adjusted C-index difference of 0.004 is not, by any clinical standard, AI beating doctors. The real pitch is 3–4 seconds against 13 minutes — the value of using AI as the first-line measurement under clinician oversight, not as a replacement. The subgroups are more interesting still: in obstructive coronary artery disease the AI model fit better and added information; in non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy the advantage shrank; and in the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy subgroup the clinicians' ejection fraction performed slightly better, which the authors attribute to the AI not fully handling hypertrabeculation and enlarged papillary muscles. That is precisely the distance between good average performance and usable for this patient.

Discount this

Retrospective design, no cause-specific mortality, possible inconsistency in clinician segmentation technique, no assessment of calibration, no assessment of effects on clinical decisions or patient outcomes, and no evaluation by ethnic group. Most participants also came from a single UK centre, and several authors disclose financial relationships with MycardiumAI, the company tied to the segmentation technology; senior author Rhodri Davies also invented the tool. Funding came from the NIHR and the British Heart Foundation rather than a vendor.

Prospective registry HeartflowDECIDE11/02

Heartflow DECIDE: 6,000-plus patients, 34% fewer events, half the heart attacks — and not one confidence interval in the release

What

On November 2, Heartflow (Nasdaq: HTFL) released its AHA 2026 data. The prospective DECIDE registry enrolled more than 6,000 well-matched patients and compared coronary CT plus Heartflow Plaque Analysis against coronary CT alone. At one year: 34% fewer major cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, revascularisation or stroke) and half the rate of myocardial infarction. By 90 days, management had changed in more than 50% of patients, 50% reached LDL-C below 70 mg/dL against 35% with CT alone, the number at goal rose 41% early, and those whose management changed saw LDL-C fall by an average of 18.7 mg/dL, sustained at one year. Co-lead investigator Leslee Shaw of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai presented it on November 9 at 11:06 CT. The same release carried the GAMEFILM registry: among 200 retired NFL players, 85% had detectable plaque, on average nearly 90% of it soft (non-calcified), 42% had obstructive CAD (over 50% stenosis) and 29% had functionally significant ischaemia, while fewer than 10% reported exertional symptoms; linemen sat at the 63.3rd percentile of plaque volume for their age against the 31.9th for non-linemen (GlobeNewswire, 2026-11-02).

Why it matters

Place DECIDE beside PARAMOUNT and the market's split evidence strategy becomes legible. Cleerly spent two years on a 530-patient randomised trial and bought a p-value with a confidence interval that can speak only to 180-day process measures. Heartflow used a 6,000-patient registry and bought hard outcomes — 34% fewer events, half the heart attacks — that drop straight into a sales deck, at the cost of matching rather than randomisation and of residual confounding it cannot exclude. Both products are FDA-cleared, both were at the same meeting, both are chasing the same cardiologists and the same reimbursement codes, and they chose opposite routes.

Discount this

The release provides no hazard ratios, confidence intervals or p-values, and says nothing about the matching method, inclusion and exclusion criteria, or number of sites; "well-matched" is the vendor's word. GAMEFILM's 200 retired players volunteered, so selection bias is severe, and "88% of those not on lipid-lowering therapy were willing to start" is an intention, not a behaviour. The release's further claims — Plaque Analysis at 95% agreement with IVUS, Plaque Staging validated in 23,000 patients with up to 16 years of follow-up, more than 1,800 institutions, over 750,000 patients and more than 625 peer-reviewed publications — are all company marketing figures that this report has not independently verified.

Abstract (not peer-reviewed) AHA 2026Duke / RURAL11/02

Nurses with two hours of training used AI guidance to take usable echocardiograms for 4,597 rural adults: 98% complete, median 9.5 minutes

What

AHA 2026 abstract MDP2290 (poster P6539), from Duke University School of Medicine and the Duke Clinical Research Institute Imaging Core Laboratory, with principal investigator Gerald S. Bloomfield, is part of the RURAL (Risk Underlying Rural Areas Longitudinal) study. Fourteen non-sonographers — nurses, nursing assistants and CT technicians — completed a two-hour, four-part virtual and hands-on course in AI-guided ultrasound, then worked from a mobile health unit that visited ten rural counties in Alabama, Kentucky, Louisiana and Mississippi from November 2021 to June 2026, scanning 4,597 volunteer adults aged 25–65 (mean age 48, 67% women) in one 3.5-hour appointment each. Results: 98% of studies contained all six required views, core-lab experts rated more than 99% adequate for measurement, diagnostic-quality rates for individual views ranged from 77% to 94%, 81% finished inside the 15-minute target, and median scan time was 9.5 minutes. 133 studies turned up critical findings, most often an ejection fraction below 50%, and 99% of those were returned to participants within three days. Three operators needed retraining after their first scans were reviewed (American Heart Association release, 2026-11-02).

Why it matters

This is the only study today that locates AI's value in who can do the scan rather than how accurately it is read, which also makes it the most transferable one. The bottleneck in echocardiography has never been the interpretation algorithm; it is finding someone who can hold the probe. What this dataset says is that two hours of training, real-time AI guidance and off-site core-lab review, taken together, are enough for non-specialists to produce measurable images in places with no cardiology service. The 133 critical findings, 99% of them returned within three days, amount to a screening pathway that did not previously exist across ten counties.

Discount this

The authors' own limitations are thorough: no direct comparison with images acquired by sonographers, reproducibility assessed internally rather than against other core labs, no systematic assessment of between-operator variability on the same participant, only one software brand and one core lab (the release does not name the brand), likely limited generalisability beyond rural populations or outside a research core-lab setting, and no assessment of whether returning results changed participants' care or health. This is an abstract and has not been peer-reviewed.

Abstract (not peer-reviewed) AHA 2026Wearables11/02

Wearables plus AI flagged hypertensive crisis up to 72 hours early: 4,190 adults, 32% positive predictive value, 97.4% crisis-free when silent

What

An AHA 2026 abstract (the release links to P5959; it is also listed as MDP0117), first author Waleed Razzaq Chaudhry of the University of Maryland Capital Region Medical Center, conducted at one hospital in Sindh, Pakistan. 4,190 adults aged 40–85 with stage 2 hypertension (at least 140/90 mmHg), mean age 62.4, 46% women, were followed for twelve months from January 2025, using a portable cuff monitor taking repeated day and night readings alongside a wrist-worn optical fitness tracker for heart rate and heart-rate variability. An AI tool analysed 47 characteristics — including sleep dipping, post-waking surge, blood-pressure trajectory and heart-rate variation — to predict hypertensive crisis (above 180/120 mmHg with acute end-organ damage) within the next 72 hours. Results: 418 crises in 312 participants (about 7%); roughly 32% of those alerted went on to have a crisis, while 97.4% of those never alerted stayed crisis-free; failure of blood pressure to fall during sleep carried nearly five times the odds of a coming crisis, and a post-waking rise of more than 35 mmHg above the night-time level nearly four times; and where medication was adjusted within 24 hours of an alert, crises fell by about 61% and emergency department visits by about 45% (American Heart Association release, 2026-11-02).

Why it matters

Two reasons. First, the predictive signal here comes not from a novel model but from known physiology: non-dipping and the morning surge have long carried documented risk, and AI's role is to turn continuous measurement into an actionable time window, not to discover a mechanism. Second, it is one of the few studies today that ties the alert to the action and then measures the outcome: the 61% and 45% reductions are not model metrics but the consequence of someone adjusting medication within 24 hours. That also exposes the hard part immediately — a positive predictive value of 32% means two of every three alerts are false, and a clinic has to absorb that load.

Discount this

Every participant came from a single hospital in Pakistan, so generalisability is unknown. The release gives no AUC, sensitivity, specificity or odds ratios with confidence intervals, and discloses nothing about the model's architecture or training. "Medication adjusted within 24 hours of an alert" was plainly not randomly assigned, so the 61% and 45% almost certainly absorb differences in adherence and access to care. The AHA itself notes in the release that wrist and finger monitors give less reliable readings and recommends upper-arm cuffs. This is an abstract and has not been peer-reviewed.

Regulation (evidence requirements) FDADarmiyan / NeuroXT11/05

FDA codified prognostic assessment software for mild cognitive impairment as Class II — and its special controls name overreliance outright

What

On November 5, the FDA used the Federal Register (document 2026-22590) to classify prognostic assessment software for mild cognitive impairment as a Class II device with special controls. The device type originates in the De Novo granted to Darmiyan's BrainSee on January 11, 2024. The definition covers prescription software that combines multiple clinical data sources to estimate the likelihood that a patient already diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment will progress to more severe impairment or dementia within a specified period, for adjunctive use and not as a standalone diagnostic or prognostic tool. The special controls require clinical performance validation in the intended population; a test dataset representative of patients seen in practice and independent of training or development data; reporting of accuracy, precision, reproducibility, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value; possible postmarket surveillance; a human factors and usability assessment showing that intended users can operate the device and interpret its output correctly, with specific attention to overreliance on that output; physician labelling covering limitations, populations where performance may differ or is unvalidated, and the risks of misinterpretation; and patient labelling explaining what the output means and what follows if it is wrong (leanRAQA, 2026-11-06; primary document: Federal Register 2026-22590). Last month, South Korea's NeuroXT obtained a 510(k) in the same space for NEXT-Cue on October 20, combining brain MRI, demographics and cognitive assessment data to estimate the likelihood of progression from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's dementia within five years (PR Newswire, 2026-10-20).

Why it matters

Read alongside the other six stories, this is the most structurally important news of the day. The first five are all arguments about whether a given number counts as evidence, and this classification writes the answer into regulatory text: the test dataset must be independent of development data, must represent the population seen in practice, must yield seven reported performance metrics, and the sponsor must show that users do not overrely on the output. Once a De Novo becomes a Class II type, later products of the same kind follow through 510(k), and this list is the bar. It is the first explicit evidence specification for prognostic AI — and what it demands is exactly what several of today's press releases do not provide.

Discount this

This report could not read the Federal Register document directly (the site restricts automated retrieval), so the special controls above are taken from leanRAQA's November 6 summary; the product code, regulation number and the specific clinical performance figures from BrainSee's original De Novo were not obtained, and the primary notice governs. NeuroXT's release provides no clinical validation data or performance metrics and does not state the 510(k) number. One further caution: classifying a device type as Class II codifies existing practice rather than setting a new, stricter bar.

Methodological critique MicrosoftScience11/03

After MAI-DxO's 83.2% against physicians' 36.1% appeared in Science, a November 3 critique took the comparison apart

What

Microsoft's sequential-diagnosis study, "Sequential diagnosis with language models" (Harsha Nori and colleagues), appeared in Science (DOI 10.1126/science.aeh7930) in late October. The headline figures: with no budget constraint, the DxO orchestrator reached 83.2% accuracy on NEJM clinicopathological conference (CPC) cases and 80.3% on MIMIC-IV-ED admissions, at a mean testing cost of $4,979 per emergency case, against generalist physicians at 36.1% on CPC cases and 46.8% on emergency cases. Across models, DxO's orchestration raised accuracy by an average of 4.4 points on emergency cases and 7.9 points on CPC cases. On November 3, Chuck Dinerstein, medical director of the American Council on Science and Health, published a point-by-point critique: the NEJM cases were selected for difficulty and AI is disproportionately exposed to that literature; the AI drew on multiple simulations while physicians worked alone without the specialist consultation they would normally use, making it a coordinated system against an individual; when information was unavailable the AI generated plausible answers, so the study never tested whether it could judge an unreliable history or conflicting tests; the AI asked four times as many questions, each carrying patient burden and interpretation time; and the cost estimates use hospital chargemaster prices rather than actual costs. His conclusion: a strong benchmark for structured information gathering, but not evidence that AI diagnoses better in practice (ACSH, 2026-11-03).

Why it matters

This one comes last because it shows the cost of the sentence this report opened with. PARAMOUNT's win ratio of 2.00 came from real patients at 21 sites, DECIDE's 34% from a year of follow-up in more than 6,000 people, and MAI-DxO's 83.2% against 36.1% is the most striking number of all — drawn from published cases inside a simulation, against a comparator of lone physicians deliberately denied consultation. The independent evaluation site nabu.science flags the same points: the CPC cases are public literature and no contamination check is reported, the orchestration gain is measured against minimally prompted models rather than genuine alternative architectures, text-only oracle interaction and US chargemaster pricing limit transfer, and safety and longitudinal outcomes are not assessed. Lined up across one week, the seven stories trace a single rule: the prettier the number, the further the design sits from the clinic.

Discount this

This report could not read the Science paper itself (paywall); the figures above come from nabu.science's evaluation page and the ACSH commentary, which give two readings of DxO's unconstrained accuracy — 80.3% on emergency cases and 83.2% on CPC cases — while ACSH separately cites 80.9% on NEJM cases at an estimated $2,241 when the system was asked to be more parsimonious. These are figures from different settings and should not be mixed. ACSH is an opinionated commentary outlet, not peer review, and it notes that the AI-versus-physician accuracy comparison appears only as an image, with the values absent from its text. The exact Science publication date could not be confirmed here and is placed in late October on the basis of secondary coverage.

02 — Product Analysis

Same organ, same modality, same meeting: Cleerly and Heartflow took opposite routes to evidence

Cleerly AI-QCT

AI quantitative coronary CT plaque analysis · Cleerly (USA)

Function and position. It quantifies plaque volume, composition and distribution automatically from coronary CT, replacing the traditional reading of "what percentage stenosis" with "how much plaque of what kind this person carries". The buyers are cardiology clinics and imaging centres, and the use is to decide, for a symptomatic patient with suspected coronary disease, whether to intensify lipid-lowering therapy and whether to go invasive. PARAMOUNT's design is itself the proof of that positioning: the comparator is not another way of reading films but the existing clinical pathway of functional stress testing.

  • Strength : the only AI plaque product so far to meet the primary endpoint of a randomised trial — a win ratio of 2.00 on the 180-day hierarchical composite (95% CI 1.37–2.56, p<0.0001) — and that endpoint contains what payers actually care about, fewer unnecessary invasive studies: 38% of those taken to angiography had no revascularisation, against 60% in usual care (Business Wire, 2026-11-07).
  • Strength : the trial population was 57% women with a median age of 57 — precisely the group in whom traditional functional testing performs worst and produces the most false negatives — which strengthens the case for displacing stress testing (HCPLive, 2026-11-07).
  • Concern : the layer contributing most to the primary endpoint is diagnostic certainty, which in an open-label trial is the measure most easily moved by expectation; and the hard endpoints, death and myocardial infarction, simply cannot be tested in 530 patients over 180 days. Full results are due in early 2027, and what is quotable today is preliminary and not peer-reviewed.
  • Concern : the release never specifies which product version was used, referring only to AI-QCT and "Cleerly's FDA-cleared solutions"; the trial was sponsored by Cleerly, and its co-principal investigator discloses consulting for both Cleerly and its competitor HeartFlow.

Heartflow Plaque Analysis

AI plaque analysis and staging · Heartflow (USA, Nasdaq: HTFL)

Function and position. The same AI plaque quantification from coronary CT, but bolted onto Heartflow's existing FFRCT business and extended with Plaque Staging, which sorts plaque burden into clinical risk stages and hands the physician a stage that can go straight into the record. All of it sits inside the Heartflow One platform, which turns coronary CT into 3D heart models (GlobeNewswire, 2026-11-02).

  • Strength : scale and distribution. The company reports more than 1,800 institutions, over 750,000 patients and more than 625 peer-reviewed publications, and DECIDE alone drew on more than 6,000 matched patients — a volume that lets it report outcomes like 34% fewer major cardiovascular events and half the myocardial infarctions at one year, which Cleerly's trial is not yet sized to produce (GlobeNewswire, 2026-11-02).
  • Strength : depth of product line. The same release carries the P4 trial from TCT 2026, in which PCI Navigator changed the intervention plan in about 70% of patients — meaning Heartflow has extended from pre-diagnostic risk stratification into procedural planning in the cath lab, where Cleerly remains concentrated on the former (GlobeNewswire, 2026-11-02).
  • Concern : DECIDE is a matched registry, not a randomised trial, and the release carries no hazard ratio, confidence interval or p-value, nor the variables used for matching. Under that design the 34% cannot exclude residual confounding — the patients selected for plaque analysis may simply be the ones their physicians were already treating more aggressively.
  • Concern : high-visibility studies like GAMEFILM — 200 retired NFL players, 85% with detectable plaque — market extremely well but carry limited scientific weight: volunteers, no control arm, no outcome follow-up. Publishing it in the same release as DECIDE invites readers to conflate the two evidence grades. Q3 results are due on November 12, which is when it becomes visible whether any of this clinical data has turned into revenue.

Close the section in one sentence: today's competition is not in the algorithm but in how evidence is priced. The FDA's special-controls list of November 5 matters because it is the first time regulation has stated in writing what a given body of evidence is worth — and at least three of today's press releases could not produce what is on that list.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan's medical AI framework already governs how to prove it works; what is missing is who pays

(1) Rural echocardiography: the RURAL dataset reads as if it were written for Taiwan. Duke's recipe — non-sonographers with two hours of training, real-time AI guidance, off-site core-lab review — produced 4,597 usable studies across ten counties with no cardiology service and surfaced 133 critical findings (AHA, 2026-11-02). Taiwan's equivalent problem is not a shortage of machines but the absence, on the outlying islands and in mountain townships, of anyone who can perform a cardiac ultrasound. The core-lab review link, meanwhile, already has infrastructure under construction: the Ministry of Health and Welfare's new-generation 3D PACS and FHIR Box, which converts hospital data into HL7 FHIR without replacing core systems, exist precisely for cross-institutional image retrieval and transfer (United Daily News, 2026-10-05). What still has to be built is the chain of responsibility — who reviews, within what turnaround, and who takes over afterwards — and RURAL's 99% returned within three days is the specification for it.

(2) Taiwan's "responsible AI" framework lines up precisely against the FDA's special-controls list of November 5. The Ministry of Health and Welfare runs three centres covering deployment, evidence and reimbursement for medical AI: the Responsible AI Execution Center, the Clinical AI Evidence Validation Center and the AI Impact Research Center. The first has completed a certification framework across ten hospitals — Linkou Chang Gung, Taipei Veterans General, Kaohsiung Medical University's Chung-Ho Memorial, China Medical University Hospital, Changhua Christian, Chi Mei, Lien Hwa International, Chiayi Chang Gung, Tucheng Chang Gung and Xinbei Renkang — built on nine transparency principles, information security and privacy protection, and lifecycle management, with the ministry reporting at least ten deployed responsible-AI applications and 229,086 uses (Liberty Times Health). Set that beside the new US device type's special controls and Taiwan is in fact ahead on governance at the point of use: the American list governs what a manufacturer files for review, while Taiwan's governs what a hospital answers for once it is running. Both are missing the same piece — technical validation such as whether the test dataset is independent of the training data is not spelled out line by line in those nine transparency principles.

(3) On reimbursement, Taiwan is already saying it out loud. At October's 3D imaging conference, Taichung Veterans General Hospital honorary president Chen Shih-an said plainly that wider adoption requires a service and payment mechanism: some hospitals have applied for self-pay billing codes for 3D imaging, and only linking these to National Health Insurance programmes could expand use. National Taiwan University Hospital president Yu Chung-jen said the hospital expects to complete an AI-driven automated 3D imaging conversion framework across the whole institution by 2028. Chijie Medical Technology's Anatomy Cloud AI converts 2D scans into 3D anatomical models in about five minutes, and the company donated ten workstations for the ministry to deploy as demonstration units (United Daily News, 2026-10-05).
