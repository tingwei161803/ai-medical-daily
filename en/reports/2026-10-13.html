Two opposite prices hung on the same track this week — Solventum marketing at $5.5–7B the coding rules engine inside more than 75% of US hospitals, while PitchBook projects that same industry's profits down about 54% by 2040

Tuesday, October 13, 2026 · Covering Oct 5–13 · Taipei time

Today's pile only takes shape when you read it together. Axios Pro reported on Oct 9 that Solventum, the 3M spinoff, has begun collecting initial bids for its Health Information Systems (HIS) unit, which one source says is being marketed at $5.5 billion to $7 billion. Per Solventum's own Aug 6 separation announcement, the unit turns $1.4 billion in annual sales, sits inside more than 75% of US hospitals, operates in over 30 countries and processes roughly 660 million clinical documents a month, its core asset a rules engine built on four decades of medical coding data. Someone, in other words, is willing to pay four to five times revenue for the fact that it already grows inside the hospital.

Two reports the same week set that price against the opposite context. Rock Health's Q3 market update, published Oct 12, counts $3.7 billion across 114 deals for the quarter and $11.1 billion across 358 year to date, with the average check up to $31.0 million — and no new public listing since HeartFlow in August 2025. Its conclusion: "can it be built?" has stopped being the limit, and advantage has moved to deployable value — integration, evidence, and a willingness to own liability. PitchBook's Q2 warning to sponsors is blunter: US RCM industry profits down about 22% by 2030 and 54% by 2040, with near-AI-native Waystar off roughly 43% year to date as the market "prices in disruption." Buyers are paying for embedded distribution; sellers are finding an exit from margins agents are about to eat. Both sides may be right — but not at the same time.

M&A / Divestiture SolventumHealth Information Systems10/09

Solventum starts taking initial bids for its HIS unit — what $5.5–7B buys is the fact that it already grows inside 75% of US hospitals

What

Axios Pro reported on Oct 9 that Solventum is collecting initial bids for its HIS unit, which one source says is being marketed at $5.5–7 billion, and frames the process as "unfolding amid a wave of RCM dealmaking." The thread starts with the Aug 6 separation announcement: $1.4 billion in annual sales, embedded in more than 75% of US hospitals, operations in 30-plus countries, roughly 660 million clinical documents a month, and at its core a rules engine built on four decades of coding data. Options include a tax-free spinoff, a sale, or a combination with a scaled peer, with close expected in 12 to 18 months; advisors are Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs and Cleary Gottlieb. The company puts its addressable market near $10 billion, growing 5–6% a year. CEO Bryan Hanson: "Health Information Systems is a differentiated business with a strong position and significant potential." GuruFocus's summary and Lawrence, Evans & Co.'s Oct 12 deal digest both note a spin-off remains possible and no buyer has been named.

Why it matters

This is the year's cleanest public price on embedded distribution. Four to five times revenue on $1.4 billion is not a software multiple; it is the price of a switching cost — replace us and you replace the claims workflow in 75% of US hospitals. And it has to win that bet against the industry profit curve PitchBook just drew in the same market.

Discount this

The $5.5–7 billion is a marketing valuation relayed by one source, not a bid range; Axios Pro sits behind a paywall and this report works only from the publicly visible headline and lede. The 660 million documents a month and the 75%-plus penetration are company-reported, with no third-party audit seen.

Market data Rock HealthQ3 202610/12

Rock Health Q3: $3.7B across 114 deals, average check $31.0M — and the IPO window has been shut for fourteen months

What

Rock Health's Q3 market update, Oct 12: US digital health startups raised $3.7 billion across 114 deals in Q3 and $11.1 billion across 358 deals year to date, a pace that would clear $14.8 billion for the year. The average check is $31.0 million, up from $29.5 million in 2025 and $20.6 million in 2024 — bigger cheques, fewer companies. M&A ran 45 deals in Q3 and 164 year to date, against 172 through Q3 2025. And there has been no new public listing since HeartFlow in August 2025; Oura reportedly postponed its IPO over market uncertainty. The fifteen-year retrospective adds scale: 2011 was $1.2 billion across 96 deals, the sector has raised over $140 billion since, and more than 3,200 venture-backed companies have taken $2 million or more. HIT Consultant's same-day write-up adds the consumer side: 57% of adults track health metrics digitally and 46% own a wearable.

Why it matters

The report's "deployable value" thesis explains what Solventum's bidders are thinking. When generative AI lets fast-followers match functionality at lower cost, "can it be built?" stops being the limit and advantage shifts to implementation depth, EHR integration, clinical validation and willingness to own operational and regulatory liability. A fourteen-month shut IPO window, meanwhile, leaves late-stage companies with private recapitalisations and dual-track M&A — which is exactly why assets like HIS are now on the table.

Discount this

The report gives no figure for AI companies' share of dollars — the headline says "AI-native platforms," the body never quantifies it. Scopes also differ: Rock Health counts US digital health startups while Corum and Capstone count broader health tech and healthcare IT, so the totals are not additive (see the next item). The $14.8 billion full-year figure is extrapolated from the Q3 average, not guidance.

Investment research PitchBookWaystarRCMQ2 2026

PitchBook's warning to sponsors: RCM industry profits down 22% by 2030 and 54% by 2040 — and Waystar is off 43% year to date

What

PitchBook's Q2 note, titled "AI Kills the RCM Star," lays out the numbers: $42.9 billion in PE-backed RCM deal value since 2017 and $22.5 billion in exit value, including a freshly announced $12 billion acquisition of Ensemble Health Partners. The sponsor thesis is to bolt agentic AI onto the labour-heavy back office, cut offshore staff and lift gross margins from roughly 20% to 30%, with a path to software-like 60–70%-plus. PitchBook calls it a trap: the same AI that expands incumbent margins also lets platform players bolt RCM on as near-pure incremental profit — EHR giants such as Epic, ambient-scribe and clinical decision-support vendors, and analytics players such as Innovaccer after its CaduceusHealth acquisition. Public markets voted first: Waystar, near-AI-native and growing organically around 11%, is down roughly 43% year to date, with the market "pricing in disruption." The earliest hard evidence is CommonSpirit Health paying Tenet's Conifer Health Solutions $1.9 billion to exit an RCM contract six years early, then insourcing the work.

Why it matters

This is today's counter-case, and the only side with a long-dated quantitative projection: cost-to-collect compressing toward below 1%, US RCM industry profits down about 22% by 2030 and 54% by 2040. If that curve holds, Solventum's buyer is not buying cash flow but a window of time before agents catch up. And CommonSpirit paying $1.9 billion to exit early and insource says hospitals have started to believe doing it themselves is cheaper than outsourcing — structurally bad news for anyone selling RCM as a service.

Discount this

In the public summary the starting figure for cost-to-collect is garbled — the text reads compression "from 4% to 5%," which appears to be a typo — so this report cites only the directional conclusion of compression toward below 1%, not the starting figure. Waystar's "down roughly 43% year to date" is as of the report's cut-off, not a live quote. The $12 billion Ensemble figure comes from Bloomberg's June 12 report; Fierce Healthcare's June 22 announcement does not confirm it, and the deal remains subject to closing conditions and approvals.

Health tech M&A back at high tide: healthcare IT deals up 23% to 179, and 97.6% of digital health exits are acquisitions

What

Fierce Healthcare's round-up of several trackers maps the wave: Corum Group counted 149 health tech M&A deals in Q1, private equity at 31% of them, with $22.7 billion in disclosed value against $30.3 billion for all of 2025; Capstone Partners had healthcare IT M&A up 23% year over year to 179 deals as of September, against a full-year record of 348 in 2025; Galen Growth put M&A at 97.6% of digital health exits in H1 (84 exits). Named deals include Thoreau Group taking control of Ensemble Health at about $12 billion, Universal Health Services buying Talkspace ($835 million), IKS Health buying TruBridge ($557 million), Frazier Healthcare buying MatrixCare from ResMed ($490 million all cash), New Mountain Capital rolling up the $1.49 billion RCM platform Smarter Technologies, Sword Health buying Kaia Health ($285 million), Hinge Health buying Cylinder Health ($105 million all cash, closed September), R1 buying Humata Health, Innovaccer buying CaduceusHealth, Hippocratic AI buying Grove AI and Abridge buying Altrina. Redesign Health's Neil Patel calls the wave a "sign of a healthy market" and the capital raised "war chests" for opportunistic M&A, adding that such deals are "both an offensive move and a defensive move at the same time." Solomon Partners' Eric Bormel says some high-quality digital health firms are "more than willing to pay in cash" to fund profitable growth.

Why it matters

Stack this against the previous two items: a fourteen-month shut IPO window, M&A taking 97.6% of exits, and at least six of the named deals being RCM or billing assets (Ensemble, Smarter Technologies, TruBridge, Humata, Knack RCM, EqualizeRCM). The money is not funding new themes so much as rehousing a cohort founded between the 2010s and the early 2020s — Fierce's analysis says plainly that capital is shifting toward AI-native companies, leaving the previous vintage to consolidate its way to profitability. Solventum's HIS unit is the largest single asset on that board.

Discount this

The piece contradicts itself on figures: a caption describes Rock Health's 115 H1 acquisitions as "just above" 2025's 199, which does not hold. Corum, Capstone, Rock Health and Galen Growth each count a different universe, so the totals cannot be added or compared directly. Many dates are given only as "this week" or across early 2026 to September, and some deals (OpenAI/Torch, Samsung/Xealth) predate this window and are listed only as context.

Funding General Medicinea16z10/06

General Medicine raises $120M Series B: PillPack's founder builds a store where 2,900 health services carry a price tag up front

What

Per the Oct 6 release, General Medicine closed a $120 million Series B led by Andreessen Horowitz, with Vineeta Agarwala leading for a16z, joined by Matrix, VXI Capital, Eli Lilly, Mercy Health (through Granger Management) and BoxGroup, taking total funding to $152 million. Founded in autumn 2023 by Ashwin Muralidharan, Elliot Cohen and TJ Parker — the latter two PillPack co-founders, now president and CEO — the product is a nationwide online healthcare store: search and compare, see insurance and cash prices before deciding, usually talk to a licensed clinician the same day, and use an AI chat to explore symptoms and options, across a catalogue of more than 2,900 medications, labs, telehealth visits, specialist e-consults and selected in-person visits and procedures. The company reports "hundreds of thousands" signed up, an average NPS of 80, 83% of customers saying it helped them address a health need they had been putting off, a third of those having delayed care more than six months, and an average customer health record spanning 11 years. Investing.com and Lawrence, Evans & Co. both rank it the largest digital health raise of the window.

Why it matters

The bet is that transparent pricing is itself the product. President Elliot Cohen's line is the business model: "When a customer can actually see their options and the price, the rest of the system has to compete." The cap table is the striking part — a pharma company (Lilly) and a health system (Mercy) in the same round, both of them parties price transparency squeezes, choosing to invest rather than resist, which usually means they think it is going to happen and would rather hold a front-row seat. It also corroborates the consumer-side return Rock Health flags.

Discount this

The NPS of 80, the 83%, "hundreds of thousands" and the 11-year average record are all company-reported with no third-party verification seen; the release discloses no revenue or unit economics, no specific prices and no state list (only "open nationwide," with offices in San Francisco and Boston). A $120 million Series B on $152 million total means barely $32 million before it, so the growth plan rests entirely on this round.

Big-player move GE HealthCareSOFIE Biosciences10/05

GE HealthCare buys SOFIE Biosciences for $945M, taking the PET radiopharmaceutical supply chain in-house

What

CNBC reported on Oct 5 that GE HealthCare agreed to acquire radiopharmaceutical and diagnostic imaging developer SOFIE Biosciences for $945 million to strengthen its radiopharma business; BioPharma Dive and DOTmed put the emphasis on the US network for producing and distributing PET tracers. Nelson Advisors' Oct 9 European round-up lists it as the week's largest medtech and diagnostics acquisition, citing a figure of "up to $945 million."

Why it matters

This is the window's only deal upstream of imaging AI, and what it buys is not an algorithm. In the imaging value chain the hardest link to copy is usually the production and cold-chain distribution of tracers and contrast agents: algorithms can be licensed and models open-sourced, but a PET tracer with a half-life measured in tens of minutes needs a physical network. GE putting $945 million here rather than into a model is a different answer to the claim that AI-natives can fast-follow any software feature.

Discount this

Sources describe the figure differently: CNBC and BioPharma Dive say $945 million, Nelson Advisors says "up to $945 million," implying possible milestones. The AI content here is indirect — this report includes it because it affects supply upstream of imaging AI, not because SOFIE is itself an AI company. The deal remains subject to regulatory approval, and this report did not obtain GE HealthCare's own press release.

What

Nelson Advisors' Oct 9 European round-up cites Dealroom: AI-building companies captured 48% of European health venture funding in 2026, against 22% in 2025. European deals in the window include Consonance Capital closing an oversubscribed $1.35 billion life sciences and healthtech fund, ambient clinical documentation vendor Tandem Health closing a $100 million Series B and deepening EHR integrations in the UK and Nordics, Biolevate's €30 million Series A for foundation models in preclinical target discovery and trial optimisation, and MedTech Solutions raising PLN 15 million while acquiring Good For You Medical. The jump from 22% to 48% is today's cleanest structural signal: in a single year European health venture moved nearly half its money to AI, while the count of European medtech companies posted its first net contraction. Read together, that is capital rotating out of a hardware-heavy market growing 1.8% and into software and models — the European edition of Rock Health's point that capital is shifting to AI-natives and leaving the previous vintage to consolidate.

Discount this

02 — Product Analysis

Two assets on the same RCM track, opposite verdicts: one being marketed at four to five times revenue, the other growing 22% and down 40% of its market value

Solventum Health Information Systems

A rules engine grown on four decades of coding data · Solventum (US, 3M spinoff)

Function and position. Medical coding, billing and clinical documentation software sold to hospitals, built around proprietary rules engines trained on four decades of medical coding data: $1.4 billion in annual sales, embedded in more than 75% of US hospitals, operations in 30-plus countries, roughly 660 million clinical documents a month. The company puts its addressable market near $10 billion growing 5–6% a year, with plans to expand into international payment markets. It is currently marketed at $5.5–7 billion with initial bids being collected.

  • Strength : penetration is the moat. Sitting inside 75%-plus of US hospitals at 660 million documents a month means a challenger must also persuade hospitals to replace their claims workflow — and four decades of coding data is the hardest part of training an autonomous coder to acquire from scratch.
  • Strength : the divestiture structure keeps options open. The company explicitly lists a tax-free spinoff, a sale and a combination with a peer side by side, with Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs and Cleary Gottlieb advising; if bids disappoint, it can still spin.
  • Concern : what it is today is a rules engine, not an autonomous coding AI. The announcement's wording is that the separated software business would "accelerate" AI-driven autonomous coding capabilities — meaning that is still ahead of it. The four-to-five-times-revenue price buys an asset that still has to be rebuilt.
  • Concern : the buyer's margin thesis is precisely the trap PitchBook names — the same agentic AI lets platforms like Epic bolt RCM on as near-pure incremental profit. In that scenario embedded penetration protects the channel, not the price.

Waystar（Nasdaq: WAY）

A near-AI-native listed RCM pure-play · Waystar Holding (US)

Function and position. A cloud RCM platform aiming AI at what it calls a $100 billion RCM labour pool: Q1 2026 revenue of $314 million, up 22% year over year, with agentic solutions launched across the whole revenue cycle.

  • Strength : growth and earnings quality are intact. Q1 revenue of $314 million, up 22%, and Meridian Growth Fund's letter notes record bookings and expanding margins in the prior quarter.
  • Strength : it is one of the few RCM assets that does not need rebuilding — the agentic products have shipped, rather than sitting on a post-merger roadmap.
  • Concern : the market is not buying it. PitchBook records it down roughly 43% year to date; it closed at $18.84 on May 21, 2026 for a $3.61 billion market cap, off 24.55% in a month and 50.34% over 52 weeks. Being AI-native and growing 22% did not defend the multiple — this is a repricing of the business model of selling billing services itself.
  • Concern : if cost-to-collect really compresses toward below 1%, the $100 billion labour pool Waystar is aiming at is also its own shrinking revenue ceiling — the same number is both the market size and the pool about to drain.

The difference is not technology but who carries the lag. Solventum's buyer uses private capital to buy a stretch of time in which switching costs still bite; Waystar's shareholders are asked to requote that same stretch every trading day. Rock Health says the moat has moved to willingness to own liability — and neither of these two assets makes its money by owning clinical liability.

04 — Taiwan Angle

The most expensive pool in America this week does not exist in Taiwan — which decides what Taiwanese medical AI can sell

(1) America's richest AI track has no Taiwanese counterpart. The unit Solventum is marketing at $5.5–7 billion, the $42.9 billion of PE-backed deal value PitchBook counts and the $100 billion labour pool Waystar targets all rest on coding, claims and denial warfare under America's multi-payer system. Taiwan has a single payer and one set of NHIA filing rules, so that pool barely exists here. That is not bad news but positioning information: a Taiwanese product built to help hospitals collect more money has no domestic buyer, which leaves clinical reading, in-hospital workflow efficiency, and exporting software as a regulated device.

(2) Three orders of magnitude apart in scale, opposite in growth. Set two numbers side by side: Solventum HIS turns $1.4 billion a year from one unit, while Acer Medical booked NT$58.496 million for January–September 2026 — roughly three orders of magnitude apart on a rough conversion. The direction, though, is reversed: Acer Medical grew 60.67% in September and 51.29% over nine months, while MedTech Europe records mature device markets growing 1.8% and European company counts contracting below 36,000 for the first time. For now the Taiwanese story can only be growth rate and depth at a single point, never scale — a scale narrative benchmarked against US assets simply does not add up.

(3) "Willingness to own liability" is the export ticket Taiwanese vendors actually need to prepare. Rock Health's conclusion this quarter is that once the build barrier falls, the moat becomes implementation depth, EHR integration, clinical validation and willingness to own operational and regulatory liability. Of those four, Taiwanese vendors most often arrive with model performance and most often arrive without the last two — and European healthcare AI adoption at 41% against a 54% general-industry average, with 28% of adopters reporting returns far exceeding investment, says buyers are not refusing to buy, only buying from the few willing to carry the risk with them. The other reality is exits: the US has had no new listing since HeartFlow in August 2025, which makes Taiwan's emerging-stock and OTC routes comparatively open right now — the mere fact that Acer Medical files monthly revenue is a kind of liquidity its US peers cannot currently get.
