Wednesday · Regulation & Policy

In a single day the United States produced two incompatible vocabularies for how far AI may act on its own: on Oct 13 the AMA issued a governance framework for agentic AI holding that clinical authority must be earned through evidence and maintained through demonstrated performance, and redefining licensure as authorising an AI system to operate in a defined clinical role rather than to practise medicine; the federal version of that same metaphor exists only inside an Aug 18 discussion paper that disclaims being draft or final guidance, carrying 26 questions and a two-axis risk model (four levels of autonomy against consequences from limited to severe), with the question of whether its postmarket half even sits inside existing statutory authority still unanswered and the comment window closing Oct 19, five days out; meanwhile the LLM-containing device that actually did clear 510(k), UpDoc V1.0 (K253281), got through under the product code Calculator, Drug Dose, on a 2018 predicate

Wednesday, October 14, 2026 · Covering Oct 8–14 · Taipei time

Three things inside forty-eight hours, all circling the same question at three different altitudes. On Oct 13 the American Medical Association published A governance framework for agentic AI assuming clinical functions, which breaks the question of whether AI may decide clinically into four questions — function, evidence and risk, authority and oversight, accountability — and treats the answer as a graded permission that can be withdrawn. It uses the word licensure deliberately, meaning authorisation for an AI system to operate in a defined clinical role. The same day, the Association for Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine took a briefing to Room 330 of the Cannon House Office Building to argue that oversight of health AI belongs to Congress. On the regulator's side of the table there is exactly one document: CDRH's Aug 18, 2026 discussion paper on generative AI-enabled medical devices, docket FDA-2026-N-7874, with comments closing Oct 19, 2026. A close reading by the FDA Law Blog finds 26 questions, a two-axis risk model, and premarket and postmarket concepts borrowed openly from medical licensure — and no answer anywhere to whether the postmarket half falls within FDA's existing statutory authority. Stack the three and the conclusion is not that the rules are too loose or too tight. It is that nobody has settled who writes them.

01 — Top Stories

In one day a professional body produced a graded framework, a trade body went to Congress for statutory authority, and the regulator's framework was still a questionnaire.
Professional governance AMAagentic AI10/13

On Oct 13 the AMA defined a new kind of licensure: not a licence to practise medicine, but authorisation for an AI system to operate in one clinical role

What

The AMA's framework for agentic AI assuming clinical functions, published Oct 13, 2026, rests on one claim: clinical authority should be earned through evidence and maintained through demonstrated performance, with both authority and oversight moving up or down as evidence, performance, risk or the technology itself changes. Four questions carry it. First, what clinical function is the system performing — documentation and monitoring at one end, triage, diagnosis, treatment and prescribing at the other. Second, what demonstrates it can perform that function, and what happens when it fails; higher stakes and greater autonomy demand stronger evidence. Third, what it should be permitted to do and under what conditions, on a range from informing and recommending to deciding and acting. Fourth, who is responsible for the risks and outcomes, allocated by authority, control, knowledge of risk and ability to prevent harm — developers, deployers, health systems, physicians or payers. The AMA says it will use these to steer its work on liability, licensure and regulation, and per Becker's, licensure here means authorising an AI system to operate in a defined clinical role, not a licence to practise.

Why it matters

This is the first time the largest US physician body has stopped at a principle — AI should support, not replace, physicians — and instead handed over a structure that can grade autonomy and take authority back. The vocabulary is the tell: licence, evidence threshold, sustained performance, reassessment. It is precisely the analogy FDA reached for in its Aug 18 paper. What differs is who may act on it. The AMA can simply publish and bind its own policy work; FDA has not yet answered whether it has the authority to implement the same metaphor.

Federal review framework FDA CDRHFDA-2026-N-787410/19

FDA's framework for generative AI devices is currently 26 questions: a two-axis risk model, review modelled on licensure, and five days of comment left

What

CDRH's Digital Health Center of Excellence published Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices on Aug 18, 2026 under docket FDA-2026-N-7874, with comments due Oct 19, 2026. The agency's own page states the paper is not draft or final guidance, does not propose policy changes, and does not set out CDRH's proposed or final expectations. Per the FDA Law Blog's Sept 3 reading, it poses 26 discussion questions and sorts risk on two axes. The first is the activity the GenAI function performs, in four levels of rising autonomy: non-directive, action directing, action taking with healthcare provider supervision, and fully autonomous. The second is the consequence of relying on an incorrect output, from limited to severe — mirroring ISO 14971's probability and severity of harm. The premarket concept borrows from medical training and licensure, including supervised practice, periodic reevaluation and public reporting, and splits into non-clinical benchmarking plus clinical confirmation (retrospective evaluation on real patient inputs, shadow deployment, standardised patient interactions, clinician adjudication, prospective studies). Postmarket, it floats periodic benchmarking, sample-based clinician review and degradation monitoring — and CDRH is weighing accepting greater premarket uncertainty in exchange for stronger postmarket monitoring.

Why it matters

What matters is not whether the framework is good but what standing it has. The FDA Law Blog notes the paper never addresses whether these mechanisms sit inside FDA's existing legal authority, and judges that the postmarket ideas may require new authorities while the premarket design-control suggestions appear to fit current processes. So a comment window closing in five days is collecting views on a framework that is, in part, without a statutory basis — and what ADLM went to Congress for the same day is exactly that basis. There is a practical gap too: trading premarket uncertainty for postmarket monitoring shifts the burden from a manufacturer's submission to a hospital's daily surveillance, and the paper asks no one in particular to pay for that.

The two axes, four autonomy levels, 26 questions and the premarket/postmarket detail all come from the FDA Law Blog's reading of the 8.8 MB PDF; this report did not read the PDF itself. FDA's page lists only four themes — risk assessment, premarket evaluation, postmarket monitoring, other — plus dates, and no tiers. The page's own dates are inconsistent (metadata says Aug 19, the body says Aug 18).

What

That shifts the stakes from review thresholds to whose data and whose interpretation is being displaced. If 80% of decision-making data originates in the laboratory, then output quality for generative AI is an input-governance problem on the lab side and a device problem on FDA's side, and the two jurisdictional logics do not line up.

Review in practice UpDoc V1.0K253281510(k)

The LLM-containing device that actually cleared 510(k) got through as a drug dose calculator, on a 2018 predicate

What

Per Innolitics' June 25, 2026 reading of the public FDA record, UpDoc V1.0 is prescription software for insulin management in adults with type 2 diabetes. FDA received the submission on Sept 29, 2025 and cleared it on Dec 23, 2025 as K253281 — a 510(k), not a De Novo — Class II, product code NDC under 21 CFR 868.1890, Calculator, Drug Dose, with Hygieia's d-Nav System (K181916) as predicate and an authorised PCCP. Architecturally the LLM handles only conversation and structured data capture by voice or text; a deterministic clinical service computes insulin instructions from provider-configured dosing parameters, glucose targets and safety protocols, and cases leaving the protocol escalate to a human. Innolitics notes the governing regulation is sparse and lacks detailed special controls, and that the public record does not identify the foundation model or the prompt configuration.

Why it matters

This case makes the gap in the two stories above tangible. While the AMA grades authority and FDA asks about four levels of autonomy, the way the current system actually clears an LLM-containing device is to confine the model to the conversation layer, hand decisions to a deterministic module, and house the whole thing under a 2018 dose-calculator predicate and a regulation with no detailed special controls. That is a cautious design — and the fact that it cleared is itself evidence that nobody has yet taken the hard case, an LLM on the decision path, through the door. The second signal is the PCCP. If CDRH really intends to trade premarket uncertainty for postmarket monitoring, the predetermined change control plan is the only lever that exists today, and this clearance is already pulling it.

First FDA-cleared clinical AI platform for real-time patient care is the company's own June 2026 wording; Innolitics states plainly that it cannot tell whether that release refers to K253281 or to a newer 510(k) not yet in FDA's public database. Measuring the gap KFFCMS950 / 60 / 182

The only quantified version of the gap: 200-plus companies cleared by CMS to test, four tools let past the standard pathway, and 60 of 950 cleared devices recalled 182 times

What

KFF's The Monitor, Volume 56, Oct 8, 2026 collects the federal pilots pushing clinical AI toward autonomy: citing the New York Times, more than 200 companies have CMS approval to test such tools; citing STAT, FDA allowed four of them for use with Medicare patients outside its standard approval process. The pilots include an AI chatbot delivering cognitive behavioural therapy and a federally funded project adjusting heart failure medication under legally required physician oversight. Set against that, the one quantified postmarket signal is an Aug 2025 JAMA Health Forum research letter on 950 FDA-cleared AI devices as of late 2024: 60 were recalled a combined 182 times, mostly for diagnostic or measurement errors, and nearly half of those recalls fell within a device's first year on the market. KFF also records FDA's position — generative AI outputs are unpredictable and shift over time, making them harder to evaluate; no rules are set for the category; comments run to Oct 19, 2026. One more number for contrast: a Sept 2025 KFF poll found 32% of the public would trust a health app or patient portal that uses AI to reach their records and give personalised information.

Why it matters

Put the three numbers side by side and the shape of the day is clear: authorisations to test autonomous tools (200-plus) are accumulating far faster than rules (26 questions, zero guidances), while the last generation of non-generative AI devices already shows that failures surface mostly in year one. That is exactly where CDRH's preferred direction is weakest. If recalls cluster in the first twelve months, then trading premarket uncertainty for postmarket monitoring places the detection window in the noisiest period, when the hospital knows the product least.

Discount this

The 200-plus and the four tools come from New York Times and STAT reporting that this report did not obtain, relayed through KFF; the 950/60/182 recall figures were published Aug 2025 with data through late 2024 and describe the non-generative generation, so they do not extrapolate to GenAI. The KFF poll measures stated willingness, not behaviour.

State law CaliforniaAB 1979 / SB 5032027-01-01

California vetoed the bill that would have stopped AI calling itself a therapist: three signed, two vetoed, and the two clinical ones take effect Jan 1, 2027

What

Per the California Health Care Foundation's Oct 8, 2026 summary, Newsom's Sept 30 deadline produced three signatures and two vetoes. AB 1979 (Bonta) defines a clinical decision support system as AI aiding decisions on timing of care, diagnosis or treatment; requires facilities, clinics and physician offices to take reasonable steps to ensure a licensed provider exercises independent judgement when AI informs care; bars AI from performing clinical functions reserved to licensed professionals or directing unlicensed staff to do so; exempts trainees and scheduling or routine communication; is enforced by the licensing boards; and treats businesses offering health care chatbots as providers under the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act. SB 503 (Weber Pierson) requires developers and deploying facilities to make reasonable efforts to identify clinical decision support AI liable to produce biased results harming access, care or outcomes on protected characteristics such as race, sex or age; obliges developers to reduce that risk and document training data, testing, intended use, failure risks and monitoring methods for deployers; and obliges deployers to keep monitoring tools and act on what they find. Both take effect Jan 1, 2027. SB 947 (McNerney) bars employers from letting an automated decision system alone justify firing or discipline — a human must verify against other evidence — from Jul 1, 2027. Vetoed: AB 2575 (Ortega), which would have barred retaliation against clinicians who override AI, and SB 903 (Padilla), which would have confined AI in psychotherapy to administrative and supplementary roles, required informed consent before AI recorded, transcribed or screened patients, barred marketing chatbots as therapy or therapists, and barred direct psychotherapeutic communication by AI unless FDA had cleared the tool.

Why it matters

The veto reasons are worth recording. Newsom called SB 903's definitions and scope broad enough to damage legitimate clinical tools never meant to replace psychotherapy; AB 2575 fell on the impracticality of its solely based on evidentiary standard and on the labour commissioner lacking the medical expertise to judge clinical standards of care. But SB 903 was the only state bill that tied direct psychotherapeutic communication by AI back to an FDA clearance. With it gone, that question returns whole to a federal paper whose postmarket half has no statutory basis and whose comment window shuts Oct 19. The two bills that did pass take the opposite route: they do not regulate the model, they regulate the facility's process and documentation. That is the division-of-labour signal of the day — states governing deployment, the federal government governing the product, and generative AI's risk sitting precisely in between.

European Union EU AI ActAnnex I2028-08-02

The EU pushed high-risk duties for AI embedded in medical devices to Aug 2, 2028: the rules exist, the date moved back a year

What

Per Gibson Dunn's May 27, 2026 client alert, the EU institutions reached provisional political agreement on the Digital Omnibus on May 6, 2026, confirmed by member state representatives on May 13. High-risk obligations move to fixed dates: standalone Annex III systems slip from Aug 2, 2026 to Dec 2, 2027, and Annex I systems embedded in regulated products — medical devices among them — from Aug 2, 2027 to Aug 2, 2028. The agreement also lets the Commission pare back AI Act requirements that duplicate sectoral law, narrows the definition of a safety component, moves the national sandbox deadline to Aug 2, 2027, and softens the Article 4 AI literacy duty from guaranteeing a level of literacy to supporting it.

Why it matters

Neither side of the Atlantic now has an enforceable, purpose-built rule for generative AI in medical devices — for opposite reasons. The American framework is still asking questions; the European one is written and dated later. For a manufacturer going to both markets, the binding constraint in this interval comes from buyers, notified bodies and insurers rather than from law — which means private machinery like CHAI's governance playbooks and the Joint Commission's RUAIH certification is effectively underwriting both regulators' delay. One device-specific detail: narrowing the safety component definition and paring duplicate requirements could meaningfully relieve software under MDR, but the agreement says nothing about how the Commission would apply that in the device space.

China split AI device rules into a horizontal and a vertical draft, and its revised device GMP took effect Oct 1

What

Per China Med Device's Oct 9, 2026 roundup, NMPA released a draft Guideline on Artificial Intelligence Medical Devices on Sept 14, 2026, setting a horizontal framework across the AI product lifecycle and its algorithms, followed on Sept 15 by a draft Guideline on Clinical Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence Multi-Disease Auxiliary Decision-Making Medical Devices, which lays down vertical clinical evaluation rules for complex AI products. Both are drafts; the roundup records no effective date or comment deadline for either. Line the three jurisdictions up and China is the only one currently writing purpose-built technical rules for AI devices while also attaching line-by-line inspection items. That does not make the rules better — a horizontal framework plus vertical clinical evaluation is likely stricter than current US practice — but it changes scheduling. Clinical evaluation expectations will be legible there before they are in the US or EU, and for a manufacturer, strict-and-knowable usually plans better than unsettled. The control group is FDA's 26 questions: same technology generation, one side issuing drafts, the other asking whether to.

02 — Product Analysis

On one side UpDoc V1.0, cleared through 510(k) with its LLM confined to the conversation layer; on the other Doctronic, operating around FDA on a state's promise not to enforce but with public operating data. Between them they mark the two available sources of legitimacy today.

UpDoc V1.0

Prescription software device, insulin management in type 2 diabetes, UpDoc (US)

Function and position. A clinician configures dosing parameters, glucose targets and safety protocols, and the system computes insulin instructions from them; the LLM layer only talks to the patient by voice or text and captures structured data, escalating when a case leaves the protocol. It does not diagnose, select therapy or replan treatment, and per Innolitics, what FDA cleared was not an autonomous LLM physician. The buyer is the prescribing clinic, slotting into an insulin-titration workflow that already exists.

  • Strength : it is one of the few LLM devices you can discuss by submission number — K253281, received Sept 29, 2025, cleared Dec 23, 2025, Class II, 510(k), predicate Hygieia d-Nav (K181916), with an authorised PCCP attached. While the rules are unsettled, a clearance number plus a PCCP is the hardest commercial asset available.
  • Strength : the architecture is the risk argument. Confining the LLM to conversation and data capture while a deterministic module decides maps onto the lowest rung of FDA's first axis, non-directive, and onto informing rather than deciding or acting in the third question of the AMA framework.
  • Concern : the housing is 21 CFR 868.1890, Calculator, Drug Dose, product code NDC. Innolitics notes the regulation is sparse, lacks detailed special controls, and names itself inconsistently in its own text. Using a dose-calculator regulation with a 2018 predicate to admit an LLM-containing product is not bad news for the next applicant, but it is bad news for regulatory consistency.
  • Concern : the missing data is specific — the public record does not identify the foundation model or the prompt configuration. Those are the dominant variables in a generative system's performance, so if postmarket monitoring goes the way CDRH is thinking, the baseline for what to monitor is simply absent from the record. The claim of being the first FDA-cleared clinical AI platform for real-time care is also the company's own, and Innolitics cannot confirm it refers to this clearance.

Doctronic（猶他州 AI 處方試辦）

Autonomous prescribing agent inside a state sandbox, Doctronic (US), outside the FDA pathway

Function and position. This is the other route on offer today: not FDA, but a state sandbox. Per Stanford HAI's abstract of Michelle Mello's JAMA Health Forum paper, Utah announced the pilot in January 2026 covering 192 chronic-condition drugs, with Doctronic's agent renewing prescriptions on consumer request and physicians initially validating its actions; the state agreed not to enforce its unprofessional conduct laws against the developer so long as it honours a contract carrying safety and privacy protections. Physician and pharmacist associations objected that AI should not be making care decisions. Utah widened the sandbox and named third-party assessors on Oct 5.

  • Strength : it is the only autonomous route with public operating data today. Per Forbes, citing five months of pilot data released by Utah's Department of Commerce in May 2026, the AI recommended approval in 72% of cases, physicians agreed with it 91% of the time on sign-off and asked for more information such as updated labs in the other 9%; the AI escalated to a physician on its own in 28% of cases, with physicians judging the escalation warranted 69% of the time and overly cautious in the remaining 31%. That is precisely the kind of material the AMA framework's second question asks for.
  • Strength : commercially it is being priced. STAT reported on Mar 23, 2026 that it raised $40 million to fund rapid growth; while federal rules are unsettled, a state sandbox amounts to an investable legal pathway.
  • Concern : its legality rests on a promise not to enforce rather than on an authorisation. A state agreeing to withhold enforcement of unprofessional conduct law is discretion, not permission; once the federal layer lands — FDA's four levels of autonomy, or whatever Congress writes — this route's standing has to be renegotiated.
  • Concern : the decisive figures are still missing. Forbes states plainly that its piece carries no total request volume, denial rate, safety events or patient satisfaction, and the only accuracy number — 80% agreement with human physicians across 500 telehealth consultations — comes from a company-authored, non-peer-reviewed study that is not part of the pilot data. The five-month set is also the state's May release, not the latest.

Its moat is where it sits in the data path: most of what generative systems consume passes through it. Its weakness is that what it wants is the slowest thing on the board — statute takes far longer than any guidance.

The competition today is not between vendors but between rule-writers. Four positions are moving at once: the one with statutory authority but a framework still asking questions (FDA), the one with definitional power but no enforcement (the AMA), the one with distribution but unsettled legitimacy (CHAI and the Joint Commission), and the state sandbox buying time with a promise not to enforce (Utah and Doctronic). The implication for a manufacturer is not to bet on one of them but to hold two assets at once: a nameable regulatory credential — a clearance number and a PCCP — and a body of operating data that answers what happens when it fails. Whichever lands first, FDA's four levels of autonomy, a new congressional authority, or the EU's Annex I in 2028, both will be demanded.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan sits exactly on the seam the day exposed: guidance exists for hospitals deploying generative AI, no rules exist for generative AI as a device, and the guidance itself explicitly excludes AI agents.

(1) Taiwan has already done the half FDA is still asking about — and only that half. Per GeneOnline Biotech Monthly, Jun 3, 2026, the Ministry of Health and Welfare issued Guidance on the Use of Generative AI in Health Care Institutions on May 29, aimed at public and private hospitals and clinics preparing to adopt or already running generative AI, across record generation, clinical decision support, administrative documents and patient communication. It names six risk classes: foundation-model risk, data-source risk, output risk (hallucination), security risk (prompt injection), user-dependence risk (clinicians leaning on AI until their own judgement erodes) and service-interruption risk (cloud outage, model withdrawal, vendor policy change). Its nine requirements run from pre-adoption risk tiering and an inventory of AI systems, through integration testing against EMR, HIS and PACS, to vendor contracts that must disclose the LLM's name, version and update mechanism, continuous post-deployment monitoring and incident reporting, and patient informed consent plus disclosure that the counterparty is an AI. But it is administrative guidance, not binding law — and it explicitly does not apply to AI agents, to non-medical AI, or to products falling under medical device regulation. Those three exclusions are, between them, the entire subject matter of today's AMA framework and FDA paper.

(2) Taiwan puts its evidence threshold at reimbursement rather than at review — the same shape as the AMA's claim, through a different door. Per Taiwan Healthcare's write-up of United Daily News reporting on a Jun 27, 2025 talk by Li Chien-chang, head of MOHW's Department of Science and Technology — last year's material, included as background — the department and the TFDA triage AI devices by risk class, with a licence comparatively easy to obtain and National Health Insurance coverage much harder: coverage requires a randomised controlled trial showing the tool saves the insurer money, after which it enters on a value-based payment basis, and such trials typically run two to three years, with the two bodies mentoring at least ten cases a year. Hold that against the AMA's line that clinical authority should be earned through evidence: Taiwan has been doing the same thing all along, only with the threshold placed at the payment gate rather than the market-entry gate. For generative AI that has one immediate consequence. If even a conventional AI device needs a two-to-three-year RCT before coverage is discussed, a system whose outputs change with each version can barely accumulate a closeable evidence file under the current payment logic.

(3) Third-party assessment already runs in Taiwan, but for a different purpose. The same reporting names MOHW's three AI centres: a responsible AI centre, an external validation centre and an impact assessment centre (source). On the American side the legitimacy of third-party assessors was publicly attacked by senior federal officials in 2025, and Utah only re-designated six of them on Oct 5 (STAT, Oct 5). Taiwan's three centres were established by the regulator itself rather than as a private coalition. That is steadier on legitimacy, at the cost of tying both capacity and independence to one ministry's budget. If the point ever becomes matching CDRH's periodic benchmarking plus sampled clinician review plus degradation monitoring, the external validation centre's actual throughput is the bottleneck — and that number is not public.

(4) On statutory basis Taiwan is a step ahead of the United States, but the health-specific rules have not landed. The Artificial Intelligence Basic Act passed its third reading on Dec 23, 2025, with the National Science and Technology Council as competent authority (CNA, Ministry of Digital Affairs). Which is to say: the thing ADLM went to the House to ask for — a legislative basis — Taiwan acquired late last year. The real difference is one layer down. After a framework act, health AI still needs MOHW and TFDA subsidiary rules to turn degree of autonomy, evidence thresholds and allocation of responsibility into enforceable text, and what exists publicly is one piece of administrative guidance that excludes AI agents. The practical upshot for Taiwanese vendors: until those rules arrive, what you can actually hold is a TFDA licence, validation records from the three centres, and the two-to-three-year path laid toward an NHI trial.
