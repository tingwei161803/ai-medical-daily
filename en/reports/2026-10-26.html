Monday · Clinical Applications & Research

Four FDA green lights, one real-world dataset, and an award for a paper on faking X-rays — a single week laid out the gap between how fast clearance moves and how thick the evidence is

Monday, October 26, 2026 · Covering Oct 15–25 · Taipei time

Line up this week's clinical AI news and something ironic emerges. On October 15 SimBioSys cleared TumorSight Risk through FDA 510(k), the first tool to read HR+/HER2− breast cancer recurrence risk directly off a breast MRI; the validation data compiled by OncLive show 10-year recurrence-free survival of 69.6% (95% CI 59.1–77.9) in the high-risk group against 88.9% (86.5–90.9) in the low-risk group across 2,138 patients, unadjusted HR 2.81 (2.03–3.88). On October 16 Korea's Coreline Soft took its 13th 510(k) for aortic quantification. On October 23 Eko Health's AI stethoscope Core 300 cleared the FDA, and the same day CaRi-Heart — granted de novo authorisation in July — published its first US real-world dataset, finding coronary inflammation in 42.9% of asymptomatic, conventionally low-risk adults. Then on October 20 RSNA gave its Margulis Award for paper of the year to a different kind of achievement: a study proving that only 7 of 17 radiologists spontaneously noticed their X-rays had been generated by GPT-4o. This issue sorts the six items by weight of evidence rather than volume of coverage, using as its yardstick the April npj Digital Medicine audit of 4,667 medical AI studies, of which 2.4% were randomised trials.

01 — Top Stories

Six items, ordered by weight of evidence: the ones with a retrospective cohort first, the ones with only a spec sheet last
Oncology imaging / prognostics SimBioSysTumorSight Risk10/15

Reading 10-year recurrence risk off a breast MRI: SimBioSys clears 510(k) on a 2,138-patient cohort where high and low risk differ by 19.3 points of 10-year recurrence-free survival

What

SimBioSys announced on October 15 that TumorSight Risk had cleared FDA 510(k) for "adult patients with hormone receptor–positive, HER2-negative stage I to IIIA breast cancer", deriving 5- and 10-year recurrence risk scores from an existing breast MRI. The validation compiled by OncLive comes from a retrospective multicentre study presented at the 2026 ASCO Annual Meeting: of 2,138 patients, 183 (8.5%) recurred; 1,815 were classified low risk and 323 high risk. Five-year recurrence-free survival was 95.5% (95% CI 94.6–96.3) versus 89.6% (86.4–92.6); at ten years, 88.9% (86.5–90.9) versus 69.6% (59.1–77.9). The unadjusted hazard ratio was 2.81 (2.03–3.88), and 2.18 (1.35–3.52) after adjustment for clinicopathologic factors, P = .002. Each 10-point rise in score corresponded to a 22% increase in relative recurrence risk; estimated 10-year recurrence ran from 6.2% in the lowest score band (0–10) to 34.2% in the highest (58–100). AuntMinnie and ITN covered it in parallel, and it appears in Health IT Answers' October 22 roundup.

Why it matters

HR+/HER2− is the largest subgroup in breast cancer, and the decision on adjuvant chemotherapy has for two decades been dominated by gene-expression assays — tissue shipped out, a report waited on, a bill paid out of pocket or fought through reimbursement. TumorSight Risk takes a different route: the patient has already had the MRI, the images are already inside the hospital, inference finishes the same day and consumes no tissue. If it genuinely adds prognostic information beyond standard clinicopathologic factors — which is precisely what the multivariable HR of 2.18 is meant to establish — what shifts is not one company's revenue but the cost structure of recurrence risk stratification itself.

Discount this

The validation is retrospective, not a prospective randomised trial. OncLive's write-up carries two mutually inconsistent sample sizes, 2,138 and 2,129, reports no AUC, and offers no head-to-head concordance comparison against established genomic assays such as Oncotype DX or MammaPrint — so the questions of whether it could substitute for one, and whose answer wins when the two disagree, remain open. The 5–10 year hazard ratio of 3.4 (2.0–5.6) is flagged in the original study as exploratory. Most important: a 510(k) clears the device to supply prognostic information, not to establish that acting on it improves outcomes, which would take a trial of an entirely different order.

Cardiovascular imaging Caristo DiagnosticsCaRi-Heart10/23

What the label "low risk" may be missing: CaRi-Heart's first US data finds coronary inflammation in 42.9% of asymptomatic adults — and in roughly 47% of those with no plaque at all

What

On October 23 Cardiovascular Business reported that CaRi-Heart, from the Oxford spin-out Caristo Diagnostics, had completed its first US study, run by the concierge clinic Biograph. CaRi-Heart analyses routine coronary CT angiography using the fat attenuation index of pericoronary adipose tissue, with no additional radiation or contrast. The findings: coronary inflammation in 42.9% of participants; "very high" inflammation in at least one major artery in 17.9%; and, among participants with no detectable plaque whatsoever, elevated pericoronary inflammation in roughly 47%. Biograph executive medical director Michael Doney puts it as more than 42% of "asymptomatic, conventionally low-risk adults" carrying elevated coronary inflammation. The technology received FDA de novo authorisation in late July, making it the first and so far only technology cleared in the US to quantify coronary inflammation from CCTA, with AMA Category III CPT codes 0992T and 0993T effective January 1, 2026.

Why it matters

The interesting number here is not 42.9% but the group with inflammation and no plaque. Current coronary risk assessment — calcium scores, plaque burden, Framingham-style risk factor models — all measure how far structural damage has already gone. Inflammation is a different axis, in principle upstream of structural change. If that axis is genuinely independent and genuinely predictive of events, then some share of the people classified today as low risk and left untreated are misclassified. This is one of the rarer cases in medical AI of growing a new variable out of an existing scan, rather than doing a radiologist's existing job faster.

Discount this

The discount here is unusually large. The report gives no cohort size, no follow-up duration, no hazard ratios, and does not name a journal or state whether the work was peer reviewed — only percentages. The setting is a self-pay concierge clinic and the participants are people who chose to pay for a full-body workup, a pre-test probability nothing like the general population's. The article itself says more research is needed to validate this and establish the prognostic value of FAI. In other words, "42.9% have inflammation" is for now a prevalence figure, and not yet a statement that those 42.9% will have events.

Image integrity / patient safety RSNAMount SinaiRadiology10/20

Paper of the year goes to bad news: only 7 of 17 radiologists spontaneously noticed the X-rays were AI-generated, and years of experience made no difference at all

What

RSNA announced on October 20 that the 2026 Alexander R. Margulis Award for Scientific Excellence, which honours the best original scientific article published in Radiology that year, goes to "The Rise of Deepfake Medical Imaging: Radiologists' Diagnostic Accuracy in Detecting ChatGPT-generated Radiographs" by Mickael Tordjman, Murat Yuce and colleagues at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, as reported by AuntMinnie and DOTmed on October 23; it will be presented at RSNA 2026 in Chicago on November 29. The study was originally published in Radiology on March 24, 2026: 17 radiologists from 12 centres across six countries, with 0 to 40 years of experience, read 264 radiographs, about half authentic and half AI-generated, split into two non-overlapping datasets — one from GPT-4o, one from Stanford's open-source diffusion model RoentGen. Unaware of the study's purpose, only 7 readers (41%) spontaneously flagged the synthetic images. Once told, mean classification accuracy was 74.8% on the GPT-4o set (individual range 58–92%) and about 70% on the RoentGen set (62–78%). Years of experience showed no correlation with detection; musculoskeletal subspecialists did significantly better. Of four multimodal LLMs tested, GPT-4o — the model that made the fakes — caught the most at 85.1%, with GPT-5 at 82.5%, both ahead of Gemini 2.5 Pro and Llama 4 Maverick, and none caught every synthetic image. One further result deserves attention: readers' accuracy in diagnosing abnormalities was 91.3% on authentic radiographs and 92.4% on synthetic ones — the fakes looked real enough not to disturb reading behaviour at all.

Why it matters

The award matters because it relocates the question. For a year the medical AI conversation has circled around whether models read images accurately; this paper asks something else — when the image itself can be generated, what is left holding up the credibility of the digital record. In the original press release Tordjman named three concrete scenarios: fraudulent litigation, synthetic images injected after a hospital network breach to manipulate diagnoses, and the undermining of the fundamental reliability of the medical record. Radiology was the first specialty AI reached and is therefore the first to hit this: the same generative technology used to train reading models is the technology that forges evidence. Subtler still is what the two findings imply together — that musculoskeletal subspecialists did better while years of experience did nothing. Spotting a synthetic image does not draw on accumulated clinical experience but on some specific sensitivity to texture and anatomical detail, and that is not what the present training system teaches.

Discount this

This week's news is the award, not a new study — the paper itself was published in March 2026 and the data are not fresh. Seventeen readers is a small sample, the design is retrospective, and the reading task was an experiment rather than a clinical setting, with participants aware they were in a study even if not of its exact purpose. The images were made with general-purpose models, not with adversarial tools optimised for forging medical records, so 74.8% would likely get worse rather than better under genuinely malicious conditions. Note too that the model versions named — GPT-4o, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Llama 4 Maverick — reflect the state of play at the time of submission.

Lung cancer screening EyonisMedian Technologieseyonis LCS10/21

The next layer of low-dose CT lung screening: Eyonis presents "from nodule detection to malignancy prediction" at I-ELCAP's 50th conference, claiming 93.3% sensitivity and 92.4% specificity

What

On October 21 Eyonis, a company of France's Median Technologies, announced it had been invited to give two presentations at the 50th I-ELCAP International Conference on Screening for Lung Cancer (October 22–24, held at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai), including "eyonis LCS in the Era of Foundation Models: From Nodule Detection to Malignancy Prediction" by Benoit Huet on October 23. eyonis LCS combines computer-aided detection of pulmonary nodules with computer-aided diagnosis of malignancy risk in a single device for low-dose CT lung screening; it received FDA clearance in February 2026 and a CE mark in July 2026, with commercial deployment underway in both markets. The company states performance of 93.3% sensitivity, 92.4% specificity and 99.9% negative predictive value, and claims that with LCS support readers gain 13 percentage points of sensitivity and 16 of specificity, with false negatives down 66% and false positives down 68%, citing an estimate from Jonas DE et al. in JAMA 2021 and the RELIVE MRMC study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06751576). Median Technologies trades on Euronext Growth Paris as ALMDT and also runs an iCRO imaging services business.

Why it matters

The real bottleneck in lung screening was never failing to find nodules but finding too many. The false-positive rate of low-dose CT, and the follow-up scans, biopsies, anxiety and cost that follow, are the most criticised part of the whole programme. Binding detection to diagnosis and emitting a malignancy probability aims squarely at that: if false positives genuinely drop 68%, what is saved is not reading time but an entire downstream chain of invasive workup. It also explains why products like this deliberately place themselves inside a "foundation model era" narrative — plain detectors are a red ocean, while stratification and decision support is the position still unoccupied.

Discount this

This is a vendor press release and every performance figure is the manufacturer's own, unverified independently. The release gives no AUC and no sample sizes, and does not explain how the reader-improvement figures — +13 points of sensitivity, −68% false positives — were derived from the literature it cites. RELIVE is a multi-reader multi-case reader study, not a prospective clinical outcomes trial: it can show reader performance changing, not patient survival improving. And an invited talk is a marketing event, not a clinical advance; the thing to wait for is RELIVE's full publication.

Chest CT quantification Coreline SoftAVIEW Aorta Metrics10/16

Squeezing more numbers out of the same chest CT: Korea's Coreline Soft takes its 13th FDA 510(k), for automated aortic quantification

What

Korea's Coreline Soft announced on October 23 that AVIEW Aorta Metrics had received FDA 510(k) clearance on October 16, the company's 13th. The software automatically segments the aorta from chest CT, measures diameter across anatomical zones and classifies aortic zones, producing curved planar reformation, cross-sectional and 3D views and an analysis report. Its existing US chest CT portfolio covered lung nodule detection (AVIEW LCS, AVIEW Lung Nodule CAD) and coronary artery calcium scoring (AVIEW CAC); this extends quantification from the lungs into cardiovascular territory. Partners include INFINITT North America (PACS-integrated "zero-click" automation, with a separate release describing a deployment at St. Joseph's Health), Optellum (lung nodule management pathways) and Oatmeal Health (lung cancer risk assessment and US reimbursement pathways). The company will exhibit at the RSNA 2026 AI Showcase, November 29 to December 3, Booth #5138.

Why it matters

The story here is not the aorta but the business model of opportunistic screening. A chest CT taken for lung cancer screening also happens to capture the heart, the aorta, the spine and the liver. Coreline's strategy is to stack ever more quantification modules onto that same image, maximising the information value of a single scan, and to use PACS integration to turn those modules into a background service the radiologist never has to click. The appeal to a hospital is direct: no new scan, no new consent, no new scheduling — only a new billing code. It is also the characteristic route by which Korean and European imaging AI firms get around the US incumbents: not competing head-on at interpretation, but selling quantification.

Discount this

The release carries no clinical validation whatsoever — no sensitivity, specificity or accuracy, no sample sizes, not even the K number or the formal indications-for-use wording. A 510(k) runs on substantial equivalence and establishes performance comparable to an existing device, not improved clinical outcomes. The company discloses no customer count, revenue or actual US installed base. And opportunistic screening is itself still contested on the evidence: quantifying an incidental finding does not make that finding worth acting on, and the cost of overdiagnosis never appears in a vendor's press release.

Primary care & home auscultation Eko HealthCore 30010/23

Putting AI back into the oldest instrument: Eko's Core 300 clears the FDA, switching between analogue and digital listening in one stethoscope

What

On October 23 Cardiovascular Business reported that Eko Health's Core 300 digital stethoscope had cleared the FDA. The design point is that one instrument keeps traditional analogue auscultation while switching to a digital mode for paediatric and adult patients, with up to 35x amplification, noise cancellation, real-time heart data shown on the device itself, and a battery measured in weeks. It connects to the Eko App and to the company's FDA-cleared AI models. Eko says it has shipped more than 750,000 devices; Core 300 is now available to US consumers with a gradual international rollout underway. CEO Connor Landgraf frames it as "bringing early disease detection confidence to every healthcare professional".

Why it matters

The five items above all happen on academic-centre-grade imaging equipment; this is the only one that shows up in a primary care clinic or a rural one. The stethoscope is the lowest-barrier, most widely distributed medical instrument there is, and early detection of murmurs and heart failure in primary care has always rested on the clinician's ear — one of the most variable links in the whole system. The decision to keep the analogue mode is worth noting too: it concedes that clinicians will not abandon twenty years of trained feel for an AI, so the AI is built as a layer added rather than a thing replaced. Against the other items here, which need CT, MRI and a PACS, this is an entirely different diffusion curve.

Discount this

This item has the thinnest evidence of the six. Everything the report lists is a hardware specification — 35x amplification, noise cancellation, battery life — with no clinical study cited, no sensitivity, specificity or other outcome data; "more than 750,000 devices" is a company figure and a commercial metric, not clinical evidence. The distinction matters: what cleared is the stethoscope hardware, while the AI models were separately cleared earlier, and the report does not say which models pair with Core 300, for which indications, at what performance. No pricing was disclosed.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products, one proposition: turning an image already taken into a number the patient would not otherwise have had

TumorSight Risk（SimBioSys）

MRI-derived recurrence risk score · SimBioSys (US)

Function and position. For HR+/HER2− stage I–IIIA breast cancer patients, it emits 5- and 10-year recurrence risk scores from an existing breast MRI, having cleared FDA 510(k) on October 15. The buyers are oncologists and breast surgeons, and the use is risk stratification feeding adjuvant therapy decisions. Per Fierce Biotech's coverage, the technical stack is tied to NVIDIA's compute.

  • Strength : the validation's scale and effect size are the hardest of the six items here — 2,138 patients, 10-year recurrence-free survival of 88.9% against 69.6%, an adjusted hazard ratio still at 2.18 (1.35–3.52), at P = .002. Mayo Clinic's Matthew P. Goetz comments that "MRI-derived assessments may provide prognostic information beyond standard clinicopathologic factors".
  • Strength : it consumes no specimen, requires no shipping and adds no scan, a structural gap in time and tissue cost against gene-expression assays; and the score is a continuous gradient — 6.2% estimated 10-year recurrence in the 0–10 band versus 34.2% in the 58–100 band — rather than a binary, which sits more easily inside existing decision frameworks.
  • Concern : there is no head-to-head concordance data against Oncotype DX or MammaPrint, which is the actual gate on clinical adoption — guidelines name genomic assays, not "any tool that stratifies". No AUC and no calibration curve are reported, and OncLive's text carries inconsistent sample sizes of 2,138 and 2,129.
  • Concern : the report does not say which scanners or acquisition protocols produced the retrospective cohort's MRIs. Sensitivity to equipment and protocol is the characteristic failure mode of imaging-derived biomarkers, and it usually surfaces only on cross-site deployment. The reimbursement pathway is also not yet settled.

CaRi-Heart（Caristo Diagnostics）

CCTA-derived coronary inflammation quantification · Caristo Diagnostics (UK, Oxford spin-out)

Function and position. It quantifies coronary inflammation on routine CCTA via the fat attenuation index of pericoronary adipose tissue, combining the FAI-Score with anatomical plaque metrics and patient demographics into a 10-year cardiovascular mortality risk estimate, having received FDA de novo authorisation in late July, with no extra radiation or contrast. The buyers are cardiology and the preventive-medicine or executive-screening end.

  • Strength : its regulatory position is distinctive — de novo rather than 510(k), meaning no substantially equivalent predicate existed, making it the first and so far only technology cleared in the US to quantify coronary inflammation from CCTA. Reimbursement moved first too: AMA Category III CPT codes 0992T and 0993T took effect on January 1, 2026.
  • Strength : it measures an axis current risk tools cannot reach. In the first US data, roughly 47% of participants with no plaque at all still showed elevated inflammation — if that holds, it is exactly the structurally normal but biologically abnormal group about which present stratification has nothing to say. Behind it sits the long evidence line of the ORFAN study, whose follow-up is claimed to reach 250,000 patients.
  • Concern : the first US data sits far below TumorSight Risk in evidentiary grade — no cohort size, no follow-up, no hazard ratios, no journal, with the report itself saying more research is needed to establish FAI's prognostic value. And the setting is a self-pay concierge clinic, so the direction of the selection bias is not in doubt.
  • Concern : Category III CPT codes are temporary and guarantee no actual payment, so reimbursement is still negotiated case by case. The more fundamental problem is what to do once inflammation is quantified — no trial has yet shown that adjusting treatment on the FAI-Score improves outcomes, and until that is settled, a 42.9% prevalence has mainly manufactured a population nobody knows how to manage.

The contrast is in evidentiary maturity, not technical difficulty. TumorSight Risk ran the standard oncology prognostics route: retrospective cohort first, ASCO presentation first, a multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio first, then clearance — and its next gate is a head-to-head against the genomic assays. CaRi-Heart is the reverse: regulation and a billing code arrived ahead of the clinical evidence, and the first US data is still at the prevalence stage. Both tempos are legitimate, but they mean entirely different things to a hospital's purchasing committee: the first can be argued inside an existing decision pathway, the second so far only fits a self-pay health screen.

Today's competitive structure is a stacking war fought on a single image. Four of the six items share one commercial logic: the patient has already had the CT or the MRI, and whoever can grow one more billable number out of that existing image wins — no new scan, no new consent, no new scheduling, just a new CPT code. 04 — Taiwan Angle

Translating the stacked-image logic to Taiwanese scale: NHI, self-pay, and the open gap in image integrity

(1) Reimbursement is the gate, but Taiwan is stuck at a different point on it than the US. The common premise behind this week's four clearances is a new code — Caristo has CPT 0992T/0993T (HIT Consultant), and Coreline's strategy says outright that opportunistic screening is how it sells into the US. Taiwan's single-payer system has no equivalent temporary Category III mechanism, so the route to adoption for imaging AI gets pushed towards self-pay. That was said plainly at the Ministry of Health and Welfare's 3D medical imaging conference on October 5: Taichung Veterans General Hospital honorary president Chen Shih-ann noted that 3D imaging "needs service and payment mechanisms", while some hospitals are applying for self-pay billing codes. Put differently, US imaging AI companies are fighting for insurance coverage while Taiwanese hospitals fight for a self-pay line item — which grows an entirely different adoption curve, and turns "which patients get this" into a question of ability to pay rather than clinical indication.

(2) Taiwan's position in imaging AI actually resembles Coreline's more than SimBioSys's. At the October 5 conference the domestic firm Anatomy Cloud donated 10 AI 3D imaging workstations, which the ministry will deploy at medical centres and public hospitals as demonstration units; NTUH president Yu Chong-jen set out a 2028 target for a hospital-wide automated AI 3D conversion framework, and Mackay Memorial president Chang Wen-han noted modelling time compressed to around five minutes (United Daily News). The ministry had earlier established three national AI centres. The essence of this route is Coreline's: stack tools and workflow onto existing images and compete on depth of integration, rather than challenge international guidelines with a 2,138-patient retrospective cohort. That is not a bad strategy — Taiwan's degree of health IT integration and the data concentration of a single payer are genuinely rare conditions globally. But this week's SimBioSys case is a reminder of something else: what actually changes treatment decisions is evidence willing to go prospective and argue with a guideline head-on, and that takes clinical trial capacity and long-horizon funding, not demonstration workstations.

(3) The risk the Margulis paper identifies has no corresponding rule in Taiwan at all. The study found radiologists at roughly 74.8% detection accuracy even after being warned, with experience offering no help (RSNA). Imaging data in Taiwan moves heavily between PACS systems and NHI claims, while the ministry is only drafting generative AI guidance for next year, and current medical device and information security rules were not designed for the category of synthetic medical images. Three concrete questions follow: is there provenance verification or an integrity signature when an image enters the PACS? For images used in insurance claims and forensic assessment, how is it shown they were neither generated nor altered? And once hospitals adopt generative image enhancement for denoising or super-resolution, how does an audit distinguish legitimate generation from illegitimate? The third is the hardest, because the AI 3D image conversion Taiwan is actively promoting is itself generation performed on an existing image.

05 — Further Reading

Chosen for pieces that help calibrate how much evidence a clearance is worth — not secondary retellings of the items above
  1. A quantitative analysis of global AI medical studies: gaps in randomized controlled trials — npj Digital Medicine 9:403 (2026-04-30)

    The yardstick for this issue. A team at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union audited 4,667 primary medical AI studies: 88.2% stop at preclinical or offline validation, only 113 (2.4%) are RCTs, 69.9% are single-centre, the median RCT enrolled 278 (IQR 80–800), and 71.7% specify no reporting guideline at all. The sharpest figure is that 82.3% of RCT primary outcomes were favourable — which the authors themselves attribute to publication bias. Read it, then look again at this week's four clearances.

  2. Deepfake X-Rays Fool Radiologists and AI — RSNA press release on the Margulis Award paper (2026-03-24)

    The original release on the award-winning paper, fuller than any of the secondary coverage: the complete author list, the composition of 12 centres across six countries, the two non-overlapping dataset design, and the three threat scenarios Tordjman names himself. Worth the primary source because it frames the problem more precisely than "can AI fake things" — the real problem is that provenance verification for the digital medical record was never designed in the first place.

  3. First Large-Scale Study Shows How Doctors Use AI in Medicine — Harvard Medical School on a JAMA paper (2026-10-15)

    This issue is about AI growing numbers on images; this paper is about what clinicians actually do with AI: some 950,000 clinicians, 270 million queries, 51.9% on clinical decision-making (treatment and management above all) and 13.9% on diagnosis, with internal medicine at 27.3% of queries against 14.5% of the physician workforce. The disclosure that matters: three co-authors are OpenEvidence employees and another was its chief medical officer during the study period — read it with that discount applied.

  4. Conversational diagnostic artificial intelligence in ambulatory primary care: a prospective feasibility study — The Lancet (2026-10-08)

    The AMIE prospective feasibility study from Google and Beth Israel Deaconess, 100 enrolled and 98 completing: AMIE's seven-item differential contained the established diagnosis in 90% of cases and its top three in 75%, with no statistically significant difference from the primary care providers on differential quality or management appropriateness, though the clinicians' plans scored better on practicality and cost-effectiveness. Worth contrasting with this issue's products: those add a number to an image, this adds a conversation before the visit — and this one was designed prospectively.

  5. AI Model Based on Facial Image Analysis Estimates Biological Age and Mortality Risk in Patients With Cancer — The ASCO Post (2026-10-20)

    The extreme version of the same proposition, from the same week: Mass General Brigham's FAHR-Face family, pretrained on more than 40 million facial photographs with 34,363 photographs of cancer patients for the survival task, reaching an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.23 for cancer mortality between the highest and lowest risk quartiles (P < .0001). Read alongside this issue the question sharpens considerably — if a selfie yields a mortality risk, where exactly is the boundary of an imaging-derived biomarker drawn, and by whom.
