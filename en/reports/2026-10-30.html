Friday · Product & Company Deep Dive

One market, two opposite bets: twelve people, $32M and 190 countries — against $1,000 CPMs and a $15B valuation

Friday, October 30, 2026 · Covering Oct 20–29 · Taipei time

The clinician answer engine has been the fastest-monetising corner of medical AI for two years. Today takes it apart. On October 27, Vera Health — founded in 2024, twelve employees in total — announced a $28.5M Series A led by Threshold Ventures, bringing lifetime funding to just $32M, on a claimed 800,000-plus clinicians across 190 countries: roughly one in four practising physicians in Spain and Italy, one in seven in France. The company says it has been the most-downloaded clinical app worldwide every month since June 2026, outdownloading OpenEvidence and Doximity combined in September. The counterweight is OpenEvidence: $250M at a $15B valuation on September 25, more than $1B raised inside a year, with Sacra estimating roughly $300M annualized revenue as of July 2026 at about 90% gross margin, ad CPMs above $1,000 and only about 5% of inventory sold. Both are free to doctors. One has bet its cost base on pharma advertising inside the United States; the other on twelve people and 190 countries. That is the contrast.

01 — Top Stories

Six of these eight stories are about buying distribution, not building models — this week's money went into slotting AI inside existing booking, reading and practice structures
Lead Vera HealthThreshold Ventures10/27

Twelve employees and $32M raised in total, against 800,000 clinicians in 190 countries: Vera Health's $28.5M Series A

What

On October 27 Vera Health announced a $28.5M Series A led by Threshold Ventures, with Mantis VC and SV Angel joining as new investors and existing backers Y Combinator and Gradient following on, taking total capitalisation to $32M. The company has twelve full-time staff across engineering and clinical roles. The product indexes more than 60 million peer-reviewed publications, clinical guidelines, consensus statements and FDA/EMA drug labels, and grades every piece of evidence before composing an answer — by study design, level of evidence, cohort size, recency, journal quality and citation impact — then colour-codes each claim by evidence strength, flagging thin support such as a single case report in red. Co-founders Taieb Bennani and Maxime Allouch are MIT graduates and sons of physicians; the company was founded in 2024 at Y Combinator and is headquartered in San Francisco.

Why it matters

This is the first time anyone has produced comparable distribution figures in this market on less capital than OpenEvidence raises in a single round. Vera reports zero enterprise marketing spend and growth almost entirely by peer referral; about one in four practising physicians in Spain and Italy use it, and one in seven in France. The sharper point is geographic complementarity: OpenEvidence's free tier is tethered to US pharma advertising and cannot naturally spill into markets with thin ad budgets, whereas Vera has been in 190 countries from day one. Vera Spaces, the enterprise tier that lets hospitals index their own order sets, formularies and antibiograms alongside the literature, is the only disclosed route to revenue.

Discount this

The 800,000 clinicians, 190 countries, download ranking and the claim of outdownloading OpenEvidence plus Doximity in September are all company-reported, with download data from Appark and no third-party audit; the release itself is paid distribution via PR Newswire. Whether a user is a registration or a monthly active, and how downloads relate to retention, is not disclosed. More importantly there is no third-party accuracy or clinical-outcome validation, and the evidence-grading methodology has not been peer reviewed. Vera Spaces pricing is undisclosed and the company reports no revenue.

Platform ZocdocInstinct10/28

While peers build walls, Zocdoc opens the door: its booking network now accepts AI agents, with agent traffic up nearly 30x since July

What

On October 28 Zocdoc said registered and approved AI agents may search for providers, check availability, verify insurance and book or cancel appointments on a patient's behalf, provided they obtain consent. Google Gemini, Amazon Health AI and Instinct, a voice-and-text agent startup, are already connected; others may apply. The terms bar agents from training on the data, reselling it or republishing it, though Zocdoc has not said how it will enforce that. Agent traffic on the platform is up nearly 30-fold since July. Founder and CEO Oliver Kharraz called the move "contrarian" and published an open letter welcoming agents in.

Why it matters

This is the week's clearest repricing of a moat. Zocdoc's asset was never an algorithm but a verified provider network wired into EHRs and scheduling systems; once agents start shopping for doctors on a patient's behalf, scraping the open web returns stale availability while Zocdoc returns a deterministic answer. The company launched Care Access Network in September, embedding its scheduling into third-party apps, insurer sites and AI tools, and in early October bought Sohar Health, which automates insurance eligibility checks — its first acquisition in nearly 20 years. Read together, the three moves position Zocdoc as infrastructure for the agent era rather than a consumer destination.

Discount this

The "nearly 30x" figure comes with no absolute base, and thirtyfold growth from a tiny base says little about scale. The coverage does not specify whether the integration uses MCP, a public API or a bespoke interface, Zocdoc has not said how the data-use restrictions will be policed, and the revenue split with agent operators is undisclosed.

M&A RadNetDeepHealthONRAD10/26

RadNet buys ONRAD for $350M cash plus 5M shares, giving DeepHealth's AI 130 radiologists and 225 hospitals to sit inside

What

On October 26 RadNet agreed to acquire teleradiology provider ONRAD for $350M in cash plus 5 million common shares, with up to $210M more payable to ONRAD shareholders in cash, stock or both if 2027 EBITDA targets are met. ONRAD, a Housatonic Partners portfolio company, contracts more than 130 radiologists serving over 225 hospital facilities in 26 states, offering remote final reads and in-hospital staffing including interventional radiology. The deal is expected to close in early 2027. ONRAD will then anchor a third reportable segment, Radiology Professional Services, alongside RadNet's outpatient imaging centres and its DeepHealth AI business. CEO Howard Berger said the combination "unites the capabilities needed to address the entire continuum of diagnostic imaging under one company."

Why it matters

This is a public statement about how imaging AI gets paid for: rather than licensing an algorithm to hospitals, buy the reading business and run the algorithm inside your own workflow. RadNet explicitly frames DeepHealth capabilities such as case prioritisation as the way to close the gap between rising imaging demand and radiologist supply — so the AI is monetised not as a per-study licence but as throughput per radiologist. Tuesday's funding wave already showed capital moving toward the channel that delivers AI into existing organisations; this is the same logic executed on a public company's balance sheet.

Earnings TeladocBetterHelp10/29

Teladoc's Q3 revenue fell 3% to $605.1M while its net loss narrowed from $49.5M to $26.9M — and the stock rose about 26%

What

On October 29 Teladoc Health reported third-quarter revenue of $605.1M, down 3%; a net loss of $26.9M, or $0.15 a share, against $49.5M and $0.28 a year earlier; adjusted EBITDA of $79.4M, up 14%; and free cash flow of $75.9M. The segments diverged sharply: Integrated Care revenue rose 2% to $398.5M with adjusted EBITDA margin up from 17.0% to 18.8%, while BetterHelp revenue fell 13% to $206.6M — consumer revenue down 25% to $174.5M, offset by insurance-covered services jumping from $3.7M to $32.1M. US Integrated Care membership was 100.1M, down 2%, and chronic care enrolment 1.344M, up 15%. The company said it is rolling out Teladoc One with early launch partners. The stock rose about 26% on the day.

Why it matters

The quarter is a sober baseline for AI in virtual care: revenue is still shrinking while EBITDA and free cash flow grow, and the market rewarded the cost structure rather than a growth story. BetterHelp's insurance-covered line going from $3.7M to $32.1M is the only order-of-magnitude move in the release, and it says the real leverage in this industry right now sits in reimbursement channels, not models. Against Vera and OpenEvidence, neither of which yet answers to a quarter, Teladoc is what this road looks like after the IPO.

Startup Sunlight AIBradford Medical10/20

Sunlight AI launches with a $17.4M seed round and buys its first practice — a software company deciding to be its own customer

What

On October 20 Sunlight AI came out of stealth with a $17.4M seed round and simultaneously acquired Bradford Medical Associates on undisclosed terms. The round was led by Lan Xuexhao, founder of Basis Set Ventures, with Sofeon Ventures, Social Leverage, Actions Capital, Overlook Ventures, Early Light Ventures and angels including Evolent Health founder Frank Williams. Founder Sina Chehrazi previously started Nayya Health; the company operates from New York and Miami. The offering combines AI software, practice-management infrastructure and human support, pitched on lower administrative cost and better insurance collections.

Why it matters

The argument is more interesting than the round. Between 2018 and 2026 hospitals and corporations acquired more than 85,000 practices, leaving roughly 18% of physicians independent, while a Yale/NBER study of 276 hospital acquisitions found prices rose 15.1% within two years with no measurable quality gain. Sunlight uses those figures to argue independence is both cheaper and clinically sound. But choosing to buy practices rather than only sell software concedes that pure SaaS cannot reach this market — and turns the company into a capital-intensive roll-up, which $17.4M will not fund for many deals.

Policy HHSASTP/ONC10/28

Washington withdraws the AI "nutrition label" requirement — and disclosure goes back to being whatever vendors choose to publish

What

Politico Pro reported, and HIStalk relayed on October 28, that the federal government is rolling back the requirement for AI-containing health IT to carry a "nutrition label" listing safety and development information. It runs with the grain of the HTI-5 final rule issued on October 26, which is framed around reducing administrative burden for developers in the ONC Health IT Certification Program and which commentator Eugene Vestel reads as deferring key in-EHR AI decisions or handing them back to vendors. This daily covered HTI-5's struck provisions and cost estimates in Wednesday's regulation edition; what is recorded here is only the product-side consequence.

Why it matters

The item is worth reading against today's lead. Once disclosure of training data, validation method and intended population is no longer mandatory, how much a product chooses to disclose becomes differentiation in itself. Vera puts evidence grading and colour-coding in the interface where users see it, states publicly that it never trains on clinicians' queries, and holds SOC 2 Type II — compliance in a world with mandatory labels, a selling point in a world without. The flip side is that buyers can no longer compare products against an official label, so hospitals must build the evaluation capability themselves.

User research JAMA Network Openn=1,18510/28

Patients will trade human review for speed, but only up to a point: 57% against 63% in a survey of 1,185 adults

What

A survey of 1,185 US adults published in JAMA Network Open found that for logistical questions such as prescription refills, 57% preferred an AI response within five minutes; but for symptoms, treatment or clinical concerns, 63% would rather have a clinician-reviewed reply. Respondents preferred human therapists even at the cost of a longer wait. The authors read it as evidence that willingness to trade human oversight for speed scales with how much is at stake.

Why it matters

The figures explain why this week's money went to clinician-facing rather than patient-facing AI. Handing an answer straight to the patient has a market for logistics and none for clinical questions; handing it to a clinician who then underwrites it works at both ends. That Vera and OpenEvidence both sell to clinicians and both make traceability to the primary literature their core interface is not a coincidence.

Discount this

This is a stated-preference survey, not observed behaviour, with 1,185 US adults and the usual gap between what people say and what they do. This entry is written from HIStalk's secondary summary; the full paper was not obtained.

Security Johns HopkinsQuest Diagnostics10/28

One employee uploaded a spreadsheet to a consumer AI tool and Johns Hopkins Bayview notified 3,182 patients — shadow AI is this year's cheapest breach path

What

Johns Hopkins Medicine notified 3,182 Bayview Medical Center patients that their information may have been exposed after an employee uploaded a spreadsheet to a consumer AI tool, discovered during a late-September internal review. The same roundup records that Quest Diagnostics restored its Quanum Lab Services Manager ordering interface after an October 21 cyberattack without yet knowing what data was accessed, and that the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center reverted to downtime procedures after a ransomware attack.

Why it matters

The item lands differently on a product day. Vera's public commitments — never training on clinicians' queries, HIPAA, GDPR, SOC 2 Type II — read like boilerplate until you notice that these 3,182 patients were exposed by a tool offering none of them. If a hospital does not supply a compliant clinical answer tool, clinicians will find one themselves. That is the real reason a free, clinician-open product can reach 800,000 users with no enterprise marketing, and it is also the hardest line for hospital security to hold.

02 — Product Analysis

Two interfaces that take a question and return a sourced paragraph — built on entirely different cost structures and assumptions about distribution

Vera Health

Evidence-graded clinical search · Vera Health (San Francisco, founded 2024)

Function and position. Indexes more than 60 million peer-reviewed publications, clinical guidelines, consensus statements and FDA/EMA drug labels, retrieving and grading evidence before generating an answer. Free to licensed clinicians and trainees worldwide; the Vera Spaces enterprise tier lets hospitals fold their own order sets, formularies and antibiograms into the search so results reflect local practice. A partnership with the American College of Emergency Physicians embeds ACEP clinical policies and decision trees with attribution.

  • Strength : it turns uncertainty into interface rather than a disclaimer. Every claim is coloured by evidence strength, with thin support such as a small retrospective case series flagged red and its limitations shown; ACEP's Dr Scott M. Silvers puts it as "a large, randomized trial and a case report should never carry the same weight." That addresses the single most dangerous property of a general chatbot at the bedside: every sentence sounds equally confident.
  • Strength : the cost base is extremely light. Twelve employees, zero enterprise marketing spend, growth by peer referral — and 190 countries of coverage. That lets it serve markets no advertising revenue could support, which is precisely where its rival structurally cannot go.
  • Concern : there is no published third-party accuracy validation or clinical-outcome study. How the grading weights are set, and whether they have been clinically validated, is not described, and the methodology has not been peer reviewed. For a clinical tool claiming 800,000 users, that is the largest gap on the page.
  • Concern : no disclosed revenue. Vera Spaces pricing, signed hospitals and renewal rates are all undisclosed. How long $32M funds a twelve-person team maintaining a 60-million-document index, service in 190 countries and enterprise deployments is an open question.

OpenEvidence

Ad-funded clinical answer platform · OpenEvidence (Miami, founded 2022)

Function and position. Free to verified US physicians and funded by pharmaceutical and medical-device advertising. Licensed content spans NEJM, the JAMA network, the AMA, NCCN, AAFP and ACEP; the product line includes DeepConsult (July 2025, a reasoning mode that synthesises across studies) and a voice mode launched in May 2026, alongside a May 2026 Cedars-Sinai integration into that hospital's EHR allowing patient-context queries. In September it added a Memorial Sloan Kettering integration into Epic with OncoKB folded in, and announced it will build its own drug pipeline on MSK and NCCN data.

  • Strength : distribution and unit economics are both already running. Sacra estimates roughly $300M annualized revenue as of July 2026, up from $150M at end-2025 and $7.9M at end-2024, at about 90% gross margin, with about 20 million consultations a month in January 2026 against roughly 3 million a year earlier. Being free let it bypass hospital procurement cycles entirely — the road rivals spend two years failing to finish.
  • Strength : ad inventory is far from sold out, so revenue has structural headroom. CPMs run above $1,000 while only about 5% of inventory is sold. The valuation is therefore a bet not on recruiting more physicians but on how many times over the same physicians' attention can be sold.
  • Concern : free evidence-based medicine funded by pharmaceutical advertising is a structural conflict of interest, and there is no published account of the separation — how ads are kept apart from answer content, or whether advertisers can target specific clinical queries, is not disclosed in any verifiable form. With the federal AI disclosure label withdrawn, nothing will compel that account either.
  • Concern : the three named risks are all unresolved. Sacra lists malpractice and regulatory exposure if a recommendation contributes to an adverse outcome; heavy dependence on Epic and Oracle-Cerner for enterprise expansion, either of which could block integrations or demand revenue share; and rising content-licensing fees from NEJM, JAMA, Wiley and Cochrane squeezing the ad-funded free model. Building an in-house drug pipeline also moves the company from software risk into clinical development risk, on an entirely different capital and time scale.

The real divergence is not the model but who pays so that doctors do not have to. OpenEvidence's answer is US pharma, so its geographic boundary is the boundary of pharma ad budgets, and the product must keep physicians inside its own interface to have inventory to sell. Vera's answer is venture capital for now and hospitals buying Vera Spaces later, so its boundary is whether hospitals will pay for localised retrieval. The first has proved revenue; the second has proved only distribution. Two numbers are worth watching over the next twelve months: whether OpenEvidence's 5% sell-through rises, and whether Vera names a first paying hospital.

Today's structure is a funnel cut into three sections, each with someone collecting rent. Of this week's eight stories only Vera's is about building product in the middle; every other dollar went to buying position upstream or down. That usually means the technical gap in the middle is no longer wide enough, and whoever locks the channel first wins.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwanese physicians already use these tools — just versions built for somebody else's market

(1) Taiwan is already a user of this market without being a target of it. The Taipei Veterans General Hospital library listed OpenEvidence as a recommended AI clinical decision support platform as early as November 2024, and Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital's library carries the same recommendation. But OpenEvidence is free because US pharmaceutical advertisers pay for it — Taiwanese physicians are a net cost in that model, not a revenue source, so there is no product reason to localise for Taiwan's reimbursement rules, drug formulary or domestic guidelines. What makes today's story matter here is that Vera's free-in-190-countries structure is one of the few that counts Taiwan in, and its enterprise tier is precisely "let the hospital fold its own formulary and antibiogram into the search" — the layer Taiwan's medical centres most lack and are least likely to get from a foreign vendor unprompted.

(2) Someone in Taiwan is already building the same thing, small but pointed at the right problem. A-Pen AI, from the Taiwanese company behind the country's largest verified medical-professional community app, announced at InnoVEX in June 2026 an unlimited free tier, with no token cap, for physicians who verify their practising licence — combining NEJM, The Lancet and Nature content with Taiwan's National Health Insurance rules, local drug data and the clinical discussions of 88,000 medical professionals on its platform; it closed an NT$50M Series A earlier this year with Japan's DCI Partners participating. The same report cites the company's 2025 survey: 66% of physicians already use AI tools clinically, 57% say the most valuable use is relieving administrative and information-gathering load, and 68% see real benefit while remaining highly concerned about accuracy, source credibility and where responsibility lands. That last concern is exactly what Vera's evidence colour-coding and OpenEvidence's expensive journal licences are each trying to answer — Taiwan is simply doing it with three orders of magnitude less capital.

(3) Reimbursement is the real bottleneck for this market in Taiwan. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has stood up a national AI impact research centre whose clinical trials of AI deployment are meant to inform National Health Insurance joint reimbursement deliberations, and has said plainly that medical AI must first show it saves the insurance system money to win payment, with some items already in clinical trials. That gives today's contrast local bite, because a clinical answer engine is among the hardest products to prove savings for: it improves decision quality and lookup time without mapping onto any reimbursement code. In Taiwan, in other words, these tools will for now travel either on hospital budgets or on a vendor's free tier, not on insurance. What hospitals have to weigh is therefore not payment but the warning in today's eighth story: if you do not supply a compliant tool, clinicians will upload the spreadsheet themselves.
