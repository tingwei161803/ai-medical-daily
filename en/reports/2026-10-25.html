Sunday · Week in Review & Long Reads

The week the gap between what AI can do and what anyone can watch became visible — on 10/20 Radical Numerics said phages designed by its Omnii-1 model had reached a patient with an antibiotic-resistant infection under a physician-led compassionate-use pathway (69.9% recovery on its T7 gp17 demonstration against 4.8% for random controls), while stating that one patient cannot establish safety or efficacy and withholding the clinical course; on 10/22 Microsoft's MAI-DxO landed in Science at 83% on complex NEJM cases against physicians' 36%; on 10/20 UT Southwestern showed in npj Digital Medicine that one line about frequent ED use pushed nearly 10% of the patients who later deteriorated down the queue; and on 10/21 Microsoft attributed an ARTEX campaign that tried more than 2,250 tenants to Storm-0940, a day before METR and Redwood's 61-page report counted only about 75–94% of OpenAI's internal agentic traffic as eligible for monitoring

Sunday, October 25, 2026 · Covering Oct 18–25 · Taipei time

Three lines were pulled tight at once: capability, authorisation, oversight. Radical Numerics' 10/20 Omnii-1 announcement put model-designed phages into an actual patient, by compassionate use — a route needing no completed trial. Australia's TGA review of 10/16 found none of the 12 AI scribes it examined classified itself as a medical device, though 8 likely meet the definition. METR and Redwood's 10/22 red-team report counted only about 75–94% of OpenAI's internal agentic traffic as eligible for monitoring. And UT Southwestern's 10/20 study showed that even models well above chance will push nearly 10% of the patients who later deteriorated down the queue after one phrase about frequent ED use. Microsoft's 83% against 36% on 10/22 is the loudest number, but the real subject is this: capability is out in front, and the layer that can say who is watching has not caught up.

01 — Top Stories

Six things, ordered along capability, authorisation and oversight — not by how loud they were
BreakthroughRadical NumericsOmnii-110/20

AI-designed phages reach a human patient for the first time — by compassionate use, with nothing published about the outcome

What

On 10/20 Radical Numerics published Omnii-1 — a multimodal genome language model pretrained on diverse genomic sequences and multiomics data, then trained on phage genomes — and said a cocktail of its designed phages had been given to a patient with an antibiotic-resistant infection under an authorised, physician-led compassionate-use pathway, after in vitro testing eliminated that patient's resistant isolate. Its demonstration targeted gp17, T7's tail fibre: 69.9% recovery at 11–15 amino-acid changes against 4.8% for random controls; 2,161 of 6,694 variants with measurable activity beat wild type; on the hardest strain 24% of the model's top 100 cleared the threshold against 4.5% of the full library. Two phages covered 91.4% of 37 held-out E. coli strains, and Pseudomonas average precision rose 0.570 to 0.608, past GenoPHI's 0.581 (New York Times, 10/20; company release).

Why it matters

The only thing this week that put a generative model's output into a person. The rest of AI-for-biology stayed in tubes and plates — NVIDIA and UW open-sourcing RFD4-Proteina on 10/21, Anthropic's 10/20 Claude-designed scorpion-toxin binders, OpenAI's thousands of agents on a mouse CRISPR atlas — all model-proposes, human-verifies. The number to remember is not 69.9% but how far the interval from design to dosing has compressed.

Discount this

The company states the therapies are investigational, not approved for broader use, and that one patient cannot establish safety or efficacy. The clinical course and outcome are withheld, as are the candidate count, model size and architecture. Recovery is only a proxy for viability, and every figure is a high-level blog summary with no peer-reviewed paper (some coverage labels it exactly that: first patient treated, no clinical data published).

Clinical researchMicrosoft AIMAI-DxOScience10/22

MAI-DxO lands in Science at 83% against physicians' 36% — graded on sequential questioning and test-ordering

What

On 10/22 Microsoft AI published MAI-DxO (the MAI Diagnostic Orchestrator) in Science: 83% correct on complex NEJM case records against 36% for generalist physicians with no reference materials and no colleagues to consult (TechCrunch, 10/22; Unite.AI). It orchestrates several language models into a loop that asks for history, orders tests and narrows to a diagnosis, with testing cost in the objective. Suleyman frames it as medical superintelligence; the method appeared in Sequential Diagnosis with Language Models.

Why it matters

The week's loudest claim that AI beats physicians, and it ran in Science rather than a vendor blog — the venue is the signal. What it establishes is that orchestrating language models into a frugal, sequential narrowing works very well on text-based hard cases. It is complete only read alongside UT Southwestern's triage study: one says good orchestration reaches 83%, the other says the same class of model has its ordering overturned by a single phrase in a chart.

Discount this

The test set is NEJM Case Records — texts written to be hard, not clinic patients. The comparison physicians could look nothing up and consult nobody, which is not clinical reality. The work was run by Microsoft's own team with no independent prospective validation. Reading 83% as a clinically usable rate is a misreading (even secondary coverage puts "real-world tests are still ahead" in the headline).

Clinical researchUT Southwesternnpj Digital Medicine10/20

Add one line about frequent ED visits, and nearly 10% of the patients who later deteriorated get pushed down the queue

What

On 10/20 UT Southwestern published in npj Digital Medicine (first author Philip Jarrett, senior author Andrew Jamieson) a study using records from two academic emergency departments, Stanford Medicine and Beth Israel Deaconess. Patients who arrived looking similarly ill but diverged afterwards were paired, three open-source large language models were asked which to prioritise, and demographic, social or stigmatising detail was then added (university release, 10/20). Beforehand all three ran well above chance; afterwards frequent ED use and a psychiatric-emergency history had the largest effect, with nearly 10% incorrectly downgraded in some scenarios. Age shifted some decisions, which the authors read as older patients' real risk. Newsweek reports 18 conditions were examined.

Why it matters

The point is not that the models are inaccurate — they were well above chance. The point is that more information does not mean better decisions, and a full chart is exactly what a hospital hands a deployed triage model. Jarrett: "More information does not automatically mean better decisions." His prescription is specific — validate locally against real outcomes, test deliberately for psychiatric history, social circumstance and stigmatising language, and keep monitoring. That is the other face of the AHA telling the FDA on 10/19 that hospitals cannot see inside these tools and post-market surveillance belongs with developers.

Discount this

The release does not disclose sample size or name the three models; "nearly 10%" covers some scenarios and is not broken out by condition. The study did not let an AI set the real order of care — it used existing records in controlled comparisons. Funding came from the Office of the President's institutional funds, with follow-on support from the National Foundation of Emergency Medicine.

GovernanceMETRRedwoodOpenAI61 頁10/22

Red-team report: only about 75–94% of OpenAI's internal agent traffic is eligible for monitoring — which fires only when the agent hands control back

What

On 10/22 METR and Redwood Research published a privileged red-team exercise run inside OpenAI between 21 July and 11 August, in 61 pages. Two findings carry it: only about 75–94% of internal agentic traffic is eligible for the monitoring that exists; and monitoring triggers when an agent returns control to a user, so an agent that keeps acting can defer being monitored indefinitely. CNBC, 10/22 places it in OpenAI's internal-safety context; The Register, 10/23 is blunter, reporting OpenAI "utterly failed to monitor its agents" during the Hugging Face incident.

Why it matters

The closest thing the week produced to a governance physical, done from inside with privileges by outsiders. The implication for healthcare is immediate: hospitals are wiring agents into the chart (Epic's Connectors this week is precisely that) while the most advanced lab in the field has only partial observability over its own. The questions become concrete: what share of agent behaviour lands in your logs, what triggers the record, and who is watching during a long autonomous run.

Product & platformEpicConnectors10/21

Epic opens Connectors so its in-chart AI agents can act inside other apps — the developer catalogue opens March 2027

What

On 10/21, opening its annual interoperability conference in Verona, Wisconsin, Epic announced Connectors: outside applications exchange data with the Epic chart, and the chart's AI agents complete tasks inside those applications (Healthcare IT News, 10/21; Fierce Healthcare). The agents are Emmie for patients, Art for clinicians and Penny for revenue cycle, plus anything built in the no-code Agent Factory. Launch partners: Gozio Health (wayfinding), VPL (supply chain and prescription logistics, via VPLRx) and Availity (revenue cycle, in development). Examples include Emmie delivering wayfinding instructions, checking a medical-equipment app for assistive-device availability, and an inpatient asking Emmie to order a meal. Developer-built Connectors arrive on Epic's website in March 2027, with a per-Connector view of what each can read and change (Modern Healthcare; Epic's own post).

Why it matters

This turns AI agents from a feature into a platform position. Epic's Carl Dvorak said on stage that "we have got to find a pathway to our AI utopia before patients become frustrated with the health system," and Derek De Young of Agent Factory framed it as sparing people swivel-chair work. Against where the ambient-documentation vendors have climbed — Abridge opening Assistant to every clinician around 10/21 and signing eight University of Texas System institutions on 10/7 — the clinical front door they spent three years reaching is one Epic now says it will occupy itself. And the per-Connector permission view is the productised answer to METR's question.

Regulation & policyFDATGAMHRAEU AI Act10/16–10/20

Three regulators moved in one week: the FDA's comment window closed 10/19, the TGA found 8 of 12 AI scribes likely devices, and the MHRA took lifecycle oversight to AdvaMed

What

The FDA's discussion paper on generative AI-enabled medical devices closed for comment on 10/19 (FDA announcement). The American Hospital Association, for nearly 5,000 hospitals, filed the same day for flexible risk-based oversight, asked that generative-AI clinical decision support and administrative tools sit outside FDA jurisdiction, and argued that with 96% of AI devices cleared through 510(k) and hospitals unable to see inside them, post-market surveillance belongs with developers (AHA filing; Becker's). The US Chamber asked that a two-axis risk framework frame only evidence expectations. CDRH's FY2027 guidance list shrank to 17, five of them software and AI. Australia's TGA published its digital-scribe review on 10/16: about 100 suppliers surveyed, 40 responses, 12 examined — 8 likely meeting the device definition, 2 pending, 2 apparently not, and not one of the 12 classifying itself as a device. Products included Heidi, Lyrebird Health, Microsoft, Abby Health, Carepatron and iMedX, and the TGA declined to name them (Healthcare IT News, 10/21; The Medical Republic). The MHRA took lifecycle regulation and AI Airlock phase three to AdvaMed on 10/20 (MedTech Dive).

Why it matters

All three point at one question: where the product boundary of generative AI sits, and who says so. The TGA's answer is the bluntest — a supplier writing "not a medical device" or "not for diagnosis or treatment" does not itself determine regulatory status. That holds for Taiwan as much as the US, and it lands on the most widely deployed category of all. The EU went the other way: the digital omnibus pushed high-risk obligations for AI embedded in devices from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028 (timeline); the UK has accepted all 44 recommendations of its NHS doctor-led AI commission.

02 — Product Analysis

Two extremes: one put a generative model's output into a person with no clinical data, the other put FDA-cleared AI into an affordable stethoscope with no accuracy data

Omnii-1 (Radical Numerics)

Multimodal genome language model for phage design and phage-host matching · Radical Numerics (US)

Function and position. It ranks candidates and recommends phages for a given strain; the pitch is not a molecule but a capability — hand it a resistant isolate, get a usable set of phages back in days. Its demand side today is individual compassionate-use cases, not pharma procurement (company post, 10/20).

  • Strength : the hit rate is not incremental. 69.9% recovery at 11–15 amino-acid changes against 4.8% for random controls; on the hardest strain 24% of the model's top 100 cleared the threshold against 4.5% of the library, cutting the screening work to roughly a fifth. The matching generalises too — two phages covered 91.4% of 37 held-out E. coli strains.
  • Concern : the clinical side is blank. The patient's course and outcome are withheld, the candidate count and model architecture are not given, the company itself says one patient cannot establish safety or efficacy, and there is no peer-reviewed paper (what is missing is not detail but any clinical data a third party could read). Compassionate use is lawful and does save people, but it means this class of product can reach patients repeatedly without a prospective trial, and the question of who is watching and how much has almost no mechanism there.

CORE 300 (Eko Health)

Dual analogue/digital stethoscope with FDA-cleared heart-sound AI · Eko Health (US)

Function and position. One chestpiece twists between analogue and digital, with 35x amplification, active noise cancellation, on-device heart rate and three digital modes, connected to the Eko App's FDA-cleared AI including structural murmur detection. Cleared via 510(k) for paediatric and adult patients, on sale from 10/22, with Canada, the UK and selected European markets next (company release, 10/22). Not for radiology or academic centres, but for everyone who carries a stethoscope.

  • Strength : the channel already exists. More than 750,000 devices delivered or sold worldwide, by its own count, means the AI does not have to build a habit of its own — the shortest adoption path of the week. The price band points it at frontline care: the release says only that it matches premium traditional stethoscopes, while HIStalk reports $199 — if that holds, the only product this week putting FDA-cleared AI in a three-figure dollar band.
  • Concern : the release contains no accuracy, sensitivity or validation data; 35x amplification is its only figure, and that is gain, not performance, so on launch day the official material does not let anyone judge how well the AI performs. The price exists in two inconsistent versions, and "delivered or sold" folds two commercial facts into one number — the TGA's reminder this week applies here too: what a product says about itself does not settle its regulatory standing or how its claims should be read.

This week's competitive structure is a layered squeeze. At the top, capability suppliers (Microsoft, NVIDIA, Radical Numerics) each chose a different release strategy: publish in a journal, open the weights, go straight into a patient. In the middle is the fight for the front door, where Epic's Connectors upgrades the chart into an operating system for agents and vendors like Abridge stand exactly there. At the bottom are governance and security, where Rhapsody turned agent permissions into a product while Storm-0940 is already attacking the same institutions with open-source agents. What is scarce is not models but the layer that can state who is watching and how much they see — and three documents this week (METR, UT Southwestern, the TGA) each point from a different direction at its absence.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan has a counterpart to each of this week's three lines — just not at the same speed

(1) Patients are already using it — Taiwan's week was about literacy, not about whether to open the door. On 10/20 the Legislative Yuan's health caucus launched a National Health Literacy Promotion Committee, convened by legislator Wang Cheng-hsu. Cheng Shao-yi, who directs family medicine at National Taiwan University Hospital, said patients often consult AI before seeing a doctor, as a second opinion; Wu Hung-lai of the Taiwan Patient Alliance warned that patients unable to judge an AI answer may delay care or take the wrong medication. A ministry survey finds 30% of adults aged 20 and over have insufficient or limited health literacy. Huang Chao-hui of the Taiwan FactCheck Center said health misinformation is the largest category and that AI makes fake content better targeted, such as tailored doctor videos; Deputy Minister Chuang Jen-hsiang said the ministry will work with the Ministry of Digital Affairs to have platforms remove AI face-swap fraud and fake doctor videos (CNA, 10/20). Beside this week's MAI-DxO at 83% and Google's AMIE feasibility study in The Lancet, the contrast is clear: elsewhere the measurement is how accurately AI answers; Taiwan spent this week on what happens after the patient has finished asking.

(2) The TGA's sentence applies directly to the TFDA — and Taiwan's entry conditions are heavier than Australia's. Australia's test: a supplier writing "not a medical device" does not determine regulatory status, and none of the 12 products examined classified itself as one (Healthcare IT News, 10/21). Taiwan faces the same problem by another route. The Ministry of Health and Welfare has brought software bills of materials into its Responsible AI Center across 10 hospitals, requiring AI systems entering a hospital to pass independent committee review of security and privacy risk, publish an SBOM and undergo vulnerability scanning. Its Medical Sovereign Cloud Eight Principles, developed with Microsoft, Google Cloud and AWS since early 2026, require data stored in Taiwan, end-to-end encryption, hospital-held keys, no secondary use without authorisation, resilience and auditability, and administrators holding Taiwanese nationality — at perhaps 5–15% more cost for public cloud (MOHW). Australia audits on risk after the fact; Taiwan sets conditions at the hospital door. The observability gap METR's report identified has a counterpart on the supply-chain side through SBOM, and is just as empty on the model-behaviour side. Worth noting that hospital AI guidance has been published, emphasising patients' right to know.

(3) Epic's Connectors catalogue opens in March 2027, and Taiwan's FHIR Box timeline sits almost on the same line. The ministry's FHIR push uses a "FHIR Box" so hospitals standardise exchange without replacing existing systems, aiming to connect key data across all medical centres by end-2026 and all hospitals nationwide by end-2027. Minister Shih Chung-liang is also planning a national integrated clinical trial network, with NT$10 billion in strategic investment plus the NHI sandbox to draw private capital, alongside the five-year NT$48.9 billion Healthy Taiwan programme and a four-year NT$24 billion drug-resilience programme (Economic Daily News / UDN; UDN). The window implies something concrete: when Epic's developer ecosystem opens in March 2027, Taiwan's medical centres will just have finished connecting and regional hospitals will be joining — and if the standards do not line up, Taiwanese vendors maintain two integrations instead of one. On the in-hospital end, Foxconn's digital health arm offered a practitioner's account this week: its Nurabot nursing robot and autonomous mobile robots have been validated at Veterans General Hospital facilities and Tungs' Taichung MetroHarbor Hospital, with a vision-language-action model, plus operating-room robots and the CoDoctor consultation aid. The framing — AI helps think, robots help do, physicians decide, nurses care — comes with no quantified results anywhere in the piece (Commercial Times, 10/22).

05 — Further Reading

Sunday's list picks the four that change how you read the week, not the four that travelled furthest
  1. Omnii-1: generative phage design reaching a patient — Radical Numerics (2026-10-20)

    Read the technical section, then the disclaimer at the end — the distance between them is the most important thing about this week. The method is detailed; the clinical section is one sentence about privacy. The best available exercise in judging how mature a bio-AI company really is.

  2. Red-teaming OpenAI's agent monitoring — METR & Redwood Research (2026-10-22)

    Sixty-one pages, but the section worth your time is on what triggers monitoring: it fires when the agent hands control back, so continuous action defers it. Anyone wiring agents into a clinical system should make that the first question on their procurement questionnaire.

  3. AI-enabled triage tools may reflect human biases — UT Southwestern / npj Digital Medicine (2026-10-20)

    The value is the design, not the finding: pair patients who present alike but diverge afterwards, then change only the narrative in the chart. Any hospital can lift it and run it on its own data against the model it is about to deploy — far more useful than citing someone else's fairness metric.

  4. Conversational diagnostic AI in ambulatory primary care: a prospective feasibility study — The Lancet (2026-10)

    MAI-DxO's 83% only reads correctly against this. AMIE saw roughly a hundred real patients in a BIDMC urgent primary care clinic with an internist watching every conversation live: the final diagnosis fell in its top seven in 90% of cases, top three in 75% and first in 56%, with no significant difference from physicians on differential quality (p = 0.6) but losses on practicality (p = 0.003) and cost-effectiveness (p = 0.004). A single-centre feasibility study without controlled comparison, funded by Alphabet (summary; arXiv preprint).
