Seconds measured to the decimal, clinical benefit left blank

Thursday, November 19, 2026 · Covering Nov 06–19 · Taipei time

Every technical figure handed over this week has the same shape: latency measured in milliseconds, time savings to the decimal place, and the clinical-benefit cell left empty. On 11/17 an arXiv paper tore autoregressive generation out of medical vision-language models altogether — MedJev maps image, question and candidate answers to a decision in one forward pass, and MedJev-4B shipped on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0 at 69.06% average accuracy across four benchmarks, 7.93 points above its backbone and 6.04x faster end to end — with no clinical validation whatsoever. The same day Isomorphic Labs announced a $5 billion Series C, a release carrying no valuation, no programme count and no trial timing. On 11/18 Synaptiq compressed head-and-neck contouring from an average 97 minutes to 2.7, benchmarked against the 80-patient dataset of a 2023 study. The week's one peer-reviewed figure runs the other way: blinded attendings judged just 65.4% of AI chest radiograph reports clinically acceptable, against 79.6% for radiologists' own. And the body that put that on the programme is RSNA itself.

Architecture CUHK · NUS · TsinghuaMedJev-4B11/17

Generation removed entirely: MedJev answers medical imaging questions in one forward pass, open weights 6.04x faster

What

On November 17 a paper credited to Fan Zhang, Hao Wu, Jinyang Wu, Hongyu Qu, Zheng Lian, Dongfu Yin, Qingsong Wen and Pheng-Ann Heng went up on arXiv (arXiv:2611.20992), inverting how medical vision-language models work. Current models generate reasoning and answer tokens autoregressively, so inference cost grows with response length and the answer still has to be parsed out of free text. MedJev takes the image, the question and every candidate answer together and scores each candidate in a single multimodal forward pass, generating no text at all. It strips out the upper language layers and the generative output pathway, keeps the full vision pathway and all visual tokens, then adds a decision head that scores candidates by pretrained query-key matching, with a lightweight MLP residual for task-specific nonlinear discrimination (ArXivSignals' paper page). MedJev-4B is built on Qwen3.5-4B; the Hugging Face model card states Apache 2.0 release, 25,768,961 trained tensor parameters (1,966,593 of them in the decision head), and one epoch over 174,360 examples from the training splits of four benchmarks: HAM10000, PneumoniaMNIST, MedThinkVQA and PMC-VQA. The code is public on GitHub.

Numbers

Per the single evaluation table on the model card: 94.23% on PneumoniaMNIST, 68.39% on HAM10000, 61.68% on PMC-VQA and 51.94% on MedThinkVQA, averaging 69.06%. Mean latency on one H20 at batch size 1 was 64.8, 73.3, 65.0 and 250.7 ms respectively, averaging 113.4 ms across the three single-image benchmarks. The authors report a 7.93-point average accuracy gain over the backbone and a 6.04x end-to-end speedup, and claim state-of-the-art accuracy and inference speed against 20 general and medical VLMs in the 4B–9B range.

Why it matters

This is the week's one technical item where the artefact can be downloaded and the numbers rerun. Apache 2.0 plus 4B scale means it runs on a single consumer or on-premise GPU, with no need to send patient images outside the hospital — and for institutions that cannot ship data to a cloud, that architectural trade-off matters more than a decimal point of accuracy. More notable is its methodological stance: it treats generated reasoning as a removable cost rather than a necessary capability, which cuts directly against the past two years' orthodoxy that a model must write out its chain of thought to be trustworthy. If the approach holds, the cost structure of deploying medical image QA changes wholesale.

Discount this

The model trained on the training splits of all four benchmarks, so these are not zero-shot results. The 69.06% average is pulled up by 94.23% on PneumoniaMNIST, while 51.94% on MedThinkVQA is not far from chance on a four-option task. The latency figures come from one H20 at batch size 1 under lab conditions, not end-to-end response time in a clinical system. Every evaluation is multiple-choice scoring, and real image interpretation does not arrive with candidate answers attached. Most important: there is no clinical validation of any kind — no prospective study, no reader study, no regulatory filing. This is an arXiv preprint, not yet peer reviewed, and both the 20-model comparison and the state-of-the-art claim are the authors' own.

What

On the morning of November 17 London time, DeepMind spinout Isomorphic Labs announced a $5 billion Series C, described as oversubscribed, co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital, with Andreessen Horowitz and several other new investors joining and Alphabet making a significant further investment. Existing backers Thrive Capital, MGX, GV, CapitalG, Temasek and the UK Sovereign AI Fund also took part. Bloomberg reported the same round that day. Founded in 2021, the company is headquartered in London with offices in Cambridge, Massachusetts and Lausanne, led by CEO Demis Hassabis and President Max Jaderberg; SiliconANGLE notes it follows a $2.1 billion Series B.

What the money buys

The release lists four uses: maturing its drug design engine IsoDDE and building foundational biology models; building a partner ecosystem around disease biology; global infrastructure for AI-led bioengineering; and AI-first translational and clinical development infrastructure as candidates advance toward trials. IsoDDE is described as a suite of foundational simulation AI models spanning multiple therapeutic areas and drug modalities, plus proprietary AI agents. Worth noting: the release never mentions AlphaFold 3 — the technical banner this company is most often cited for does not appear in the account of its largest round.

Why it matters

Five billion dollars is a single round for a company with no drug yet in clinical trials, and its previous round was $2.1 billion. The number's significance is not pharmacological but a measure of how large a bet on compute and time investors will take: what they are buying is the proposition that drug design can be made a repeatable optimisation problem, and that it converges within a few years. Greenoaks' Neil Mehta calls new medicine development "an optimization challenge of almost infinite scale" — a claim and a risk disclosure in the same sentence. Set against MedJev the same week, producing a downloadable artefact from 174,360 examples and one H20, the distance between the two is the capital structure of medical AI today.

Discount this

The release gives no valuation, no programme count, no named pharma partners, no trial timing and no headcount. Preclinical work is described as "positive results" in oncology and immunology across internal and partnered programmes, "setting the stage for clinical entry" — with not one figure, no third-party verification and no indication named. Hassabis says the engine is "generating molecules with unprecedented precision and speed"; Jaderberg says "every week we are making repeatable scientific advancements". Both are company statements with no verifiable comparator attached.

Radiotherapy SynaptiqMediq RT11/18

Head and neck from 97 minutes to 2.7: Synaptiq takes a Class IIa CE mark, benchmarked on someone else's 80-patient dataset from 2023

What

On November 18 Romanian health-tech company Synaptiq announced that it had folded tumour delineation and organ-at-risk (OAR) contouring into a single radiotherapy planning workflow in its Mediq RT platform, with clinicians retaining approval of contours. The approach is automatic segmentation followed by interactive refinement, which the company calls Active Intelligence: a clinician outlines one gross tumour volume slice, the AI extends the contour through the rest of the volume, and corrections on one slice guide revisions elsewhere. The platform supports more than 250 OAR and lymph node regions across anatomical cancer sites; initial contour processing takes about 30 seconds per patient and responses to interactive prompts 1 to 2 seconds. Input is 3D CT of adults with a radiotherapy indication, intended users are trained radiation oncologists and medical physicists, and it connects to existing treatment-planning systems and PACS over DICOM, running in the cloud or on hospital premises.

Numbers

The company reports an average 92.6% reduction in CT contouring time versus manual work — including clinician review and correction, not raw AI inference alone — with head and neck falling from an average 97 minutes to 2.7, a 97.2% reduction. The evaluation covered head-and-neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis CT cases; across it, 92.1% of AI contours were accepted without edits, 7.6% needed minor edits and under 0.3% needed major corrections. For comparison, Synaptiq benchmarked itself against the 80-patient dataset of a 2023 Frontiers study, reporting 2.7 minutes of head-and-neck correction time against 4.4 for the next-fastest system. Regulatory status is a Class IIa CE mark under the EU MDR covering tumour contouring; US entry is planned via an FDA 510(k) submission, with no clearance yet. Founded in Cluj-Napoca in 2021, the company says it serves customers in 11 countries and has processed more than 63,000 patients in real-world use.

Why it matters

Contouring is the most labour-intensive stage of radiotherapy planning, and one of the few where saved time converts directly into patient waiting days — when a clinician spends 97 minutes on a head-and-neck case, that is where the scheduling bottleneck sits. The 92.1% accepted-unedited figure means more than a bare time-saving percentage, because it measures what clinicians actually accepted rather than geometric similarity between algorithm and ground truth. It is also the week's only technical item carrying an EU medical device certification and a named comparison against competing systems.

Discount this

Every efficiency and acceptance figure is the company's own, with no third-party audit. The comparison has a structural problem: Synaptiq mapped its results onto the 80-patient dataset of that 2023 Frontiers study, which evaluated five other vendors and did not include Synaptiq — placing itself into someone else's comparison table after the fact, not a contemporaneous blinded test. Most important is the choice of endpoint: these figures measure contouring efficiency and clinical acceptability, and none of them shows reduced total treatment waiting time, better tumour control or a survival benefit. The Class IIa CE mark covers tumour contouring only, and there is no US clearance.

Peer-reviewed University of WashingtonCT-Chat · Merlin · BrainGPT11/06

The week's one peer-reviewed figure runs the other way: 65.4% against 79.6%, AI chest reports still behind radiologists

What

A review published November 6 in Current Problems in Diagnostic Radiology, written by Yee Seng Ng and Elise Dunning of the University of Washington, examines where foundation models stand as drafting assistants for radiology reports; AuntMinnie covered it on November 12. It cites blinded results from an AI challenge at the 2025 American College of Radiology Annual Meeting: attending radiologists judged 65.4% of AI-generated chest radiograph reports clinically acceptable, against 79.6% for reports radiologists wrote themselves. The review names several models with limited clinical validation to date: CT-Chat, Merlin and BrainGPT for CT, and Mammo-FM for mammography; evaluation tools listed include CheXbert, GREEN and CheXprompt. MIMIC-CXR, commonly used on the training side, holds more than 370,000 image-report pairs.

Why it matters

Line this 14.2-point gap up against today's first three items and you have the issue's spine: MedJev measures latency, Synaptiq measures time saved, Isomorphic measures the size of a round — and the one figure measuring whether clinicians would accept the output is 65.4%, on the losing side. The gap also explains how the drafting-assistant story can be simultaneously true and false: 65.4% works as a starting draft for human review, and does not work as a substitute for a radiologist, and those two readings are routinely conflated in vendor decks. The review's own value is that it labels a set of frequently cited foundation models as having limited clinical validation — including ones appearing on this year's conference programmes.

Discount this

This is a review, not original research, with no new trial design. The coverage does not give the number of reports or readers in that blinded test, so the sample behind 65.4% and 79.6% is unknown and whether the difference is statistically meaningful cannot be judged. No accuracy, sensitivity, specificity or agreement statistics are reported, and the challenge's case mix and difficulty are not described. The challenge also ran in 2025, and models have iterated repeatedly since, so the figure may not hold for late-2026 systems — though no more recent equivalent blinded test exists to replace it, which is itself the problem.

Conference RSNA 2026McCormick Place11/11

RSNA itself scheduled the sessions on vendor numbers not guaranteeing performance: Nov 29–Dec 3 in Chicago, three on AI's downside

What

The RSNA 2026 AI programme preview published by AuntMinnie on November 11 (by Erik L. Ridley, last modified November 13) places the meeting at McCormick Place in Chicago, with listed sessions running Sunday November 29 through Thursday December 3. Three sessions take AI's downside head on: on December 1, one argues vendor-reported performance metrics do not guarantee good clinical performance; on November 30, one finds AI does not always reduce radiologists' mental workload and sometimes adds to it, with a companion session on using AI-assisted reporting usage logs to measure cognitive load; and on December 2, one holds that AI shifts diagnostic risk in radiology rather than eliminating it. The same day's programme includes a report that implementing AI at a comprehensive stroke centre did not measurably improve patient outcomes, and a cerebral microbleed study finding AI flagged far more microbleeds than a radiologist but also produced more false alarms.

Worth noting in advance

In the same preview: on November 30, an open-source AI system is reported to have detected pancreatic cancer on routine CT nearly a year before clinical diagnosis, and a separate study finds deep-learning models on routine chest x-rays predict cardiovascular risk about as accurately as the American Heart Association's PREVENT score. On December 3, a group describes rolling out 11 triage AI algorithms across three hospitals. On November 29, a large real-world deployment of thyroid ultrasound AI reports high agreement with radiologists.

Why it matters

A programme is a field's assessment of itself, and it is harder for communications teams to rewrite than any single paper. That the Radiological Society of North America has put vendor metrics not guaranteeing performance, AI adding to cognitive burden, and AI shifting rather than removing risk on its formal programme means these are no longer commentators' opinions but research questions inside the field. Against today's first three items — all vendor- or author-reported — the RSNA programme supplies a timetable: the performance figures read today will, in two weeks in Chicago, be put in front of reader studies and deployment sites by the same community.

Discount this

These are unpublished conference abstracts, not peer-reviewed papers. The preview names no institutions and gives no sample size for any study — apart from the "nearly a year" pancreatic lead time and "11 algorithms across three hospitals", quantitative detail is absent, and the microbleed finding is described only qualitatively. Conference abstracts and final papers often diverge, and some sessions may ultimately be withdrawn or rewritten.

Foundation models AeviusMass General Brigham11/17

Turning a patient into a refreshable mathematical state: Aevius spins out of Mass General Brigham with $22 million

What

On November 17, HIT Consultant reported that Boston clinical intelligence company Aevius launched with $22 million led by Insight Partners, as a spinout from Mass General Brigham. Its approach trains multimodal models on structured EHR fields, unstructured clinical notes and specialty data such as pathology, imaging and genomics, building foundation models that learn longitudinal relationships across a patient's full medical history. The output is a "refreshable multimodal patient representation", which the report describes as a living, computable mathematical state of the patient's condition and trajectory, updating as new encounters, diagnostics and treatments occur. The report contrasts this with text-only LLMs, which reconstruct patient context one question at a time. Applications listed: outcome forecasting, clinical similarity search, longitudinal disease progression tracking, candidate biomarker discovery and automated cohort identification for clinical trials. MobiHealthNews covered it the same day.

Who is behind it

The founding team is CEO Rowland Pettit (MD, PhD), CTO Ming Yang (Max) Lu (PhD), Faisal Mahmood (PhD) and Long Phi Le. The report notes this group previously built Modella AI, which AstraZeneca acquired, and that Eric Topol chairs Aevius's Scientific Advisory Board. Target customers are hospital networks, health plans and biopharmaceutical enterprises.

Why it matters

This is today's clearest architectural fork. For two years the mainstream in medical AI has been to feed records into a language model and answer one question at a time; Aevius proposes the inverse — build a continuously updated patient state representation first, then have many applications read from the same representation. If that works, forecasting, cohort identification and biomarker discovery stop needing a separate model each. It is also what Aevius shares with MedJev: both question whether generating an answer in text is a necessary intermediate step.

Discount this

This is a launch announcement and every technical claim comes from the company. There are no model performance figures, no benchmarks, no published papers cited and no named customers. A "computable patient state" is at present a product narrative rather than a verifiable specification — the report itself notes that it never formally defines "computable medicine". At $22 million this is small by today's standards, 227 times smaller than Isomorphic's $5 billion the same day, and neither has verifiable clinical results yet.

02 — Product Analysis

Two products selling speed — one publishes it so it can be reproduced, the other wraps it in a certification

MedJev-4B

Non-autoregressive medical VLM decision model · academic team (CUHK / NUS / Tsinghua)

Function and position. It does not generate reports. It does one thing: given an image, a question and candidate answers, pick the answer in a single forward pass. Intended users are researchers and in-house engineering teams deploying medical image QA on constrained hardware, not clinical departments. The model card lays out the evaluation table, latency, parameter counts and training volume in full, the code is public, and the Apache 2.0 licence permits commercial use.

  • Strength : reproducibility. Weights, licence, per-benchmark accuracy and per-benchmark latency are all public, so anyone can rerun it on their own hardware and falsify it — the only one of today's seven items that clears that bar.
  • Strength : deployment cost structure. At 4B with a single forward pass it runs on one on-premise GPU and patient images need never leave the institution; the 6.04x end-to-end speedup comes from removing generation rather than quantisation, so it is not trading accuracy for speed.
  • Concern : the task shape does not match the clinic. It requires candidate answers to be supplied with the question, and real interpretation offers no options; 51.94% on MedThinkVQA shows the advantage vanishing as soon as a question needs reasoning. Clinical validation figures are zero — not few, none.
  • Concern : evaluation overlaps training. The training splits of all four benchmarks went into that one epoch over 174,360 examples, so the scores are not zero-shot and generalisation to unseen datasets is unknown; the paper is not yet peer reviewed.

Synaptiq Mediq RT

Radiotherapy contouring platform · Synaptiq (Romania)

Function and position. A planning tool sold to radiation oncology and medical physics teams, putting tumour delineation and contouring of 250-plus organ-at-risk and lymph node regions into one workflow: the clinician draws one slice, the AI extends through the volume, and it connects to existing treatment-planning systems and PACS over DICOM, cloud or on-premise (Unite.AI, 2026-11-18). Tumour contouring carries an EU MDR Class IIa CE mark; US entry is planned via a 510(k).

  • Strength : its endpoint sits closer to the patient than peers'. 92.1% of contours accepted unedited, 7.6% needing minor edits, under 0.3% major corrections — measuring what clinicians actually accepted, not a Dice coefficient against ground truth.
  • Strength : it has certification and deployment volume. The Class IIa CE mark under MDR for tumour contouring means EU notified-body review, and the company says it serves 11 countries and has processed more than 63,000 patients — the only product today with both a regulatory certification and a real-world volume.
  • Concern : the comparison was applied after the fact. The company benchmarks itself against the 80-patient dataset of a 2023 Frontiers study that evaluated five other vendors and not Synaptiq, so "2.7 minutes versus 4.4" is not a contemporaneous blinded test but a self-insertion into someone else's table.
  • Concern : the time saved is not connected to an outcome. The coverage states plainly that these figures do not show reduced total treatment waiting time, better tumour control or a survival benefit — and all efficiency numbers are company-measured, with no third-party audit.

Side by side, the difference is not whose numbers look better but whose numbers can be falsified. MedJev puts weights, licence, per-benchmark accuracy and per-benchmark latency online, so anyone can rerun it on their own hardware and overturn it — at the cost of sitting furthest from the clinic, with zero clinical validation and a task shape that requires candidate answers to be supplied with the question. Synaptiq reached the clinic, with a CE mark, 63,000 patients of real-world volume and a measured clinician acceptance rate — at the cost of a comparison it could only apply retroactively to someone else's 80-patient dataset from 2023, and a 92.6% time saving that connects to no patient outcome at all. Each incompleteness has an honest shape: one is checkable but not yet a medical device, the other is a medical device whose numbers it measured itself. Today no product does both.

04 — Taiwan Angle

4B open weights, one on-premise GPU, and a timetable that ends in 2028

(1) MedJev's scale lands exactly in the bracket Taiwanese hospitals can actually run. What usually blocks medical AI deployment in Taiwan is not model capability but that data cannot leave the hospital: putting images in a cloud means a long IRB and information-security process at most academic centres.
