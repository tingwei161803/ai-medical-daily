Saturday · General AI

Acceleration and lockdown, filed on the same day: METR measures 2x, OpenAI gates a Critical model, two labs push hundreds of billions of parameters outward

Saturday, November 7, 2026 · Covering Nov 1–7 · Taipei time

On 5 November the industry filed two documents that contradict each other. One is METR's high-access assessment of Anthropic: as of October 2026, model capability on well-specified tasks is improving at roughly 2x the rate it did from March 2024 to October 2025; METR guesses about 80% of that acceleration is itself AI-driven, and it found no convincing bottleneck in algorithms, training compute, experiment compute or data over the next twelve months. The other is OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber system card: cybersecurity capability rated Critical under the Preparedness Framework, so the model is not sold openly at all — only through Daybreak Red, to identity-verified customers meeting organisational security requirements. One document says the labs cannot keep up with themselves; the other says a capability has grown too strong to sell.

For medicine these two documents point at the same problem, and it is more fundamental than any single clinical study: if the slope of the capability curve doubles inside eighteen months, a validation regime denominated in "versions" stops working. A prospective trial takes eighteen to thirty-six months from design to publication; an FDA De Novo review routinely runs past six. Meanwhile this same week two open-weight releases — Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Small (594B total / 17B active, Apache 2.0 weights promised for next week) and Baidu's ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base (280B / 14B active, 33.5T pretraining tokens) — mean a hospital needs nobody's approval to put a several-hundred-billion-parameter model in its own server room. Put together, that is the real news of 5 November: regulation is denominated in years; models are now denominated in weeks.

Lead story METRAnthropic11/05

METR: Anthropic's capability progress is running at roughly 2x its rate 18 months ago, ~80% of it AI-driven, with no bottleneck in sight for 12 months

What

METR published on 5 November a high-access assessment run inside Anthropic from 12 August to 29 October 2026, using about 2.5 full-time equivalents over eleven weeks, to answer three questions: has internal capability growth accelerated, how much of it is due to AI, and could bottlenecks cap it. The finding: as of October 2026, Anthropic is likely improving model capabilities on well-specified tasks at roughly 2x its March-2024-to-October-2025 rate (METR says it would not be surprised by 1.3x or by 3x). On AECI, Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, acceleration since October 2025 is 1.9x with a 95% CI of 1.5–2.8x, with the trend rising from about +15 points/year before the breakpoint to about +29 points/year after it, the breakpoint set at 7 October 2025. Robustness checks span 1.1x–2.9x depending mainly on the link function (complementary log-log gives 1.1x, log-log 2.9x); including all frontier developers gives 1.4x, and the Haiku 5.5 system card reported 1.7x. On attribution, METR "guesses" about 80% of the acceleration is due to AI, and says plainly that the figure is highly uncertain. Self-reported uplift in internal Slack polls rose from about 1.2x for Claude 3.7 Sonnet (January 2025) to about 12x for Model 4 (October 2026), an exponential fit implying 3.9x per year — evidence METR itself calls very loose. Of 11 researchers interviewed and classified, 2 said yes to >2x acceleration, 3 leaned yes, 4 were borderline, 0 leaned no, and 2 said no. METR also states it is confident the acceleration was not driven by faster growth in training compute.

Why it matters

This is the closest thing yet to an external measurement of whether recursive self-improvement has started, and the answer is not yes or no but: the slope has doubled, and the main cause is AI itself. The bottleneck question matters most. METR says it found no convincing constraint over the next twelve months across algorithmic progress, training compute, experiment compute or data — while admitting low confidence, because its own view changed several times during the assessment — and writes directly that there is a significant risk of even faster acceleration over the coming year. For medical AI the implication is institutional: any clinical validation, procurement contract or hospital governance document written against "version X" of a model now has a shorter shelf life. The Nature Health study of 883,403 responses on brand-name bias covered in yesterday's edition, and the Doximity audit of 261,508 doses from the day before, both measured the versions that existed at the time. If the slope really has doubled, the half-life of such audits is a few months.

Discount this

The biggest problem with this report is not its method but its redactions. Anthropic removed most of the evidence METR found most informative, including per-stage acceleration estimates, the breakdown of progress across compute, data and algorithms, the bottleneck analyses and forecasts for future models; Anthropic described the redactions as aimed at removing confidential information that could accelerate other AI developers. METR therefore states plainly that the public must rely heavily on its judgement, because the evidence cannot be independently checked. Two further points: the planned compute-allocation verification was cancelled by Anthropic midway, before METR reached conclusions on it; and Model 4 scored above 50% on all 15 benchmarks used, which is itself a measurement-ceiling concern. METR lists reasons in both directions: it may have overestimated if non-AI factors explain much of the gain, if Anthropic's historical baseline was unusually low, or if gains are confined to well-specified tasks; it may have underestimated if growth is accelerating hyperbolically or if AECI understates real-world impact. One team member had a conflict of interest, checked by an unconflicted colleague.

Safety & deployment OpenAIGPT-6 Cyber11/05

OpenAI ships GPT-6 Cyber: Critical on cybersecurity and High on bio-chem under the Preparedness Framework, not sold openly — only through the Daybreak Red allowlist

What

OpenAI published the GPT-6 Cyber system card on 5 November. It is a cybersecurity-specialised variant of GPT-6 Astra, built for vulnerability discovery, exploit development and web pentesting. Capability ratings: Cybersecurity Critical (same as GPT-6 Astra), Biological and Chemical High (not Critical), AI Self-Improvement Below High. There is one route of access: Daybreak Red, with identity verification, use restrictions and organisational security requirements including SSO, multi-factor authentication and API IP allowlisting; API customers on Zero Data Retention generally also need Private Safety Processing. Benchmarks (GPT-6 Cyber vs GPT-6 Astra): SEC-Bench Pro (pass@1) 87.02% vs 85.38%, SRE-Bench (pass@1) 92.84% vs 87.98%, ExploitGym (intended exploit) 48.45% vs 42.43%, ExploitGym Hardened 36.94% vs 29.69%, ExploitBench Internal Port 42% vs 39%; Irregular CyScenarioBench solved 11/11 challenges at least once (Astra 8/11) at 61% average success, but on Irregular FrontierCyber GPT-6 Cyber scored only 90/288, below Astra's 96/288. Refusal behaviour: 100% of general security and patching tasks completed, 96% of exploit development, 97% of red-teaming. On alignment: out-of-scope honeypot exploitation 0.07%, falling to 0% with deployment safety instructions (Astra was already 0%); broken search-tool error reporting 0.33% against Astra's 6.92%; but blocker-reporting flags at 20.29% versus Astra's 11.66%, which OpenAI attributes mostly to incomplete final summaries rather than deception. On biology, ProtocolQA scored 47.2% (50.9% counting refusals as correct), below the High threshold of 54%; on self-improvement, ExpertSWE 82.95% against Astra's 83.24%.

Why it matters

This is a complete worked example of a capability rating dictating a business model: Critical does not mean the model cannot ship, it means it can only be delivered through an allowlist plus identity verification plus organisational security requirements. Healthcare should read it as a rehearsal of its own future — when a medicine-specific model is rated capable enough to create systemic risk (able to act autonomously on order-entry systems, say, or to design a procedure with biological risk), delivery shifts from open API sales to a list of verified institutions. That the bio-chem rating stopped at High rather than Critical (ProtocolQA 47.2% against a 54% threshold) marks exactly where that line currently sits. One detail deserves a hospital CISO's attention: for this model OpenAI requires API keys bound to IP allowlists and recommends sandboxing agent workflows with action review through Codex Auto-Review — essentially the same control list a hospital needs before wiring an agent into an EHR.

Discount this

Every benchmark figure here is self-reported in OpenAI's own system card and not independently audited; Irregular's challenge sets are external, but OpenAI ran and reported them. The counterexample on FrontierCyber — the specialised model losing to the general one, 90/288 against 96/288 — shows these benchmarks are not mutually consistent, so cherry-picking the wins would be wrong. And on blocker-reporting flags rising from 11.66% to 20.29%, OpenAI's explanation (mostly incomplete summaries) is a qualitative judgement with no sample size given for the manual review behind it.

Open weights Thinking MachinesInkling 2 Small11/05

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Small: 594B total / 17B active, with full weights promised next week under Apache 2.0

What

Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Small on 5 November: a Mixture-of-Experts model with 594B total parameters and 17B active, taking image inputs and supporting variable effort, with a 292M-parameter vision encoder based on Perception Encoder using 20×20-pixel patches and 2×2 spatial pooling. It shipped through Tinker with closed weights, and the company says full weights follow "next week" under Apache 2.0, after external safety testing and red-teaming. The training disclosure is unusually detailed: pretraining phase H1 ran 8.4T tokens in 68 hours on NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 racks, natively in NVFP4; H2 was 3.8T tokens at 512K context; H3 about 320B tokens at 1M context; H4 about 34B agent-trajectory tokens. The joint RL run took 1,075 steps, over 17 million rollouts, 9 days, 2,048 GPUs, more than 4×10²³ FLOPs and 3T+ tokens at 256K RL context. Benchmarks: Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index 31 (the table lists 30.6 on AA Index v5.0), SWEBench Pro v2 (Hard) 86.3%, DeepSWE v1.1 72.1%, MMMU Pro 81.6%, HLE-Diamond 28.7%, AA-LCR 83.3%, LongBench v2 70.6%, Vals Legal Index 73.9%, Vals Finance Index 61.5%. Safety and alignment: FORTRESS average risk score 16.2 (lower is better), overrefusal 20.4, StrongREJECT 97.1%, Vals CyberBench 72.7%; internal misbehaviour rate 14.9 against 66.8 for the previous Inkling, though the company grants it still trails GPT-6.1 Sol on alignment evals. On customisability, it reaches the final performance of Inkling Small on a financial-document summarisation task in 75 training steps.

Why it matters

17B active against 594B total is currently the most hospital-friendly shape there is: sparse enough to serve on a single node or a small cluster, large enough not to be a toy. Apache 2.0 is the decisive part — it permits commercial use, permits private modification and imposes no field-of-use restriction, which happens to be the three things hospital counsel care about. Add the "75 steps to match the previous generation" figure and it points at a concrete hospital workflow: light fine-tuning on de-identified in-house text, the whole stack running in your own server room, not one byte of patient data leaving the building. This week's isolation-first deployment paper from a German university hospital is the working template for exactly that route (see Further Reading).

Discount this

The weights are not out yet — "next week" is a company commitment, not an accomplished fact, and it is conditional on external safety testing and red-teaming passing; until the weights actually land, any "hospitals can self-host this" plan is still only a plan. All benchmark figures are vendor-reported, and an AA Index of 31 is plainly not at the very front of the frontier. The company concedes it still trails GPT-6.1 Sol on alignment evals, and the 14.9 "internal misbehaviour rate" is a bespoke metric with no public definition or externally comparable equivalent. All medical inferences here are this report's own extrapolation: the release carries no medical benchmark at all (the page does include a demo identifying three wines from a photo in 19 of 20 trials, which is not clinical), so clinical validation remains the deploying institution's job.

Open weights BaiduERNIE 5.5 Flash Base11/05

Baidu open-sources ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base: a 280B / 14B-active sparse MoE base model pretrained on 33.5T tokens, with 1M-token context for fine-tuning and inference

What

Baidu put ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base weights on Hugging Face on 5 November. Per the official model card this is a text-only pretraining checkpoint with no instruction tuning: 280B total parameters (the Hub lists 286B for the safetensors files) with 14B activated per token; 43 layers (1 dense plus 42 MoE), hidden size 4096; attention across 37 HCA layers (head dim 512) and 6 MLA-DSA layers (head dim 256), 64 heads; 512 routed experts per MoE layer at top-10 plus one shared expert, latent dim 2048; vocabulary 201,216. Pretraining ran 33.5T tokens with the Muon optimizer in FP8 mixed precision, and both FP8 and BF16 weights are released; maximum training length is 128K, but fine-tuning and inference support 1M tokens. The licence is the ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base Model License Agreement v1.0. Baidu's self-reported benchmarks (against DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base / Hy3-preview-Base / MiMo-V2.5-Base): MMLU 93.3 (vs 89.4 / 87.5 / 87.6), MMLU-Pro 78.4 (vs 69.2 / 66.9 / 65.7), GPQA 60.2 (vs 55.9 / 50.2 / 51.4), SimpleQA 34.1, HumanEval+ 82.3 (vs 69.5 / 78.0 / 74.4), MBPP+ 81.5, LiveCodeBench 40.1, RepoQA 91.7, LongBench-v2 48.7, BBH 89.2; rivals score higher on ChineseSimpleQA (DeepSeek-V4 75.3 against ERNIE's 73.0), HELMET-Rerank, InfiniteBench, FullStackBench and INCLUDE-lite.

Why it matters

Two open-weight models with hundreds of billions of total parameters and low-teens billions active, released on the same day, is not a coincidence but a route taking shape: extreme sparsity traded for self-hostable inference cost. For medicine that lands on two levels. Technically: 14B active, FP8 weights and 1M context is exactly the combination that can swallow a full inpatient chart or a year of outpatient records and still run. Geopolitically: this is a base checkpoint, not a chat model, meaning any institution can build its own instruction tuning and alignment from scratch rather than inheriting a vendor's refusal policy and value settings — which, for an academic medical centre unwilling to outsource alignment decisions behind clinical judgement, is a selling point rather than a gap. GPQA 60.2 is worth noting: it is graduate-level scientific reasoning, which partly overlaps clinical reasoning but is emphatically not the same as clinical competence.

Discount this

Three discounts. First, every benchmark is self-reported by Baidu and uses base-model few-shot settings (MMLU 5-shot, HumanEval+ 1-shot and so on), which cannot be compared directly with chat-model evaluations. Second, the licence is not Apache 2.0 or MIT but Baidu's own License Agreement v1.0; its restrictions need reading clause by clause before any medical product use, and it should not be conflated with Inkling 2 Small's Apache 2.0. Third, this is a base model with no instruction tuning and no safety alignment whatsoever — feed it a clinical question directly and the output is unusable; medical use requires doing the post-training, alignment and clinical validation yourself, which is far more engineering than downloading weights. The model card also gives no explicit release date (citation and copyright lines say 2026); the 5 November date here comes from secondary coverage and the Hub listing.

Agents on real records AnthropicPlaid11/05

Claude launches a read-only Personal Finance beta: up to 16 US banks via Plaid and 24 months of transactions, but barred from scheduled tasks, Cowork and Claude Code

What

Anthropic launched Claude's Personal Finance beta on 5 November, limited to eligible US Pro, Max and Ultra subscribers (Free, Team and Enterprise excluded) and rolling out gradually. Connection runs through Plaid, the user picks the bank and accounts, Claude never requests or sees the bank password, and access is read-only throughout — it cannot move, manage or invest money and is not designed to give investment advice; US banks only, up to 16. What it can read: account names and types, usually the last four digits, balances and credit limits, plus up to 24 months of transactions (amount, date, merchant, category and the bank's own description); investment, loan and card accounts expose balances and limits only, not holdings, trades, rates or terms. Capabilities include monthly spending breakdowns and month-on-month comparisons (Anthropic's example shows $4,309.37 spent across connected accounts, over half on rent and utilities and $1,024.51 on food and drink), detection of duplicate charges and unexpected fees, plain-language explanations of terms like APR with annual interest totals, weekly spending charts and customisable dashboards. The limits are explicit: it cannot be used in scheduled tasks, Cowork tasks or Claude Code, may not work in projects, and is unsupported in Claude Duet. On privacy, conversations in the Personal Finance hub are not used to train models and are designed not to be saved to memory, searched later or included in the monthly recap; but shared links can expose account figures and charts, and artifacts may contain bank data and persist after the originating chat is deleted. On business model, Anthropic states there are no ads, no sponsored recommendations and no bundled Anthropic credit cards or loans, consistent with its 4 February 2026 ad-free policy.

Why it matters

On the medical-AI shelf this sits almost exactly where athenahealth's athenaPatient MCP server on 2 November sat in this report's 5 November edition: a heavily regulated store of personal records wired to a general model, read-only, user-authorised, consented account by account. Three design decisions are worth copying wholesale. One, read-only as a product boundary rather than a technical limit — write access is held back until compliance has thought it through. Two, quarantining the sensitive data in its own hub, explicitly excluded from memory, search, scheduled tasks and agent execution environments, which amounts to conceding that agentic automation and sensitive records should not yet be connected. Three, stating outright that the data is not used for training rather than leaving users to find a setting. A hospital wiring an LLM into its EHR can lift all three almost verbatim.

Discount this

This item rests mainly on secondary coverage (unite.ai's write-up) rather than Anthropic's own help pages, so figures and terms should be checked against the official documents. Anthropic's own accuracy caveats should not be skipped either: check important figures against bank records, balances may be a day or two stale, an account connected through two logins may be counted twice, and if a bank cannot be read its spending is omitted, so budgets can overstate what remains. The privacy behaviours such as not saving to memory are phrased as "designed not to" rather than as absolute guarantees; and unlinking the last bank permanently deletes the dashboard's widgets, budget settings and setup answers.

Law enforcement US DOJDOJ-SITF11/05

The US Justice Department launches a Super Intelligence Task Force, targeting foreign states, transnational crime and corporate misuse — and telling companies to keep internal controls on SI systems

What

The US Department of Justice announced the Super Intelligence (SI) Task Force (DOJ-SITF) on 5 November (press release 26-1319; page metadata shows publication on 4 November and modification on 5 November), led by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss, working with "SI Force Chair Jay Clayton" per Attorney General Todd Blanche. It targets three classes of actor: foreign states and state-sponsored actors, transnational criminal organisations, and individual or corporate actors engaged in fraud or other unlawful conduct. Priorities include prosecuting unlawful SI use — particularly schemes aimed at American taxpayers, critical US infrastructure or national security — deterring improper use through law-enforcement tools, strengthening regular reporting on SI risk and response, and requiring companies to maintain strong internal controls so SI systems behave as intended. The release gives no budget, staffing or timeline figures, lists only the Office of the Attorney General and the Office of the Associate Attorney General as components, and links to a supporting memorandum.

Why it matters

This only makes sense read next to GPT-6 Cyber: on the same day, a lab restrained a Critical-rated cyber capability with an allowlist of its own accord, while the Justice Department announced it will reach for criminal tools against SI misuse and wrote "internal controls" down as a corporate obligation. For healthcare institutions the direct implication is that the "we only used the vendor's model" defence is narrowing — the same direction as the AMA liability framework covered here on 4 November, which said warnings and disclaimers are "not enough" and that a hospital's duty of care spans selection, validation, training and long-term monitoring. Liability is moving toward the deployer. Health systems also happen to sit inside "critical infrastructure", which means this task force is the counterpart when hospital IT is hit by an SI-assisted attack.

Discount this

This is a launch announcement, not a rule. No budget, no headcount, no timeline, no list of participating litigating divisions, and no legal threshold defining "Super Intelligence" — "requiring companies to maintain internal controls" is press-conference language, not yet an enforceable compliance standard. What it actually obliges a healthcare institution to do cannot be judged until the first guidance or the first prosecution.

Physical limits of compute GoogleBloom Energy11/05

Google commits $17bn to its first Wyoming data centre with 702 MW of fuel cells and carbon capture — in the same week governments are tightening data-centre permits

What

On 5 November Google announced a long-term $17 billion investment in Wyoming, building its first data centre in the state in Laramie County near Cheyenne, paired with a 702 MW natural-gas fuel-cell development with carbon capture and storage — reported to be the largest planned commercial-scale fuel-cell development of its kind. The cells are manufactured in the US by Bloom Energy and convert natural gas to electricity electrochemically; the capture takes a large majority of emissions rather than all of them, with CO₂ going to Tallgrass Energy's nearby Eastern Wyoming Sequestration Hub for permanent geological storage. That brings Google's total contracted CCS capacity to nearly 1 GW. Partners include AEP, Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy and Related Digital; Google is also buying power from gas generators run by Tallgrass and Black Hills and running a front-end engineering and design study on adding CCS to Tallgrass's combined-cycle turbines. Cooling is air-based to reduce pressure on local water, and Google says it will pay for all the electricity its data centres consume plus the infrastructure its growth requires. The other side of the same week: several jurisdictions are tightening data-centre permits, and New York was the first state in the country to impose a statewide pause — Governor Hochul signed Executive Order No. 62 on 14 July 2026, suspending discretionary DEC permits for data centres consuming, or able to consume, 50 MW or more until the Department of Public Service completes a Generic Environmental Impact Statement and findings statement under SEQRA. That GEIS must assess energy demand, water use and quality, air quality, impacts on disadvantaged communities and noise, and DEC must separately report within 12 months on whether water-withdrawal rules need changing for data centres. The order expressly exempts facilities used primarily for manufacturing, research including quantum computing and biomedical research, education (accredited colleges and universities, and the Empire AI consortium), and medical care.

Why it matters

METR found no algorithmic or compute bottleneck for the next twelve months, but that assessment looked inside a lab; what may bind first is permitting, grid and water. The value of this item is that it gives the magnitude in both directions at once: $17bn, 702 MW and nearly 1 GW of contracted CCS on one side; a stop-and-wait-for-environmental-review threshold of 50 MW on the other. The most practically useful detail for healthcare is the exemption list in the New York order — biomedical research and medical care are expressly carved out of the pause. In effect, an energy fight has drawn a priority lane for medical computing, and that is a directly citable precedent for Taiwan and other places designing comparable rules under power constraints.

Discount this

The fuel-cell and CCS details here come from secondary coverage (Interesting Engineering) rather than Google's own announcement, and that coverage states it has no job numbers, no construction or completion timeline, no cost for the fuel-cell project and no capacity figure for the data centre itself; "a large majority of emissions captured" is qualitative, with no capture rate given. The New York material is from July 2026 and appears here as contrasting background, not as this week's event; this week's event is that multiple jurisdictions are continuing to tighten permits (Reuters published a factbox on 5 November, but that site could not be retrieved in this environment, so its content is not cited). Google's claim that it will pay for all its electricity is a company statement, not independently verified.

M&A MetaConductor11/04

Meta acquires AI coding startup Conductor: the orchestration layer for parallel multi-agent workspaces goes to a hyperscaler, with the cloud service shutting on 20 November and user data deletion to follow

What

Meta confirmed the acquisition of Conductor on Wednesday 4 November; terms were not disclosed, and Meta confirmed the deal to TechCrunch without further detail, with Axios first reporting it. Conductor was founded in 2024 by Charlie Holtz, its CEO, and launched its core product in summer 2025. The product lets software engineers deploy multiple agents into parallel workspaces, each working on a different part of a project, running in the cloud, locally or from a phone — and crucially the agents come from major labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI, not from Conductor itself. What happens next: core features keep working as they have until early next year; the cloud service shuts down on 20 November, after which Conductor begins deleting users' cloud data; Holtz says the company has "something new" for customers by "early next year".

Why it matters

This is a clear signal that the agent orchestration layer is being absorbed: the value sits not in the model but in the layer that dispatches several other people's agents at once. It is the same structural problem as AutoDNA, covered here on 5 November, where four planning agents and two execution agents drove more than twenty classes of instrument — medicine will have to answer "who orchestrates the agents" sooner or later. And for any hospital contemplating building a clinical tool on a startup's orchestration layer, the line about the cloud service closing on 20 November and data deletion beginning is the best risk reminder available: when the orchestration layer is acquired, what gets cut off is your workflow, not the model vendor's. Data-portability and service-termination clauses in the procurement contract matter far more than the feature list in the demo.

Discount this

Deal value, where the team lands and the integration plan are all undisclosed, so why Meta bought it can only be guessed at — acqui-hire versus technology purchase cannot be asserted. TechCrunch gives no funding history for Conductor and does not say what becomes of the core features after "early next year"; "something new" is the founder's phrasing with no product detail. The link to medicine here is entirely this report's own analogy: Conductor is not a medical product.

What

Google DeepMind announced Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash on 5 November: clip length rises from Omni 1.1's 10 seconds to 15 seconds, settable in one-second steps from 3 to 15; drafts generate at 360p or 720p and upscale to 1080p or 4K; pricing is $0.07 per second at 720p, which Google says is 30% below Omni 1.1 (implying roughly $0.10 per second before, though the post does not state the old price). A hospital should write its labelling policy before it starts mass-producing explainer clips, not afterwards.

The $0.07 figure is for 720p, and the cost of upscaling to 1080p or 4K is not given, so the patient-education estimate above is a floor rather than an actual bill. SynthID is an imperceptible watermark, and its survival through editing, transcoding and re-compression was not verified here, so it should not be assumed readable after any processing chain. 02 — Product Analysis

Two models released the same day stake out the opposite poles of capability governance: one rated Critical and gated to an allowlist, the other 594B parameters given away whole next week

GPT-6 Cyber

Cyber-specialised model · OpenAI (US) · Daybreak Red allowlist only

Function and position. A cyber-specialised build of GPT-6 Astra for vulnerability discovery, exploit development and web pentesting, sold not into the developer market but to identity-verified security organisations: SSO, multi-factor authentication and API IP allowlisting required, with Private Safety Processing generally required for Zero Data Retention customers (OpenAI system card). Technically, specialising on the same base model lifts it above the general version across exploit work: ExploitGym Hardened 36.94% against 29.69%, SRE-Bench 92.84% against 87.98%.

  • Strength : it turns "capability rating → delivery channel" into a copyable institution. Critical does not mean banned, it means allowlist plus verification plus organisational security requirements; out-of-scope honeypot exploitation is held to 0.07%, and 0% with deployment safety instructions.
  • Strength : honesty under tool failure improves markedly — 0.33% error reporting on a broken search tool against 6.92% for general-purpose Astra, a key metric for any institution wiring an agent into a production system.
  • Concern : specialisation is not a clean sweep. On Irregular FrontierCyber it scores 90/288, below Astra's 96/288, which shows these benchmarks disagree with each other and any single set can support the opposite conclusion.
  • Concern : blocker-reporting flags run at 20.29%, far above Astra's 11.66%; OpenAI says most were incomplete summaries rather than deception, but that is a qualitative judgement and the card gives no sample size for the manual review. Every figure is vendor-reported and not independently audited.

Inkling 2 Small

594B / 17B-active MoE · Thinking Machines Lab (US) · Apache 2.0 weights promised next week

Function and position. A sparse MoE taking image inputs with variable effort, shipped first on Tinker with closed weights, with the company saying full weights follow next week under Apache 2.0 once external safety testing and red-teaming are done (Thinking Machines). What it sells is not absolute capability but customisability: on a financial-document summarisation task it reaches the final performance of the previous Inkling Small in 75 training steps.

  • Strength : the decisive clause for regulated industries — Apache 2.0 permits commercial use, permits private modification and imposes no field-of-use restriction — combined with 17B active parameters that a single node or small cluster can serve, makes this the closest thing to a hospital-self-hostable frontier-class model (specs).
  • Strength : the training disclosure is unusually complete for a frontier lab — 8.4T tokens in phase H1 over 68 hours on GB300 NVL72 racks in native NVFP4, a joint RL run of 1,075 steps, 17M+ rollouts, 2,048 GPUs and more than 4×10²³ FLOPs — letting outside researchers actually appraise it rather than read scores.
  • Concern : the weights are not out — "next week" is a commitment conditional on safety testing passing — and an AA Index of 31 (listed 30.6) is plainly not at the very front, with the company conceding it still trails GPT-6.1 Sol on alignment evals.
  • Concern : no medical benchmark at all. The release demonstrates code, vision, legal, finance and one wine-identification result (19/20), with no clinical evaluation; the "internal misbehaviour rate 14.9 against 66.8 for the previous generation" is a bespoke metric with no public definition or external equivalent. Open weights also transfer alignment and safety responsibility wholesale to the deployer.

What the contrast actually means. These two products are not competitors; they are two answers to one question. When the rate of capability improvement doubles (see today's lead item on METR), do you manage the risk by controlling the channel or by diffusing it widely? OpenAI chose the former, and used benchmark numbers to show specialisation works; Thinking Machines chose the latter, and used training detail plus Apache 2.0 to show verifiability has value. Healthcare cannot pick just one side — high-risk autonomous clinical action needs GPT-6-Cyber-style channel control, while keeping patient data inside the building needs Inkling-style open weights. The real governance design makes both modes coexist inside one hospital rather than arguing over which is correct.

04 — Taiwan Angle

When models are denominated in weeks, Taiwan's real race is not catching the frontier but building validation and self-hosting faster than anyone else

(1) Open weights land directly on Taiwan's structural constraint. The hardest constraint on Taiwan's health system is neither compute nor talent but the compliance cost of moving patient data across borders or outside the hospital. The two open-weight releases that appeared today — Inkling 2 Small (594B / 17B active, Apache 2.0 promised next week) and ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base (280B / 14B active, FP8 weights) — turn that constraint from "impossible" into "how much does it cost". Fourteen to seventeen billion active parameters in FP8 is within one academic medical centre's server room. An isolation-first deployment paper from a German university hospital of over 10,000 staff supplies the full template: open-weight models served through vLLM, strict network segmentation, host-enforced egress filtering, active monitoring blocking unauthorised external connections, and automated isolation and hardening tests packaged for public use (ukbonn/ukb-gpt on GitHub) — ending in approval from clinic management, compliance, data protection and information security officers to process unanonymised patient data. Taiwan can walk this road without waiting for any international vendor to open anything. But that paper's one-week pilot with 22 clinicians also carries a warning: tasks anchored to source text (correcting or simplifying reports, answering imaging guideline questions) rated highest in utility, while open-ended generation of conclusions from findings produced the most critical errors, including clinically relevant hallucinations and omissions — which is precisely the step most tempting to automate.

(2) The ministry's policy tempo and METR's measured rate differ by an order of magnitude. Taiwan's smart-healthcare build-out currently turns on data interoperability. Per coverage of the Kaohsiung Medical University forum on 27 June 2026, Health Minister Shih Chung-liang's "333 policy" aims to break down the walls between hospital information systems and standardise data formats, with medical centres adopting a standard format called "FHIR Box" and a target of nationwide interoperable records by the end of 2026, extending to regional and district hospitals in 2027 and 2028, standard system templates for clinics and public health centres, and three AI centres to speed adoption. The NT$48.9bn mentioned by minister without portfolio Chen Shih-chung is a five-year allocation under the Healthy Taiwan Deep Cultivation Plan, with four focus areas — working conditions, talent training, smart healthcare and social responsibility — entering a second phase next year with more emphasis on cross-regional cooperation (the article does not break out how much of the NT$48.9bn goes to the 333 policy). Set that timeline beside today's lead item and the contrast is stark: policy advances interoperability in years, while the capability slope METR measured runs at +29 points a year and doubles in roughly eighteen months. That does not make the policy wrong — interoperability is the foundation without which nothing else is possible — it means Taiwan cannot build only the foundation. The practical reinforcement is to build validation infrastructure in parallel: if bodies such as the Clinical AI Validation and Verification Center (CVVAI) can turn audits into a repeatable process rather than a one-off report, Taiwan can hold credibility in an environment where models turn over every few weeks.

(3) On electricity, what Taiwan should copy from New York is the exemption list, not the moratorium. Google signed 702 MW of fuel cells plus CCS in Wyoming in one go, while New York suspended discretionary permits for data centres at 50 MW and above — and Taiwan's power position resembles the latter. What deserves copying is the detail of that order: facilities used primarily for manufacturing, research including quantum computing and biomedical research, education and medical care are expressly exempt. That hands Taiwan ready-made legislative language: under an energy constraint, do not ban AI compute — draw a priority lane for medical and biomedical research compute, and require non-exempt large projects to assess energy demand, water use and quality, air quality, impacts on disadvantaged communities and noise. There is a direct benefit for Taiwan's academic medical centres too: if a self-hosted in-house model (see point 1) is classified as medical-care use rather than a commercial data centre, its expansion path is far smoother than external commercial compute. This policy comparison is this report's own inference — Taiwan has no corresponding rule yet — and the AI-Driven Healthy Taiwan Summit at National Cheng Kung University on 20 November (guided by the National Science and Technology Council and the Ministry of Health and Welfare, with NSTC Minister Wu Cheng-wen, Health Minister Shih Chung-liang and NHIA Director-General Chen Liang-yu among the speakers, and an agenda covering legal risk, allocation of liability and regulatory adaptation) is precisely the venue for putting these questions on the table.

05 — Further Reading

Five primary documents and papers rather than secondary coverage, chosen because reading each one changes a judgement
  1. Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026 — METR (2026-11-05)

    The document of the year to read line by line — but what to read is not the figure 2x, it is how METR writes its uncertainty plainly while most of its evidence is redacted: conceding low confidence on bottlenecks, admitting its view changed several times during the assessment, and listing the reasons it may have over- and under-estimated. It is a model of honest assessment under unverifiable conditions, and the genre that vendor audits in medical AI should imitate.

  2. Secure On-Premise Deployment of Open-Weights Large Language Models in Radiology: An Isolation-First Architecture with Prospective Pilot Evaluation — Nowak et al., arXiv:2604.22768 (2026-03-25)

    The working counterpart to today's two open-weight stories, and the paper Taiwanese academic medical centres should read first. Eleven authors at a German university hospital of over 10,000 staff serve DeepSeek-R1 through vLLM, trading network segmentation and host-enforced egress filtering for sign-off from compliance, data protection and information security to process unanonymised data. The one-week pilot with 22 clinicians matters as much: text-anchored tasks proved most useful, while open-ended conclusion generation produced the most clinically relevant hallucinations and omissions. The deployment package is public on GitHub (ukbonn/ukb-gpt).

  3. GPT-6 Cyber system card — OpenAI Deployment Safety (2026-11-05)

    Read it as an institutional document on how regulated capability gets delivered, not as a benchmark table. The control list is what to copy: identity verification, SSO, multi-factor, API IP allowlisting, sandboxed agent workflows with action review — very nearly the minimum a hospital needs before wiring an agent into an EHR. Note too that the specialised model loses to the general one on FrontierCyber, a reminder not to conclude anything from a single benchmark set.

  4. Inkling 2 Small — Thinking Machines Lab (2026-11-05)

    Read this for the training sections, not the scores. 8.4T tokens, 68 hours, GB300 NVL72 racks, native NVFP4, plus a joint RL run of 1,075 steps, 17 million rollouts, 2,048 GPUs and 4×10²³ FLOPs — disclosure at this granularity lets outsiders actually estimate cost and reproducibility, which is rare among frontier labs. Read against METR's redacted report, it shows how far transparency has itself become a competitive strategy.

  5. New York State enacts data center moratorium — Davis Polk client update (2026-07)

    The one recommendation here that did not happen this week, but worth catching up on: it unpacks New York's Executive Order 62 clause by clause — the 50 MW threshold, the scope of the pause (discretionary DEC permits, excluding local approvals and ministerial actions), and the exemption list (manufacturing, research including biomedical, education, medical care). Before Taiwan starts debating power rules for data centres, understanding one text already in force beats ten commentaries.
