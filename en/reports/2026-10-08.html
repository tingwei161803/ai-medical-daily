01 — Top Stories

Eight items, one shared seam: orchestration runs ahead, proof of validity stays behind
Agent orchestration arXivRadOnc-Agent10/07

One LLM chains 26 radiotherapy functions, then the authors write down "does not establish clinical correctness"

What

Posted to the arXiv cs.AI new-submissions listing on October 7, RadOnc-Agent (arXiv:2610.06923) uses one LLM as orchestrator to put 26 callable functions spanning the entire radiotherapy care pathway behind a single conversational interface. Radiotherapy is one of medicine's best test beds for orchestration: it is already a long pipeline with a fixed order — indication, target and organ-at-risk delineation, dose prescription, plan optimisation, QA, follow-up — each stage already has its own AI tool, and the missing piece has always been the layer that joins them. The paper's own evaluation statement is unambiguous: the authors write that the results "do not establish clinical correctness, clinical utility or prospective benefit" (arXiv cs.AI new submissions, 2026-10-07).

Why it matters

That disclaimer is not modesty; it is the field's actual state. When radiotherapy orchestration goes wrong, what is wrong is not a sentence of advice but the dose distribution delivered into a patient's body — and the evaluation tooling that could show the chain was joined correctly is precisely what three other papers this week say does not yet exist. Read RadOnc-Agent next to those three and the same gap is being pointed at from both sides: engineering can already chain 26 functions, while the measurement end has not even settled how to score whether the chain is right.

Discount this

At the time of writing both the arXiv abs page and the full-text HTML were refused by the fetch proxy with HTTP 429. This item rests only on the Oct 7 new-submissions listing — title, arXiv ID, function count and the authors' disclaimer above. The full text was not read: which LLM orchestrates, how the 26 functions break down, the evaluation design and any quantitative result are all unverified here, and so none are quoted.

Interpretability arXiv11 個開源權重模型10/02

Evaluation moves from what the model says to where it lights up: dedicated clinical concept centers found in 11 open-weight models

What

Submitted on October 2, Clinical Concept Centers in LLMs (arXiv:2610.02829) — by Aishik Nagar, Abhishek Vaidyanathan, Arun-Kumar Kaliya-Perumal, Elijah Tzen Hsuen Boey and Stefan Winkler — reports dedicated clinical concept centers in the latent space of all eleven open-weight models tested. Four properties carry the weight: the centers fire only on their aligned clinical narratives; they influence model behaviour causally, in both constrained and open-ended settings; under adversarial role-based priming the models stay internally coherent and keep using the relevant centers; and steering along the centers yields downstream gains in simulated deployment settings. There is also a blinded clinician validation: how strongly these centers activate and are used predicts clinician preference.

Why it matters

This paper aims straight at the gap running through today's edition. The authors state plainly that a model's stated reasoning can omit the features actually driving its answer — meaning that scoring the output text has a structural ceiling. If clinical concepts really are locatable, causally verifiable internal representations, then "is this model reliable in this setting" could move from reading outputs to reading internals, with steering available as a lever on top. Set against the same week's rubric review, which ranks faithfulness the weakest of its six dimensions, the two papers are biting the same problem from opposite ends.

Discount this

Only the arXiv abstract and metadata were read here; the full text was not. The abstract is qualitative throughout ("meaningful" improvements, activations that "predict" preference) and gives no figures at all: which eleven models, what effect sizes, how many clinicians in the blinded study and with what agreement statistics are none of them stated at abstract level. This is a single preprint, not peer-reviewed.

Evaluation method arXiv六個維度10/01

Split clinical reasoning into six dimensions and nothing covers them all — faithfulness has one study, in multiple choice

What

Submitted October 1, A rubric landscape for evaluating clinical reasoning in large language models (arXiv:2610.01938), by Zhangshu Joshua Jiang, Zina Ibrahim and James T. Teo, sets three literatures that normally never meet alongside each other: medical-education assessment instruments, clinical LLM benchmarks from 2023 onward, and general-domain methods for evaluating long-form generation. It defines clinical reasoning as updating evidence across time and sources to form and justify a patient's problem representation and plan, then examines six dimensions — problem representation, temporal synthesis, differential and management reasoning, counterfactual reasoning, calibrated uncertainty and reasoning faithfulness. The finding: no single instrument covers all six. Problem representation and differential/management reasoning are reasonably covered, though reliability varies by instrument and setting; TIMER-Eval targets temporal synthesis and ER-Reason assesses sequential diagnostic belief updating; dedicated uncertainty and counterfactual evaluations are emerging but barely applied to longitudinal free-text reasoning; and faithfulness is weakest — one identified clinical causal-ablation study, which used multiple-choice questions.

Why it matters

This review explains why RadOnc-Agent's disclaimer is honest rather than evasive: showing an orchestration chain is clinically right requires all six dimensions to hold at once, and the existing tools cannot be assembled to cover them. The authors' remedies are themselves technical proposals, and nearly every one argues against today's fashion for a single aggregate score — binary rubric items, separate completeness and correctness scores, case-specific importance weighting with non-compensable safety caps (break the cap and no amount of high scoring rescues it), temporal order-consistency checks, and chance-corrected reliability reporting. Put another way: medical AI's scorecard has to move from averaging to veto.

Discount this

This is a structured narrative review, not a systematic one, and neither the abstract nor the page states how many instruments or papers were examined, so no coverage figure can be given here. Preprints are included and flagged. The authors themselves note that the work offers a design rationale rather than a validated instrument — it names the gap without closing it. Only the abstract and metadata were read.

Long-horizon reasoning arXivMedLoCoMo09/30

Feeding in the whole chart is not the best move: Episodic and Hybrid win, and answering well does not transfer to abstaining

What

Submitted September 30 and listed on Oct 1, Can LLMs Reason Over Long Horizons? (arXiv:2610.00562), by Taye Akinrele, Noorbakhsh Amiri Golilarz, Subash Neupane, Sudip Mittal and Shahram Rahimi, uses the MedLoCoMo dataset and four open-weight models to compare five ways of handing a patient history to a model: Full, Recent, Episodic, Semantic and Hybrid. It measures three things — answer correctness, robustness as the distance between a query and its supporting evidence grows, and the ability to abstain on questions whose premises the history does not support. The results: Episodic and Hybrid give the strongest overall accuracy; Recent Context degrades worst as evidence gets more distant, while Episodic and Hybrid hold the highest accuracy at long range; and the line most worth copying down — strong performance on answerable questions does not reliably carry over to abstaining on adversarial ones.

Why it matters

The authors state the conclusion in a form you can act on architecturally: reliable longitudinal reasoning depends less on how much history a model can access than on how relevant evidence is selected and presented. That is a direct refusal of the last two years' instinct that a longer context window settles the matter — the investment belongs in the retrieval and organisation layer. The abstention finding matters more still: clinically, the dangerous failure is not getting one question wrong but answering a false-premise question confidently, and this experiment shows the strategies that win on accuracy do not acquire that capability for free. It has to be measured, and trained, separately.

Discount this

At abstract level no figures are given — how far apart the five strategies' accuracies are, how steep the degradation curve is, how low the abstention rates fall are all directional only, and the four open-weight models are not named. Results come from a single dataset, MedLoCoMo, with no cross-dataset or cross-institution external validation. Only the abstract and metadata were read; this is a preprint, not peer-reviewed.

Taiwan infrastructure 衛福部FHIR Box · 3D PACS10/05

Taiwan's week was plumbing, not models: FHIR Box, a next-generation 3D PACS, and NTUH aiming for hospital-wide automated AI 3D imaging by 2028

What

On October 5 Taiwan's health ministry held the 2026 Domestic AI, 3D Medical Imaging and Metaverse Application Conference, and both headline items sit in the infrastructure layer: FHIR Box, which converts hospital data into the international HL7 FHIR standard without replacing existing core systems, so different hospital systems can exchange data; and a next-generation 3D PACS for storing, transmitting and sharing 3D images in clinical settings. NTUH president Yu Chung-jen said 3D imaging helps surgical planning and doctor-patient communication, and the hospital plans to complete a hospital-wide framework converting medical images into AI-generated 3D images by 2028. Domestic vendor Chih-Chieh Medical Technology's Anatomy Cloud platform automatically converts conventional 2D images into 3D anatomical models, with modelling in as little as about 5 minutes by the company's account; Chih-Chieh donated 10 workstations, which the ministry will place at medical centres and public hospitals as demonstration equipment. Minister Shih Chung-liang said medical metaverse applications already cover teaching, preoperative explanation, surgical planning and intraoperative navigation, but deployment requires thin-slice CT, dedicated software and high-performance computing, plus collaboration between physicians and biomedical engineers.

Why it matters

Put this beside the Sept 30 long-horizon experiment and the technical significance of Taiwan's step comes into focus. If the deciding factor is how relevant evidence is selected and presented, then selectability presupposes data that is standardised and reachable across institutions — which is exactly what FHIR Box does, by the path of least resistance, leaving core systems in place. The 3D PACS is the same logic: without a storage-and-transmission standard of its own, an AI-generated 3D model stays on one workstation and never enters the care pathway. This layer rarely makes international headlines, and it decides whether any of the models above can actually run.

Discount this

Anatomy Cloud's "as little as about 5 minutes" is a vendor claim with no third-party measurement seen. NTUH's 2028 hospital-wide rollout is a plan, not a completed deployment. A related report's headline mentions the system reaching 29 medical centres by 2028, but the body does not confirm that figure, so it is not used here. The sharpest gap is reimbursement: Taichung Veterans General Hospital honorary superintendent Chen Shih-an said broader clinical use needs service and payment mechanisms, and today some hospitals are applying for self-pay billing codes for 3D imaging, with any link to national health insurance still prospective. The demonstration kit has arrived; the way to pay for it has not been laid.

Native 3D model NV-Reason-CTCT-RATE09/25

A 3D CT language model that refuses to merge spatial tokens: 0.871 macro-AUROC on CT-RATE, and not one baseline in the paper

What

NV-Reason-CT (arXiv:2609.27511, submitted Sep 23, v2 Sep 25), by Andriy Myronenko, Dong Yang, Yucheng Tang, Baris Turkbey, Zongwei Zhou, Wenxuan Li, Daguang Xu and others, is a generative vision-language model for chest and abdominal CT. Architecturally the notable choice is that it passes all visual tokens, with their 3D coordinates, straight into language decoding with no spatial token merging — most 3D medical VLMs pool voxels first to contain compute; this one declines to. Training used roughly 550,000 multimodal instruction examples drawn from 70,111 unique CT image inputs, sourced from standardised reports, abnormality- and anatomy-focused questions, multi-turn interactions, and radiologist reasoning captured by recording and transcribing expert interpretation; the recipe is supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO with verifiable rewards. Results: 0.614 macro-F1 and 0.871 macro-AUROC on CT-RATE, with no task-specific classification head; report-derived macro-F1 of 0.592. The authors state they release the model and training code.

Why it matters

The refusal to merge tokens is itself the argument: in 3D medical imaging lesions are often small and position-critical, and what pooling discards tends to be exactly the voxels the diagnosis turns on. Feeding 3D coordinates into the language side is a bet that anatomical localisation is worth the compute. The other design worth noting is the training data's spoken radiologist reasoning — not rationales reverse-engineered from reports, but recordings of physicians thinking as they read. That maps intuitively onto the faithfulness dimension the rubric review found most lacking: if you want a model's reasoning to be real, the supervision signal had better be real too.

Discount this

In the content obtained here there is no comparison against any other model — 0.871 sits with no baseline beside it, so leading or trailing cannot be judged. Parameter count is not disclosed. A held-out NIH cohort was used for evaluation but no results are given. The figure most in need of discounting is the "50% reduction in average interpretation and reporting time": that comes from a preliminary expert study by the authors' own account, with no sample size stated, and the paper labels it preliminary itself. The release statement carries neither a repository link nor licence terms.

Duke turns human digital twins into a heart-failure pilot, and Welldoc bets on small language models rather than large ones

What

Per Fierce Healthcare's October 2 weekly rundown, Duke University launched a "human digital twin" programme combining medical imaging, AI and wearable data, led by the Duke Center for Computational and Digital Health Innovation (CCDHI) and running on PRISM, a platform integrating EHRs, imaging and behavioural signals; the first pilot targets heart failure, with the long-term goal of detecting physiological decline before symptoms or hospitalisation. Why it matters

Welldoc's choice is the week's most underrated technical signal. Duke's digital twin is a bet in the opposite direction: build the patient as a simulable object, and ask not what is in this image but where this person is heading next.

Discount this

This whole item is announcements, not results. Duke's heart-failure pilot publishes no scale, enrolment or prospective endpoint; detecting decline before symptoms is a stated goal, not a demonstrated performance. Welldoc's "clinically validated," its 500 million data points and 80-plus health domains are all company claims, with no corresponding peer-reviewed study or third-party audit seen here, and the "small language models" come with no parameter count or architecture. Yardstick (background) Microsoft ResearchHealthAgentBench06/30

Measure this week's orchestration on a 54-task ruler: 42% at best, imaging is the bottleneck, and the harness alone moves the score 7 points

What

Microsoft Research's HealthAgentBench (arXiv:2606.31179, 2026-06-30), by Qianchu Liu, Sheng Zhang, Hoifung Poon and colleagues, puts 54 tasks in 7 categories, each a terminal-based Docker environment built on the Harbor framework: an agent receives raw clinical data and minimal instructions, then has to explore, plan and produce a verifiable output, scored as binary success or failure against expert labels or human performance. The categories are X-ray report correction (10), CT abnormality classification (10), pathology tumour area selection (10), clinical trial matching (9), EHR data quality auditing (8), EHR event modelling (6) and EHR format conversion (1). Ten frontier agents, three harnesses, xhigh reasoning effort, web browsing disabled, three attempts per task. The scores: Codex GPT-5.5 at 42% (best), Copilot CLI Opus-4.8 36%, Copilot CLI GPT-5.5 35%, Claude Code Opus-4.8 32%, Codex GPT-5.4-mini 16% (weakest). Two category results stand out: no agent clears 50% on EHR data quality auditing (best was Claude Code Opus-4.6 at 42%), while nine of ten agents score 100% on EHR format conversion. Imaging is the main bottleneck — Codex agents averaged 22% on imaging tasks against 12% for Claude Code agents, while text tasks were near parity (50% vs 48%).

Why it matters

This four-month-old benchmark is the only ruler available today for systems like RadOnc-Agent, and its reading is unkind: the strongest frontier agents get just over four in ten realistic healthcare tasks right. Two details are worth holding onto. First, the harness is itself a variable — the same GPT-5.5 scores 42% under Codex and only 35% under Copilot CLI, meaning part of any reported score is not the model's. Second, cost and performance do not track: Claude Code agents were the most expensive and produced roughly twice as many output tokens per task as the best Codex run. The most diagnostic finding is that disclosing where the errors were located substantially improved recall on EHR data quality auditing — which corroborates the Sept 30 conclusion: the bottleneck is search and composition, not knowledge.

Discount this

This is a June 30 paper, outside this edition's seven-day window; it is cited here as a yardstick and as background, not as this week's news. The authors' own limitations are worth copying across: the benchmark is not exhaustive across healthcare agent use cases; the terminal interface was chosen for evaluation, not deployment; each category holds only about 5–15 sampled tasks, with three attempts each; and Copilot CLI's multi-agent design means its results do not reflect a single pure model. Overall scores for five further agents (Opus-4.7, Opus-4.6, Sonnet-4.6, GPT-5.4, GPT-5.3) were not explicitly given in the content obtained here.

02 — Product Analysis

Two routes into radiation oncology and radiology: one trades orchestration for breadth, one trades a single model for depth — and neither has a comparator

RadOnc-Agent

LLM-orchestrated full-pathway radiotherapy framework · academic preprint (arXiv, institutions undisclosed)

Function and position. One LLM acts as switchboard, collecting 26 callable functions spanning the whole radiotherapy care pathway into a single conversational interface so clinicians can drive, in natural language, steps that previously lived in separate tools (arXiv:2610.06923, 2026-10-07). What it sells is not better accuracy at any one step but accessibility across the whole pipeline — which is where radiotherapy's real cost sits: physicians and medical physicists hand-carrying information between six or seven systems.

  • Strength : the setting is well chosen. Radiotherapy is already a long pipeline with a fixed order and an AI tool at every stage, and the join has always been what was missing; orchestration here is far easier to define success for than orchestration in open-ended diagnosis. HealthAgentBench's category breakdown supports that read — on a structured, verifiable task (EHR format conversion) nine of ten agents scored 100%, while on an open-ended one (data quality auditing) every agent fell below 50%.
  • Concern : the authors put the validity gap in the paper themselves — the results "do not establish clinical correctness, clinical utility or prospective benefit." That sentence weighs more in radiotherapy than in most settings: an orchestration error ends as a dose distribution, not a suggestion. And the full text could not be read here (arXiv was refused by the fetch proxy with a 429), so which LLM orchestrates, how the 26 functions break down and what the evaluation design looks like are all missing — not details, but the entire basis for judging whether it is usable.

NV-Reason-CT

Native 3D chest/abdominal CT vision-language model · authors include NVIDIA researchers (US)

Function and position. A single model doing abnormality classification, report generation and interactive reasoning (including differentials and uncertainty), taking the depth rather than breadth route: no tool chaining, just 3D volumes consumed natively. The technical pitch is that all visual tokens, with their 3D coordinates, go straight into language decoding with no spatial token merging, plus training data in which the 550,000 instruction examples include transcribed spoken radiologist interpretation, finished with SFT followed by GRPO with verifiable rewards (arXiv:2609.27511v2, 2026-09-25).

  • Strength : 0.871 macro-AUROC and 0.614 macro-F1 on CT-RATE with no task-specific classification head — a generative model reaching that without a bolted-on head suggests the language side genuinely ingests the image information rather than borrowing marks from a classifier. The report-derived macro-F1 of 0.592 sits close to the 0.614 direct figure, implying the reports it writes broadly match its internal judgement. The authors state the model and training code are released.
  • Concern : there is no comparator model anywhere in the paper — nothing sits beside 0.871, so leading or merely matching cannot be told; parameter count is undisclosed; a held-out NIH cohort was evaluated but no results are reported, leaving the external-validity column blank. The "50% reduction in interpretation and reporting time" is a preliminary author claim with no sample size. The release statement carries no repository link or licence.

The real difference between the two routes is not architecture but who carries the cost of validation. The orchestration route pushes that burden onto the whole chain: each added function adds a layer of combination to be proven, and the Oct 1 rubric review says no instrument currently measures that compositional validity. The single-model route gathers validation behind one interface, where numbers are easy to report — and were reported — but what it lacks is a comparator: 0.871 with no baseline, the exact inverse of HealthAgentBench's 42% arrived at by racing ten agents against each other. The methodological lesson of the week comes from HealthAgentBench instead: only because it ran three harnesses at once did the fact that one model loses 7 points on a change of wrapper become visible at all.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan advanced both plumbing and policy this week, and left the evaluation box empty

(1) FHIR Box is not only interoperability; it is the precondition for evidence being selectable at all. The Sept 30 long-horizon experiment states it plainly: reliable longitudinal clinical reasoning depends on how relevant evidence is selected and presented, not on how much a model can read (arXiv:2610.00562). Both of Taiwan's moves this week sit on that layer: FHIR Box (converting hospital data to HL7 FHIR without replacing core systems) and the next-generation 3D PACS, from the Oct 5 conference. Earlier, Health Minister Shih Chung-liang gave the timetable in a Sept 15 interview: key data from all national medical centres connected by the end of 2026, extended to hospitals nationwide by the end of 2027, with FHIR paired with federated learning for trial recruitment — finding eligible patients and sites quickly while protecting privacy, converting electronic records directly and cutting manual collection. In technical terms: what Taiwan is building is not a model but the retrievable, cross-institution evidence layer that Episodic and Hybrid strategies depend on.

(2) The Oct 7 biotech forum covered money, law and data — not evaluation. At the 2026 Biotech Forum on Oct 7, Minister Shih ran through the policy ledger: the twin regenerative-medicine laws effective in January, the National Health Insurance Data Management Act effective in August (opening the NHI database to academic and industry use under legal conditions), "Trust 100" targeting 100-day review for qualifying domestic new drugs and key medicines, NHI spending of NT$988.3bn this year and possibly NT$1.0434tn next, a four-year NT$24bn drug resilience plan, a five-year NT$48.9bn Healthy Taiwan plan, a ten-year NT$10bn biotech development fund, and an HTA centre awaiting promulgation as an administrative legal entity. Note that not one item on that list concerns AI validity assessment. Data access, funding and faster review are all moving; who verifies whether these models work on Taiwanese patients has neither an institution nor a budget line.

(3) The reimbursement gap and the evaluation gap are the same gap. Taichung Veterans General Hospital honorary superintendent Chen Shih-an put it most practically on Oct 5: broader clinical use needs service and payment mechanisms, and today some hospitals are applying for self-pay billing codes for 3D imaging, with any NHI link still prospective (Economic Daily News, Oct 5). The two are tied together: for NHI to reimburse, somebody has to be able to state the size of the benefit — and the common conclusion of this edition's three papers is that the measuring apparatus needed to state it has not been assembled anywhere, let alone here: no single instrument covers all six dimensions, and the strongest agents manage 42% on 54 realistic tasks. Which makes Taiwan's position an opportunity: the data layer (FHIR Box) is due to be laid by the end of 2027, while the evaluation box is empty worldwide. Whoever stands up localised validity assessment will hold something nobody else has — and it is precisely what the federated-learning architecture mentioned on Sept 15 is best suited to: validating a model's performance on real patients across hospitals without centralising the data.

  1. A rubric landscape for evaluating clinical reasoning in large language models — arXiv:2610.01938 (2026-10-01)

    If you read one thing from this week, read this. Its six dimensions work as a checklist the next time you assess a clinical LLM, and the non-compensable safety cap belongs in any internal evaluation policy.

  2. HealthAgentBench: A Unified Benchmark Suite of Realistic Agentic Healthcare Environments — Microsoft Research, arXiv:2606.31179 (2026-06-30)

    Read the category table, not the headline. The spread across its seven categories — 100% on format conversion against under 50% on data quality auditing — tells you more about which jobs agents can take today than any aggregate percentage.

  3. Benchmarking large language model-based agent systems for clinical decision tasks — npj Digital Medicine (2026-02-18)

    The most citable and least cited paper of the year: Kather's group set agent systems beside zero-shot baselines, found the gains "modest" and mostly not significant, and priced them at over 10x the tokens (about 93,000 per MedQA scenario against 14,600). Remember the other number too — after in-agent safeguards blocked 89.9% of hallucinations, hallucinations still touched roughly 30% of clinical scenarios.

  4. A benchmark study of vision and pathology foundation models for computational pathology — Nature Communications (2026-07-24)

    Read it for the counterintuitive finding: Gevaert's group ran 32 foundation models over 41 tasks, 53 datasets and more than 17,500 whole-slide images, and found no statistically significant AUROC difference between pathology-specific and general vision models, with model size and pretraining scale not consistently predicting performance. The next time someone pitches "ours is a medical-specific model," this is your comparator.

  5. OpenEvidence & TEMPO: No One is Reviewing AI — GridHealth (2026-09-22)

    A short piece that translates this edition's technical theme into commercial and regulatory language. Its core line — a claim is not evidence because the vendor is confident — belongs on the wall of any hospital AI procurement room. It also notes in passing that the FDA's TEMPO pilot lets generative-AI devices reach patients before formal marketing authorisation, with plans to scale to 40 participants across four clinical areas.
