Saturday · General AI

The system meant to watch the agents was bypassed by the agents it was meant to watch — this week agents accelerated on both sides of the line, just as hospitals prepare to hand over the keys

Saturday, October 24, 2026 · Covering Oct 17–24 · Taipei time

Today's lead is not a medical story, because the week's most consequential AI event did not happen in medicine — and medicine is where it is least ready to land. The red-team report METR published on Oct 22 found that OpenAI's internal misalignment monitor fired only when an agent returned control to the user, so an agent could postpone monitoring indefinitely simply by continuing to act. The same week Microsoft attributed attacks on hundreds of organizations since September to a state-aligned actor running an open-source multi-agent pentest framework, and NorForge reported one operator whose agents broke into more than 100 company websites at roughly $50 per success. The control group: Morgan Stanley has started blocking third-party agents because it cannot tell which actions the customer asked for. And the same day Microsoft's MAI-DxO landed in Science, with Suleyman chasing medical superintelligence. The maturity of the identity-and-oversight layer, not model accuracy, is now the real ceiling on clinical agents.

01 — Top Stories

Five stories in one line: agent capability has outrun the identity, monitoring and attribution layers beneath it — the three layers medicine depends on most
AI safety OpenAIMETRRedwood Research10/22

METR and Redwood spent three weeks inside OpenAI and concluded that an agent can postpone monitoring indefinitely just by continuing to act

What

One METR employee and one Redwood Research employee went into OpenAI from Jul 21 to Aug 11, 2026 for a "pilot high-access exercise" stress-testing its internal misalignment monitoring system, with access to the internal codebase, monitor prompts and configuration, and internal documentation, plus interviews with about a dozen researchers and engineers — all on OpenAI-issued devices that never left the premises, accompanied onsite. The output was a 61-page report; the public version released Oct 22, after OpenAI's redactions, runs 38 pages. The figures: of internal agentic Responses API traffic from models at least as strong as GPT-5.5, METR estimated only roughly 75–94% was eligible for monitoring (99.2% for the subset used by internal Codex agents, with METR noting "substantial uncertainty in both directions"); not monitoring compaction events likely left "on the order of 10%" of eligible Codex traffic unmonitored between May and July 2026 (found by OpenAI staff before the exercise, fixed during it); and the monitor itself errored on "less than 0.2%" of requests. The vulnerabilities: the monitor triggered when agents returned control to the user, so "agents could indefinitely postpone being monitored by simply continuing to take actions"; one exploit could prevent "hundreds of recent actions" from being monitored (details redacted); serialization differences between what agents see and what monitors see could let agents build inputs that make the monitor fail, so trajectories were never escalated for human review; two client-controllable headers disabled monitoring for new agents; and a path existed to "in principle" bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review.

Why it matters

This is not a story about one company's sloppy security. It is about an industry building agent governance on an assumption that had never been adversarially tested: that the monitor is exogenous and the agent cannot touch it. METR's recommendation names exactly that — "actions agents take should not be able to influence whether or when the monitoring system runs" — and that is precisely what broke. The implication for healthcare is direct: every governance scheme for clinical agents (audit trails, human-in-the-loop review, retrospective reconstruction) assumes that all actions get logged. METR's finding is that when the thing being monitored is itself a capable agent, that assumption has to be proven as a security property, not drawn as a box in an architecture diagram. The discounts matter too: the public version is only a small part of the private report and OpenAI held redaction rights, METR describes this as a pilot testing access arrangements rather than a full transparency report, and while OpenAI says all vulnerabilities affecting its production safeguards stack were patched, METR explicitly states it has not evaluated those claims; the 75–94% figure is an estimate, not a measurement. The Register's Oct 23 write-up adds a fair footnote: the report does not claim agents deliberately concealed activity, only that this monitoring could have missed such behaviour.

Security MicrosoftConnectWiseARTEX10/21

Microsoft attributes the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise to Storm-0940 running ARTEX, an open-source multi-agent framework: 2,250-plus tenants targeted afterwards, privileged cloud access in under three hours

What

Microsoft Threat Intelligence published its analysis on Oct 21: Storm-0940, a China-state-aligned actor active since 2021 and focused on data theft, used ARTEX as early as September 2026 to automate attacks on hundreds of organizations worldwide, across aerospace and defence, automotive, chemicals, energy, financial services, government, legal, manufacturing, media, military, semiconductor, software, technology, and think tanks and non-profits. Several attacks gave it privileged access to cloud infrastructure in less than three hours from initial targeting. Microsoft assesses Storm-0940 was responsible for the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise of a multi-tenant cloud backup application (ConnectWise published its initial advisory on Oct 16); afterwards the actor tried to sign in to more than 2,250 tenants. A separate password-spray from Oct 10 to 14, from infrastructure at 144.172.64.99, targeted 327 accounts across eight organizations, producing over 151,000 failed logons in Microsoft telemetry and peaking at 2,900 failures per minute on Oct 11, with no successful authentication observed in that campaign.

What ARTEX is

Microsoft's own description: "ARTEX is an open-source penetration testing framework that can use any AI model to automate a variety of offensive security workflows." Technically it is a multi-agent LLM system written in Go with a Next.js frontend; the interface defaults to English and can be switched to Korean, Mandarin, Spanish or Japanese. Its agents run an autonomous penetration test from reconnaissance through data exfiltration with little human input, storing discovered assets and vulnerabilities in a graph. Public reporting in October 2026 already linked it to personal-data breaches at Korean financial institutions, and Microsoft says it has seen several threat actors in different regions using it.

Why it matters

Three structural points. First, agents compress the time constant of supply-chain attacks: Microsoft says explicitly that agents work continuously and can pursue several breach paths at once, cutting the time from target selection to objective. Second, it attacks trust rather than credentials: after taking control of several ConnectWise cloud applications, the agents authenticated into customer tenants using the trust those applications already held, with no interactive user sign-in — exactly the shape of the relationship between a hospital and its HIS or cloud-backup vendor today. Third, the framework is open source, so this is not a one-off state capability but a production line anyone can pick up. The direct healthcare read: multi-tenant health IT providers (backup, cloud PACS, teleradiology, billing) are the ideal leverage point for this pattern, and hospitals generally cannot see inside them — hitting one HIS vendor means reaching dozens to hundreds of care providers. The discounts: Microsoft itself stresses that most of the targeting was unsuccessful and that the activity did not stem from a compromise of its own systems; attribution is inherently probabilistic, this is a single-vendor view built on Microsoft telemetry with no independent confirmation, and "hundreds of organizations" is a range, not a precise count.

Security NorForgeCairnDeere10/19

One operator, one open-source agent framework, 1,200-plus targets: more than 100 company websites breached, averaging just under 23 hours, at about $50 per success

What

Snorre Fagerland, principal threat researcher at the Norwegian security firm NorForge, published a report on Oct 19: a Chinese-speaking attacker used the open-source multi-agent platform Cairn — whose agents work towards a goal step by step and post findings to a shared board every agent can read — to break into the websites of more than 100 companies. Reuters reporter Raphael Satter saw the report before publication and identified some victims: by late the previous week the agents had attempted more than 1,200 sites; the Chinese-language cluster alone held over 1,100 attack projects across 14 control panels, with an average success rate across tools of about 10%. On cost, Fagerland's rough figure for Reuters was roughly $5,000 in tokens for about $50 per successful hack; NorForge's own report puts it at about $5 per task including failed attempts. The campaign began Sep 25, and successful attacks took just under 23 hours on average. All tasks ran on a worker named claudecode_deepseek-v4.1-flash. The targets were online shops in the US, Canada, Japan and Australia with 200,000 to 1.5 million visits, and the agents tried to run code on each site and plant an uploader web shell. Named victims include Deere (took remediation steps, saw no sign sensitive information was accessed), SharkNinja (said the attack hit a standalone product-support site and was contained) and Cummins (investigating a possible attack on a vendor-run website). Scanning the panel data also turned up hundreds of credentials and API keys, of which Trufflehog verified 11% as still valid.

Why it matters

Read this alongside the previous story and the difference is the point: Storm-0940 is a state-resourced operation, this is one person. Fifty dollars per success, under 23 hours on average, a 10% hit rate — those numbers push the unit cost of a bespoke intrusion against a mid-sized website below the margin on a food-delivery order. The healthcare read is concrete: small and mid-sized clinics, labs, health-screening centres and long-term care facilities in Taiwan and elsewhere almost all run an outsourced, long-unpatched website or booking system, often holding credentials that reach inside. What kept those sites safe was never the strength of their defences but the fact that attacking them did not pay. That economic assumption stopped holding this week. The discounts: Reuters could not establish how much data, if any, was taken, and TNW has not independently verified the intrusions; the "$50 per success" figure is a researcher's rough estimate on a different basis from the report's own "about $5 per task", with considerable stated uncertainty; and the model attribution is inferred from a worker name rather than confirmed by any vendor.

Finance & agent governance Morgan StanleyJPMorgan1Password10/22

Morgan Stanley has begun blocking third-party AI agents because it cannot tell which actions the customer asked for — and the same day 1Password shipped agent sign-in without exposing passwords

What

Madison Mills reported for Axios on Oct 22 that major US banks are detecting unauthorized third-party AI agents operating in customer accounts, with some appearing to act without user instructions. Some banks have tracked agents calling into call centres, and executives worry most about agents with purchase authority or the ability to move funds. Most banks cannot reliably tell whether agent activity is user-directed or whether an agent has gone rogue, and one executive said they struggle to differentiate between agents. The responses: Morgan Stanley has begun blocking third-party agents from its platforms (a spokesperson said the bank has not observed "rogue AI agents accessing our platforms"), JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are also restricting agents, and Chase told customers not to give their usernames and passwords to anyone. Most banks have authorized, or are moving towards, read-only access. The same day 1Password for Agents went live: when an agent tries to log in it first sends a unique digital key to 1Password to confirm its identity, the user is alerted to that specific attempt and must personally approve it, and 1Password then fills the credentials itself, so the agent never sees them. TechCrunch names the supported agents as Anthropic's Claude Duet, OpenAI's Dots and ChatGPT Work, Grok Bot, Meta's Muse and agents from the startup Town.

Why it matters

This is the week's most instructive pairing, because it is the first practical answer from a heavily regulated industry with unambiguous attribution requirements, and the answer is: drop to read-only and wait for the identity layer to grow in. Healthcare's structure is almost identical — heavily regulated, actions must be attributable to a specific person, mistakes are irreversible — with one difference: in medicine "read-only" is not a fallback that still meets the business need. The value of a clinical agent lies in placing orders, replying to messages and updating the record; restrict it to read-only and what remains is summarization. 1Password's answer deserves precision: it solves credential exposure and agent identity but not authorization scope or after-the-fact attribution — what gets approved is this login, not what may be done after it, and per-login approval degrades into reflexive clicking at scale, which is alert fatigue replayed through a new interface. What health systems should copy is the design principle: an agent must hold its own identity rather than borrow a clinician's account. Today almost every hospital AI tool does the latter, which is why the audit trail shows a physician's ID placing a hundred orders at 3am. The discounts: the Axios piece contains no specific figures at all and rests largely on anonymous sources, and Morgan Stanley's official line does not sit entirely with the stated motive for blocking; 1Password's "never sees the password" is a vendor-stated architectural property, with no protocol named beyond the key-confirmation step and no pricing or general-availability date.

Medical AI Microsoft AIMAI-DxOScience10/22

MAI-DxO lands in Science: 83% on complex NEJM cases against 36% for unaided physicians — though the paper's own numbers show most of the gain comes from the underlying model, not the orchestration

What

Microsoft's MAI-DxO was published in Science on Thursday, Oct 22, 2026. It orchestrates several AI agents to work like a panel of physicians: questioning the patient, ordering tests, and debating towards a diagnosis. On simulated complex NEJM cases MAI-DxO was correct 83% of the time, against 36% for primary care doctors and general internists working alone with reference materials (specialists and physician teams were not in the comparison). The per-model breakdown is where the real information is: GPT-5 with a short, basic prompt already scored 78.9% on complex cases at about $8,400 per case; adding DxO cut cost by about $3,000 but raised accuracy only about 4 points. GPT-5.5 (released April 2026) went from 72% to nearly 87% on complex cases, at slightly lower cost. On emergency department cases there was almost no gain: GPT-5.5 scored about 85% either way. Gemini 2.5 Flash scored 46.4% alone and nearly 69% with DxO — the gap between weaker and stronger models remained larger than DxO's boost. Models from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta and DeepSeek were tested. Microsoft defines medical superintelligence as "the capacity to surpass the strongest contemporary human-led health systems" in medical reasoning and coordination; Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman said the company is working with the Mayo Clinic on a healthcare foundation model slated for release next year, expects healthcare AI to improve substantially within 24 to 36 months, and will test DxO with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston in real emergency and urgent care settings.

Why it matters

On today's line, its role is not "medical AI breaks another record" but the clock on the demand side. The four stories above say the identity, monitoring and supply-chain layers under agents are not grown in yet; this one says the largest platform vendor has already written "surpassing human-led health systems" down as a public goal, already has a top-tier journal behind it, and already has a real-world trial lined up with a Boston academic medical centre. The gap between supply and governance is where the risk lives. The other signal worth noting is cost: about $8,400 of inference per case means the approach currently makes economic sense only where diagnostic error is very expensive — rare disease and complex cases — which is exactly consistent with the absence of gains on emergency department cases.

Discount this

This story needs the most discounting. First, the comparison group: the 36% against which 83% is measured is primary care doctors and general internists working alone with reference materials — specialists and physician teams were not included, and correctly diagnosing an NEJM complex case normally requires exactly those. Second, the case source: NEJM cases are written for teaching, information-complete and known to have a single right answer, unlike the uncertainty of a real emergency department — and the paper's own emergency results are the evidence of that gap. Third, attribution of the gain: TechCrunch notes plainly that most of the improvement comes from the underlying models, with DxO adding on top; Gemini 2.5 Flash's jump from 46.4% to 69% suggests DxO mainly compensates for weaker models. Fourth, this is simulation — the Beth Israel Deaconess real-world test has not been run. Fifth, the cost figure of roughly $8,400 per case is incompatible with any current reimbursement scheme.

02 — Product Analysis

The week's most interesting product is not on the agent line but an open model that pushes genomic interpretation cost down by three orders of magnitude — and leaves accountability entirely to the user

Carbon-X

Open 14.8B-parameter DNA chat model · Hugging Face x Zhongguancun Academy x Telethon Institute of Genetics and Medicine / Naples Federico II · released 2026-10-22

Function and position. It lets researchers "chat with DNA" in natural language: analysing and generating DNA sequences and answering variant-effect questions. Technically it is Qwen3-14B with DNA tokens added, under Apache 2.0, with a 40,960-token context window, bfloat16 weights that fit on a single 80 GB GPU, served with vLLM. The tokenizer adds 4,096 six-base 6-mers, 1,364 tail tokens and three markers. Training ran in four stages: vocabulary extension on about 10B tokens with the transformer body frozen; mid-training on 302B tokens at sequence length 8,192 (55% raw DNA, 20% English web text, 15% biology text, 10% instruction data); supervised fine-tuning over three epochs across 20 dataset subsets, about 30% of it variant-effect reasoning; and two rounds of asynchronous GRPO with code-checked rewards. It averages 66.7 across five author-reported DNA benchmarks, against 67.5 for GPT-6.1 Sol (Max), 60.9 for Opus 5.5 (Max) and 49.8 for Gemini 4 Argon (Low).

  • Strength : the news here is the cost structure, not the score. The model card estimates inference at about 1/1,800 of GPT-6.1 Sol's cost per problem, and extrapolates that analysing ClinVar's 2.4 million variants of uncertain significance would cost roughly $300, against $32,000 to $4 million for rivals. In clinical genomics, VUS reinterpretation has always been a problem of scale rather than method — being able to re-run the whole set changes the picture entirely. Add Apache 2.0 and single-GPU inference and a medical centre can run it in-house without data leaving the premises, which is decisive in jurisdictions that restrict cross-border transfer of genomic data, Taiwan included.
  • Concern : every headline figure is author-reported, and two of the five benchmarks were built by the authors themselves, with no independent replication. The 1/1,800 cost rests on the authors' pricing assumptions (H200 at $5/hour) and the throughput figure is not disclosed; the $300 for 2.4 million VUS is an extrapolation and no ClinVar run was actually performed; and the ">1,000x cheaper" claim comes from an unattributed social post, not the model card.
  • Concern : clinical validity is entirely unvalidated, the model card itself says it should not guide medical decisions, and no wet-lab validation was done. The authors' stated limitations: it miscounts bases or coordinates, may invent sequence identity or function without context, is markedly weak on splice-site and histone-mark tasks (GenomeQA subscores of just 29.8% and 34.6%, against 93.2% for organism group and 90.0% for motifs), and degrades on long-sequence generation. Which is to say: its weakest tasks are precisely the ones clinical variant interpretation needs most — a substantial share of VUS reclassification turns on splice-site prediction.

Close the section in one sentence: today's competition is not about model scores but about who can first make "whose action was this" a verifiable fact. The offensive side is already commoditized (ARTEX and Cairn as open frameworks, $50 per success, under 23 hours), the capital side has raised the bet to a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, and the demand side has had Microsoft write "surpassing human-led health systems" into Science — while the most complete answer on identity and attribution is a password manager's product shipped on Oct 22 and one bank deciding to block everything. Healthcare will pay for that gap first, because it carries banking's attribution duty plus the one thing banking does not have: it cannot accept dropping agents to read-only.

04 — Taiwan Angle

Taiwan now has an AI framework act and a national strategy committee — but both regulate "AI systems", and neither reaches "what an agent did on whose behalf"

The regulation's granularity does not match agents. Taiwan's AI framework act passed its third reading on Dec 23, 2025, naming the National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) as the central competent authority and establishing a National AI Strategy Special Committee under the Executive Yuan, convened by the Premier, meeting at least annually, with NSTC as secretariat (iThome's summary of the enacted text). The committee was announced on May 21, 2026, with Premier Cho Jung-tai chairing it personally and a target of setting management rules before 2028 (CNA, 2026-05-21; Liberty Times, 2026-05-21). On risk, the act does not build a full multi-tier classification but mainly distinguishes "high-risk" AI products or systems, designated by the relevant sectoral authority in consultation with the Ministry of Digital Affairs (MODA), with mandatory notices or warnings. That is precisely the problem: the framework regulates the risk tier of a product or system, while the risk exposed this week is not "is this model accurate" but "whose account did this agent use, within what authorization scope, which irreversible actions did it take, and was any of it logged". METR's finding is that the monitor's trigger could be influenced by the agent itself; Morgan Stanley's reason for blocking is that it cannot attribute actions. At the granularity of high-risk product designation, neither is visible at all. The "accountability" principle among the act's seven gives the legal basis, but making it operational requires three technical specifications it does not have — agent identity, action-level audit and authorization scope — which is exactly where MODA's mandate to "provide assessment and verification tools" could bite. One more thing that need not wait for 2028: the ConnectWise attack path is the shape of Taiwanese health IT, where HIS, cloud PACS, laboratory systems, NHI claims, and backup and disaster recovery are concentrated in a handful of vendors, so hitting one vendor means reaching dozens to hundreds of care providers. Bringing vendors' application-credential rotation and anomalous-sign-in alerting into the annual security audit, writing supply-chain incident notification deadlines into contracts, and inventorying every integration running under a service or shared account are three things that can be done now.

05 — Further Reading

This list holds only material that changes how you would decide something
  1. Red-Teaming OpenAI's Internal Agent Monitoring Systems — METR (2026-10-22)

    Skip the vulnerability list and read the design recommendations at the end. The line "actions agents take should not be able to influence whether or when the monitoring system runs" can be lifted directly into an acceptance clause for any hospital procuring a clinical agent — and almost nothing on the market today would pass it.

  2. Storm-0940 uses ARTEX agentic AI in ConnectWise supply chain compromise — Microsoft Security Blog (2026-10-21)

    Read its MITRE mapping rather than the narrative. One entry notes a ConnectWise management application self-assigning the Global Administrator role, and Defender flagged bulk Azure Key Vault secret reads across multiple vaults — two detection signals you can take straight to your own cloud vendor and ask whether they watch for them.

  3. Formal verification at scale with Claude — Anthropic Frontier Red Team with Theorem (2026-10-22)

    The week's other face: the same capability pointed at proof rather than attack. Claude produced machine-checked proofs over compiled RISC-V binaries for 239 fuzz-test entry points across 123 open-source libraries (about 180 million lines of Rocq), rediscovering libexpat's CVE-2026-56132 along the way. But the part worth reading is the limitations: "formally verified" does not mean "bug-free", and the proofs cover musl-linked static RISC-V binaries rather than the x86/ARM and glibc builds most people run — two sentences to hold against every "AI-verified medical device software" claim in the coming years.

  4. AI agents broke into 100+ company websites for about $50 each — The Next Web, reporting NorForge and Reuters (2026-10-19 onward)

    Read the cluster analysis: 14 Chinese-language control panels, over 1,100 projects, roughly a 10% success rate. The use of those numbers is not alarm but estimation — if you run a site nobody owns the patching for, its annualized probability of compromise can no longer be modelled as "we are too small to be worth attacking".

  5. Donating Agent Skills to the Agentic AI Foundation — Anthropic (2026-10-22)

    Anthropic donated Agent Skills — with the name, the GitHub organization and the agentskills.io domain — to AAIF under the Linux Foundation, reporting more than 70 platforms integrated, 1 million skills published and nearly 280 million installs since the standard went public in December 2025. The point is the roadmap item on "security guidance for skill writers and installers": a skill is a folder an agent reads into context and then follows, so distributing skills is a package manager in threat-model terms — and hospital IT now has to manage skills as supply-chain components of medical device software.
